What this means

A 1,500-watt load running 5 hours a day uses 7.5 kilowatt-hours each day it runs.

At $0.19 per kWh that's $1.43 a day of use, about $43.34 a month and $520.13 a year.

The label shows the maximum power. Devices that cycle, such as refrigerators, heaters with a thermostat and air conditioners, draw full power only part of the time, so enter the hours they actually run, or measure with a plug-in usage meter.

How to use this electricity cost calculator

Enter what the appliance draws and how long you use it, and the cost per month appears instantly.

  1. Power rating. Most appliances list their power in watts on a label on the back or bottom, or in the manual. If the label only shows amps, switch to amps and volts. Standard U.S. outlets are 120 volts.
  2. Hours per day. How long it actually runs. For a TV, that’s viewing time; for a space heater, the hours it’s switched on.
  3. Days per week. Use 7 for things you use daily, or fewer for a weekly laundry routine or a weekend workshop.
  4. Electricity price. The default is the latest U.S. average from the Bureau of Labor Statistics, with its date shown under the field. Replace it with the rate from your bill for a more accurate answer.
  5. Number of devices. Under More options, enter how many identical devices you’re running, such as light bulbs in a room.

The result shows the cost per month and per year, and the table breaks the energy and cost down per hour, per day of use, per week, per month and per year.

The formula

Energy per day (kWh) = watts × hours per day ÷ 1,000

Energy per year (kWh) = kWh per day × days per week × 365 ÷ 7

Cost = kWh × price per kWh

A month is a twelfth of the year. When you enter amps instead of watts, the calculator first works out watts = amps × volts. Energy is kept unrounded; only the costs are rounded to the cent.

Worked example: a space heater

A portable electric heater is rated at 1,500 watts, the most common size because it’s close to the limit of a standard 15-amp household circuit. It runs 5 hours every evening, at an electricity price of 19.6¢ per kWh.

  • Energy per day: 1,500 × 5 ÷ 1,000 = 7.5 kWh.
  • Cost per day: 7.5 × $0.196 = $1.47.
  • Energy per year: 7.5 × 365 = 2,737.5 kWh.
  • Cost per year, if used every day: 2,737.5 × $0.196 = $536.55, or $44.71 a month.

Few people run a heater all year, so a better estimate is the cost per day multiplied by the days you’ll actually use it. For a five-month heating season of about 150 days, that’s 150 × $1.47 = $220.50. If the heater has a thermostat and cycles off half the time once the room is warm, the real cost is closer to half that.

LED vs incandescent: a quick comparison

Lighting is where small numbers add up. Replacing one 60-watt incandescent bulb with a 9-watt LED that gives similar light, used 5 hours a day, saves 93 kWh and about $18.24 a year at 19.6¢. Across 20 bulbs in a house, that’s around $365 a year. LED bulbs also last many times longer, so you buy fewer replacements.

To check your own lights, enter the bulb’s wattage, the hours it’s on and the number of bulbs under More options.

Reading your electric bill

An electric bill usually has three kinds of charges:

  • Energy or supply charges, per kWh, for the electricity itself.
  • Delivery or distribution charges, often also per kWh, for getting it to your home.
  • A fixed customer charge, a flat amount each month whatever you use.

For this calculator, add the per-kWh supply and delivery charges together. That’s the true cost of using one more kilowatt-hour. The fixed charge doesn’t change when you run an appliance more or less, so leave it out.

According to the U.S. Energy Information Administration, the average U.S. residential customer used 10,791 kWh in 2022, about 899 kWh a month. At 19.6¢, that much electricity would cost about $176 a month before fixed charges.

Charging an electric car

An electric car is often the biggest new load a household adds. Its efficiency is rated in kilowatt-hours per 100 miles on the window sticker and on fueleconomy.gov; many cars fall between about 25 and 35 kWh per 100 miles.

For a car rated at 30 kWh per 100 miles driven 1,000 miles a month:

  • Energy at the battery: 1,000 ÷ 100 × 30 = 300 kWh.
  • Allowing about 10% for charging losses: roughly 330 kWh from the outlet.
  • Cost at 19.6¢: 330 × $0.196 ≈ $64.68 a month.

For comparison, a gasoline car averaging 28 mpg would burn about 35.7 gallons over the same 1,000 miles, about $148 at $4.15 a gallon. To model a charger in this calculator, enter its power (a Level 2 home charger often draws about 7,200 watts) and the hours it charges each day. The fuel cost calculator prices the gasoline side.

Heating and cooling

Space heating, air conditioning and water heating usually make up the largest part of a home’s electricity use, so small changes there matter more than anything else. A window air conditioner rated at 1,000 watts running 8 hours a day for 90 summer days uses 720 kWh, about $141 at 19.6¢. Raising the thermostat a couple of degrees, closing curtains on sunny windows and cleaning filters reduce how long it runs, which is what lowers the cost. A heat pump moves heat instead of making it, so it delivers more heat per kWh than a resistance heater with the same wattage.

Common mistakes

  1. Forgetting to divide by 1,000. Watts × hours gives watt-hours. Divide by 1,000 for kilowatt-hours, the unit on your bill.
  2. Using the maximum wattage for appliances that cycle. A refrigerator, freezer, dehumidifier or thermostat-controlled heater draws full power only part of the time.
  3. Using the total bill as the rate. Dividing the whole bill by the kWh used includes fixed charges, which overstates what one more kWh costs.
  4. Ignoring standby power. A few watts each from a dozen devices, all day, every day, adds up to a noticeable amount over a year.
  5. Mixing up kW and kWh. Power (kW) is the rate; energy (kWh) is the amount used over time.

Key terms

  • Watt (W): a unit of power, the rate at which an appliance uses energy.
  • Kilowatt (kW): 1,000 watts.
  • Kilowatt-hour (kWh): the energy used by a 1,000-watt load running for one hour. Utilities bill in kWh.
  • Amp (A): a unit of electric current. Watts = amps × volts.
  • Volt (V): electrical pressure. U.S. outlets supply about 120 V; large appliances use 240 V.
  • Standby power: electricity drawn by a device while it’s switched off or idle.
  • Duty cycle: the share of time an appliance that cycles on and off is actually running.

What to do with your result

  • Target the big loads first. Heating, cooling, water heating and laundry dryers usually use far more than lights and electronics.
  • Check before you buy. Compare the yearly kWh on EnergyGuide labels, multiply by your rate, and add the difference over the years you’ll own the appliance.
  • Measure, don’t guess. A plug-in electricity usage meter, often available to borrow from libraries or utilities, shows exactly what a 120-volt device uses over a week.
  • Cut standby power. A switched power strip for the TV and computer area turns off several standby loads at once.

Frequently asked questions

How do I calculate the cost of running an appliance?

Multiply the watts by the hours of use and divide by 1,000 to get kilowatt-hours, then multiply by your price per kWh. A 1,500-watt heater running 5 hours uses 1,500 × 5 ÷ 1,000 = 7.5 kWh; at 19.6¢ per kWh, that's $1.47 a day.

How many kWh does a 1,000-watt appliance use?

One kilowatt-hour for every hour it runs at full power. A 1,000-watt microwave used for 15 minutes uses 0.25 kWh. A 100-watt device takes 10 hours to use 1 kWh.

What is the average price of electricity per kWh?

The U.S. city average was about 19.6¢ per kWh in September 2026, according to the Bureau of Labor Statistics average price data, which this calculator uses as its default. Rates vary a lot by state and utility, so your bill is the best source.

How do I find my electricity rate?

Your bill lists the price per kWh, sometimes split into supply (generation) and delivery charges. Add the per-kWh charges together. Dividing the total bill by the kWh used gives a slightly higher figure, because it also spreads the fixed monthly charge over your usage.

How do I convert amps to watts?

Multiply amps by volts. A hair dryer drawing 12.5 amps on a 120-volt outlet uses about 1,500 watts. Larger appliances, such as dryers and ranges, run on 240 volts.

Why is my estimate higher than my real usage?

The label shows maximum power, and many appliances don't run at full power all the time. Refrigerators, freezers and anything with a thermostat cycle on and off. Enter the hours the appliance actually runs, use the yearly kWh from its EnergyGuide label, or measure it with a plug-in usage meter.

Do appliances use electricity when they're turned off?

Many do. TVs, game consoles, chargers, printers and devices with clocks or remote controls draw standby power, often a few watts each. Unplug them or use a switched power strip to stop it.
