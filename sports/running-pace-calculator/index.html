What this means

Covering 42.2 km in 4:00:00 means running 9:09 per mile (5:41 per km), or 6.6 mph.

Based on this run, Riegel’s formula predicts about 52:10 for a 10K and 4:00:00 for a marathon. Longer races are slower per mile, so predictions are more realistic than just holding the same pace.

Predictions assume similar training for each distance; marathon predictions in particular need long runs to be achievable.

Worked example: a 1:58:30 half marathon

A runner finishes a half marathon (21.0975 km, 13.11 miles) in 1 hour 58 minutes 30 seconds.

  • Pace: 7,110 seconds ÷ 13.11 miles = 9:02 per mile, or 5:37 per kilometer
  • Speed: 6.64 mph (10.68 km/h)

What does that suggest for other distances? Holding exactly the same pace would give a 28:05 5K and a 3:57:00 marathon, but runners are faster over short races and slower over long ones. Riegel’s formula adjusts for that:

Race Same pace Riegel prediction
5K 28:05 25:46
10K 56:10 53:42
Half marathon 1:58:30 1:58:30
Marathon 3:57:00 4:07:04

So a realistic marathon target after solid long-run training would be around 4:05 to 4:10. Going the other way, a runner targeting 8:30 per mile for a full marathon would finish in 3:42:52.

The formulas in plain English

Pace, time and distance are linked by one relationship: pace = time ÷ distance, so time = pace × distance and distance = time ÷ pace.

  • Times are converted to seconds first: hours × 3,600 + minutes × 60 + seconds.
  • Miles and kilometers are converted with the international definition, 1 mile = 1.609344 km.
  • Speed is distance ÷ time in hours.
  • Race predictions use Riegel’s formula, T₂ = T₁ × (D₂ ÷ D₁)^1.06. The 1.06 exponent captures the fact that pace slows slightly as distance grows.

The official race distances are 5 km, 10 km, 21.0975 km for the half marathon and 42.195 km for the marathon. The Show the math panel walks through each step with your numbers.

Even pacing vs negative splits

For most runners, the fastest way to finish a race is to run it at a nearly even effort, or slightly faster in the second half, which is called a negative split. Studies of large marathon fields consistently find that most runners slow down in the second half, often because they started too fast.

A simple plan for a goal race:

  1. First third: run 5 to 10 seconds per mile slower than goal pace. It will feel too easy; that’s the point.
  2. Middle third: settle into goal pace.
  3. Last third: if you feel strong, gradually speed up.

Banking time early rarely works: every second gained in the first miles tends to be repaid with interest at the end. The splits table above shows your target time at each mile so you can check your pacing as you go.

Walking and run-walk pacing

The calculator works just as well for walking. A brisk walk is typically around 15 to 20 minutes per mile (3 to 4 mph), so a 5K walk takes roughly 47 minutes to an hour and a half marathon roughly 3 1/4 to 4 1/2 hours. Many races have cut-off times, so check the event’s pace requirement before you enter, and use the calculator to see whether your usual walking pace finishes comfortably inside it. If it doesn’t, many events offer an earlier start for walkers.

Run-walk intervals, such as running 4 minutes and walking 1, are a popular way to finish longer races with less fatigue and lower injury risk. To set a pace for a run-walk plan, time a practice session over a known distance and enter that time; the calculator gives your average pace across both running and walking, which is what matters for the finish time.

How to get faster

Pace improves through consistent training more than any single workout. Most runners progress fastest by:

  • Running more easy miles, increasing weekly distance gradually, by around 10% at a time, to build aerobic fitness while limiting injury risk.
  • Adding one quality session a week, such as tempo running or intervals, once you have a regular base.
  • Recovering properly, with easy days, enough sleep and enough food, especially carbohydrate on heavy training days.
  • Racing occasionally. A recent race result is the best input for this calculator’s predictions and training paces.
  • Strength training two days a week, which helps runners stay injury-free and hold their form late in a race.

Using pace in training

Most structured plans use different paces for different runs:

  • Easy runs are usually 1 to 2 minutes per mile slower than goal marathon pace. They build endurance with little fatigue and should make up most of your weekly running.
  • Tempo runs are “comfortably hard,” roughly the pace you could hold for an hour.
  • Intervals are short, fast repeats at around 5K pace or faster, with recovery between.
  • Long runs build marathon endurance and are typically run at easy pace.

Plug a recent race result into the calculator, look at the predicted times, and use them to set realistic paces for each type of run. Re-check every 6 to 8 weeks as your fitness improves.

Race day pacing tips

Start a little conservatively: adrenaline makes the first mile feel easy, and going out too fast is the most common reason for a slow finish. Your GPS watch will usually read slightly long compared with the course markers, so check your split times at the official mile or kilometer signs. Print or save the splits table so you can compare as you go.

Pace on hills, heat and trails

Even pacing assumes a flat course and good conditions. On hilly courses, aim for even effort rather than even pace: slower on the climbs, faster on the descents. In hot weather, many coaches suggest slowing by roughly 10 to 30 seconds per mile or more, and trail runs are best judged by effort or time rather than pace.

GPS watches can also drift on tall buildings, tree cover and tight turns, so for interval training on a track it’s more accurate to time each lap than to trust the watch’s instant pace. On race day, the official mile or kilometer markers are the best guide, and the splits table here gives you the target time at each one.

Key terms

  • Pace: time per unit of distance, such as minutes per mile.
  • Splits: elapsed time at each mile or kilometer marker.
  • Negative split: running the second half faster than the first.
  • Riegel formula: a widely used model for predicting race times across distances.
  • Tangents: the shortest line through a course’s turns; courses are measured along them.

Frequently asked questions

How do I calculate my running pace?

Divide your total time by the distance. A half marathon (13.1 miles) in 1:58:30 is 7,110 seconds ÷ 13.11 miles = 542 seconds per mile, or 9:02 per mile. The calculator does this instantly and converts between miles and kilometers using the exact definition of 1 mile = 1.609344 km.

What pace do I need for a 4-hour marathon?

About 9:09 per mile, or 5:41 per kilometer, held for all 26.2 miles. A sub-4 needs a little faster than that, and most runners aim a few seconds per mile quicker to allow for water stops, crowded starts and the extra distance from not running perfect tangents.

How accurate are race time predictions?

Riegel's formula, T₂ = T₁ × (D₂ ÷ D₁)^1.06, has been widely used since 1981 and is usually close for distances from about 1.5 to 15 miles if you've trained for the target race. Marathon predictions from short races are often optimistic, because the marathon depends heavily on endurance training and fueling.

What is a good running pace for beginners?

Any pace you can keep while still able to talk in short sentences is a good place to start. For many new runners that's somewhere around 10 to 13 minutes per mile. Speed comes with consistency; most training plans keep the majority of runs at an easy, conversational effort.

How do I convert pace per mile to pace per kilometer?

Divide the pace per mile by 1.609344. For example, 9:00 per mile is 540 seconds ÷ 1.609344 = 335.5 seconds, or about 5:36 per kilometer. To go the other way, multiply pace per kilometer by 1.609344.

What are even splits?

Running each mile or kilometer in the same time. Even or slightly negative splits, where the second half is a little faster, are widely considered the most efficient way to race long distances. The splits table shows the elapsed time you'd see at each marker running perfectly even.
