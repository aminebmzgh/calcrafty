## How to use this calorie calculator

Enter your details, choose how active you are and pick a goal. The calculator estimates the calories your body burns at rest (BMR), the total you burn in a day (TDEE), and the daily intake that matches your goal.

1. **Units, sex, age, height and weight.** Switching units converts what you've entered.
2. **Activity level.** Be honest; most people overestimate. If you sit at a desk and exercise a few times a week, "light" or "moderate" is usually right.
3. **Goal.** Maintain, lose up to 2 pounds a week, or gain up to 1 pound a week.
4. **More options.** Mifflin-St Jeor is the default. Harris-Benedict is an older alternative; Katch-McArdle uses lean body mass and needs your body fat percentage.

## Worked example: a moderately busy 38-year-old

A 38-year-old woman is 5 feet 5 inches (165.1 cm), weighs 158 pounds (71.7 kg) and does light exercise two or three times a week. She wants to lose about a pound a week.

- **BMR (Mifflin-St Jeor):** 10 × 71.7 + 6.25 × 165.1 − 5 × 38 − 161 ≈ **1,398 calories**
- **TDEE:** 1,398 × 1.375 (light activity) ≈ **1,922 calories**
- **Target to lose 1 pound a week:** 1,922 − 500 = **1,422 calories a day**

The formulas don't fully agree: Harris-Benedict puts her TDEE at about 2,004 and Katch-McArdle, at 32% body fat, at about 1,956. A spread of around 80 calories is normal and is one reason to treat any result as a starting estimate.

Her full range of targets, from losing 2 pounds a week to gaining 1, runs from 922 to 2,422 calories. The 922-calorie option is below the usual 1,200-calorie minimum for women, so the calculator flags it.

## How the calculation works in plain English

There are three steps.

1. **Basal metabolic rate (BMR)** is the energy your body uses at complete rest. Mifflin-St Jeor estimates it as 10 × weight (kg) + 6.25 × height (cm) − 5 × age, plus 5 for men or minus 161 for women.
2. **TDEE** multiplies BMR by an activity factor: 1.2 for sedentary, 1.375 for light, 1.55 for moderate, 1.725 for active and 1.9 for very active.
3. **Goal calories** add or subtract about 500 calories a day for each pound a week you want to gain or lose, based on the common estimate that a pound of body weight stores about 3,500 calories.

The **Show the math** panel substitutes your own numbers into each step.

## What the activity levels mean

The activity factor has the biggest effect on the result, so choose it carefully:

| Level | Factor | Typical week |
|---|---|---|
| Sedentary | 1.2 | Desk job, little or no exercise |
| Light | 1.375 | Exercise 1–3 days a week |
| Moderate | 1.55 | Exercise 3–5 days a week |
| Active | 1.725 | Hard exercise 6–7 days a week |
| Very active | 1.9 | Physical job plus training, or training twice a day |

If you're between two levels, pick the lower one and adjust after a few weeks of tracking.

## Typical calorie needs by age and activity

To show how much age, size and activity matter, here are Mifflin-St Jeor estimates for two example adults. Your own numbers will differ with your height and weight, so use the calculator for a personal figure.

| Example adult | Age | BMR | Sedentary | Moderately active |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Woman, 5 ft 4 in, 140 lb | 25 | 1,365 | 1,638 | 2,116 |
| Woman, 5 ft 4 in, 140 lb | 45 | 1,265 | 1,518 | 1,961 |
| Woman, 5 ft 4 in, 140 lb | 65 | 1,165 | 1,398 | 1,806 |
| Man, 5 ft 10 in, 180 lb | 25 | 1,808 | 2,169 | 2,802 |
| Man, 5 ft 10 in, 180 lb | 45 | 1,708 | 2,049 | 2,647 |
| Man, 5 ft 10 in, 180 lb | 65 | 1,608 | 1,929 | 2,492 |

Two patterns stand out. Moving from sedentary to moderately active adds about 400 to 650 calories a day, far more than ageing removes. And each decade of age lowers the estimate by about 50 to 80 calories a day, depending on activity, which is why a diet that held your weight steady at 30 can lead to slow gain by 50 unless activity rises too.

## Why weight loss slows down

Almost everyone who sticks to a calorie target sees progress slow after the first few weeks. Several things are going on:

- **The first drop is partly water.** Eating fewer carbohydrates and less salt releases stored water, so the first week or two often shows a bigger loss than the deficit alone explains.
- **A smaller body burns less.** The woman in the example uses about 1,922 calories a day at 158 pounds; at 138 pounds, the same formula gives about 1,797, so the original 500-calorie deficit has shrunk to about 375 without any change in eating.
- **The body adapts.** Beyond the effect of weight itself, people tend to move a little less and burn slightly less energy during a long deficit.

This is why the old rule that every 3,500-calorie deficit removes a pound overestimates long-term results. Research by the US National Institutes of Health found that weight loss follows a curve that flattens over months, not a straight line. Re-running the calculator every 10 pounds or so, and tracking a weekly average rather than daily weights, keeps your target realistic.

## Where calories come from

Every calorie you eat comes from one of four sources, each with a fixed energy content:

| Source | Calories per gram |
|---|---|
| Protein | 4 |
| Carbohydrate | 4 |
| Fat | 9 |
| Alcohol | 7 |

Fat has more than twice the calories of protein or carbohydrate per gram, which is why small amounts of oil, butter and nuts add up quickly. Alcohol provides calories with no nutrients, and drinks are easy to forget when tracking. The [macro calculator](/health/macro-calculator/) splits your calorie target into grams of protein, carbohydrate and fat.

## Eating to gain weight or build muscle

The same calculator works in the other direction. If you're underweight, or want to build muscle, choose a gain goal:

- **Keep the surplus modest.** About 250 to 500 calories above your TDEE is typical. Larger surpluses mostly add fat rather than muscle.
- **Pair it with strength training.** Without a training stimulus, extra calories are stored as fat. Progressive resistance training, adding weight or reps over time, is what signals the body to build muscle.
- **Get enough protein.** Spreading protein across meals through the day supports muscle repair; the macro calculator suggests a daily amount based on your weight.
- **Expect slow progress.** Gaining a pound of muscle takes far longer than gaining a pound of fat. A gain of about half a pound a week is a reasonable pace for most adults.

## Minimum intakes and when to get help

Very low-calorie diets can leave you short of protein, vitamins and minerals and are hard to sustain. As a general rule, women shouldn't eat fewer than about 1,200 calories a day and men fewer than about 1,500 without medical supervision; the calculator flags targets below those levels. Medically supervised very low-calorie diets do exist, but they're designed and monitored by clinicians.

Talk to a doctor or registered dietitian before a large change if you're pregnant or breastfeeding, under 18, managing diabetes or another condition, or taking medication that affects appetite or weight. If counting calories starts to feel stressful or obsessive, step back from tracking; in the US, the National Alliance for Eating Disorders offers a free helpline.

## Making a calorie target work

A number on a screen only helps if it fits real life. A few habits make the difference:

- **Track for a short period, not forever.** Logging meals for two or three weeks teaches you portion sizes and where calories hide, such as cooking oil, drinks and snacks.
- **Prioritize protein and fiber.** Both help you feel full on fewer calories, and enough protein helps keep muscle while you lose fat.
- **Keep some strength training.** Lifting weights or bodyweight exercise two or three times a week helps preserve muscle, which supports your metabolic rate.
- **Judge by the trend.** Daily weight swings of 1 to 3 pounds are mostly water. A weekly average is far more reliable than any single morning.
- **Recalculate as you change.** Every 10 pounds lost lowers your TDEE a little, so re-run the calculator after a meaningful change.

## Key terms

- **BMR (basal metabolic rate):** calories burned at complete rest.
- **TDEE (total daily energy expenditure):** calories burned in a typical day, including activity.
- **Calorie deficit:** eating fewer calories than your TDEE, which leads to weight loss over time.
- **Lean body mass:** everything that isn't fat, such as muscle, bone and organs; used by Katch-McArdle.
- **Metabolic adaptation:** the slowdown in calorie burn that comes with weight loss.
