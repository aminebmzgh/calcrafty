## How to use this percentage calculator

Pick the question you're trying to answer from the list, enter two numbers, and the answer appears as you type. The calculator covers the five most common percentage questions:

1. **X% of a number:** what is 15% of 80?
2. **What percent X is of Y:** 30 is what percent of 120?
3. **Percent change:** what's the percent increase or decrease from 50 to 65?
4. **Price after a discount:** what does a $120 item cost at 30% off?
5. **A number increased by X%:** what is 200 plus 8%?

You can type numbers with commas or symbols, such as `1,250` or `6.5%`. Calculations use exact decimal arithmetic, so results like 0.1% of 0.3 come out as 0.0003, not a long rounding error.

## Worked examples

**Tip on a restaurant bill.** An $86.40 bill with an 18% tip: 86.40 × 18 ÷ 100 = $15.55 (rounded to the cent), for a total of $101.95.

**Test score.** Getting 42 questions right out of 48 is 42 ÷ 48 × 100 = 87.5%.

**Rent increase.** Rent going from $1,650 to $1,749 a month is a change of $99, and $99 ÷ $1,650 × 100 = a 6% increase.

**Sale price.** A $249.99 jacket at 35% off saves 249.99 × 35 ÷ 100 = $87.50 (to the cent), so you pay $162.49.

**Price with sales tax.** A $40 purchase with 7.25% sales tax is 40 × 1.0725 = $42.90.

**Salary raise.** A $62,000 salary with a 4% raise becomes 62,000 × 1.04 = $64,480.

## The percentage formulas in plain English

"Percent" means "per hundred," so 15% is 15 ÷ 100 = 0.15. Every percentage question rearranges the same relationship: **part = whole × percent ÷ 100**.

- **X% of Y:** Y × X ÷ 100
- **What percent X is of Y:** X ÷ Y × 100
- **Percent change:** (new − original) ÷ |original| × 100
- **After a discount of X%:** price × (100 − X) ÷ 100
- **After an increase of X%:** number × (100 + X) ÷ 100

Percent change is measured against the original value, using its absolute value so the direction is clear even for negative starting numbers. Percent change from zero is undefined, because you can't divide by zero; the calculator tells you rather than showing an error code. The **Show the math** panel shows the formula with your numbers.

## Common percentage mistakes

- **Using the wrong base.** "A is 20% more than B" and "B is 20% less than A" are not the same. If A = 120 and B = 100, A is 20% more than B, but B is only 16.7% less than A.
- **Adding percentages that apply in sequence.** A 20% discount followed by an extra 10% off is a 28% total discount, not 30%, because the second discount applies to the already-reduced price.
- **Confusing percent and percentage points.** A change from 10% to 12% is 2 percentage points but a 20% relative increase.
- **Reversing a percentage change.** To undo a 25% decrease you need a 33.3% increase, not 25%.

## Reverse percentages: finding the original number

Sometimes you know the result after a percentage was applied and need the number you started with. The trick is to divide, not subtract:

**Original = final ÷ (1 + percent ÷ 100)** for an increase, or **final ÷ (1 − percent ÷ 100)** for a decrease.

- **Sale price.** A jacket costs $84 after 30% off. The original price was $84 ÷ 0.70 = **$120**, not $84 + 30% ($109.20).
- **Price including tax.** A receipt shows $54 including 8% sales tax. The price before tax was $54 ÷ 1.08 = **$50**, and the tax was $4.
- **Salary after a raise.** If your new salary of $63,000 includes a 5% raise, your old salary was $63,000 ÷ 1.05 = **$60,000**.

Subtracting the percentage from the final number is the most common mistake here, because the percentage was calculated on the original, smaller (or larger) amount.

## Why losses need bigger gains to recover

Because a percentage decrease and the increase that undoes it are measured from different starting points, recovering from a loss always takes a larger percentage gain:

| Drop | Gain needed to get back to the start |
|---|---|
| 10% | 11.11% |
| 20% | 25% |
| 25% | 33.33% |
| 50% | 100% |
| 75% | 300% |
| 90% | 900% |

This matters for investments: a portfolio that falls 50% must double just to break even. It's also why a price that's cut by 20% and then raised by 20% ends up 4% lower than where it started.

## Percentages over several periods

When a percentage applies repeatedly, such as yearly growth, interest or inflation, the effects compound rather than add:

- **5% a year for 10 years** is a total increase of 62.89%, not 50%.
- **3% inflation for 5 years** raises prices by 15.93% in total.
- **7% a year for 10 years** nearly doubles a number (+96.72%), which is the Rule of 72 at work.
- **Three straight 10% declines** add up to a 27.1% drop, not 30%.

To find the total change, multiply (1 + rate) by itself once for each period, then subtract 1. The [compound interest calculator](/finance/compound-interest-calculator/) does this for savings, including regular deposits.

## Percentage or percentile?

The words sound alike but mean different things. A **percentage** describes a part of a whole: 85% on a test means 85 out of every 100 points. A **percentile** describes rank within a group: scoring in the 85th percentile means you did better than 85% of the people who took the test, whatever your raw score was. A score of 70% can be in the 95th percentile on a very hard test. Growth charts, standardized tests and income statistics usually report percentiles.

## Reading percentages in the news

Headlines often report **relative** changes, which can sound dramatic, without the **absolute** numbers behind them. If the risk of a side effect rises from 2 in 1,000 people to 3 in 1,000, that's a 50% relative increase, but only 0.1 percentage points in absolute terms: one extra case per thousand people.

Both numbers are true, and both are useful in their own way, but they answer different questions. The relative change says how much bigger the risk became; the absolute change says how many people it actually affects. When a percentage sounds surprising, look for the starting value and ask "a percentage of what?" A 200% increase in something that happened only twice is just six occurrences in total.

To turn any fraction into a percentage, divide the top number by the bottom and multiply by 100. The [fraction calculator](/math/fraction-calculator/) shows the decimal form of any fraction.

## Mental math shortcuts

- **10%:** move the decimal point one place left (10% of 340 = 34).
- **1%:** move it two places (1% of 340 = 3.4).
- **5%:** half of 10%. **20%:** double 10%. **25%:** divide by 4. **50%:** divide by 2.
- **Swap trick:** X% of Y equals Y% of X. So 8% of 50 is the same as 50% of 8, which is 4.

## Percentages in money decisions

Percentages hide in almost every financial decision, and small differences add up:

- **Interest rates.** A credit card at 24% APR charges about 2% a month on the balance; a savings account at 4% earns about 0.33% a month.
- **Raises and inflation.** A 3% raise in a year with 4% inflation means your pay buys about 1% less than before.
- **Investment fees.** A 1% annual fee sounds small, but on a $200,000 portfolio it's $2,000 a year.
- **Tips and taxes.** Both are percentages of the bill; tips are often calculated before tax.
- **Discounts.** "Up to 70% off" describes the best item in the sale, not the typical one.

Whenever you compare two options, convert percentages into dollar amounts for your own situation. That makes the real difference obvious.

A quick reasonableness check helps too. Ten percent of a number is easy to find in your head; if your answer for 12% of a number is far from that ten-percent figure, re-check which box each number went in.

## Key terms

- **Percent:** a number out of 100.
- **Percentage point:** the arithmetic difference between two percentages.
- **Percent change:** the relative change from an original value.
- **Markup:** a percentage added to a cost to set a selling price.
- **Discount:** a percentage taken off a price.
