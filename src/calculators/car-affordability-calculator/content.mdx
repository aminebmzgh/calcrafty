## How to use this car affordability calculator

Enter your income and how much of it you're willing to spend on a car. The calculator works backwards: from your monthly budget to a payment, from the payment to a loan, and from the loan plus your cash to the highest price you can pay.

1. **Gross annual income.** Before tax.
2. **Share of income for car costs.** 10% is the 20/4/10 guideline. Lower it if you have other large debts.
3. **Insurance, fuel and maintenance.** Monthly running costs come out of the budget first. Get an insurance quote for the kind of car you're considering; it varies a lot by model.
4. **Down payment and trade-in.** Use the trade-in value minus anything you still owe on it.
5. **Interest rate and term.** The rate starts at the Federal Reserve's latest average for 60-month new-car loans at banks. Used cars and lower credit scores usually cost more.
6. **More options.** Sales tax, title and registration fees, and whether your state taxes the price before or after the trade-in.

## Worked example: a first car on $60,000

Sam earns $60,000 a year and has $4,000 saved for a down payment. Insurance, fuel and maintenance will run about $180 a month. The loan rate is 7.5% for 48 months, sales tax is 6% and fees are $500.

- **Monthly car budget:** $60,000 ÷ 12 × 10% = **$500**
- **Left for the payment:** $500 − $180 = **$320**
- **Largest loan:** $320 a month for 48 months at 7.5% repays **$13,234**
- **Highest price:** ($13,234 + $4,000 − $500) ÷ 1.06 = **$15,786**, including $947.16 of sales tax

Over four years, Sam will pay $2,125.25 in interest. A $25,000 car would mean borrowing $23,000 and a $556.11 payment on the same terms. With insurance and fuel, that's $736 a month, nearly 50% over the budget.

## The formulas in plain English

**Monthly budget = gross annual income ÷ 12 × budget %**

**Payment = budget − insurance and running costs**

**Largest loan = payment × (1 − (1 + r)⁻ⁿ) ÷ r**, where r is the APR ÷ 12 and n is the number of months. This is the loan payment formula run in reverse.

**Highest price = (loan + down payment + trade-in × (1 + tax rate) − fees) ÷ (1 + tax rate)**, because the price plus sales tax and fees must equal the money you have from the loan, your cash and your trade-in. The **Show the math** panel above runs each step with your numbers.

## How much car can you afford by income?

Using 10% of gross income, $200 a month for insurance and running costs, $5,000 down, 7.5% for 48 months, 6% sales tax and $500 in fees:

| Income | Monthly budget | Payment | Car price |
|---|---|---|---|
| $40,000 | $333.33 | $133.32 | $9,447 |
| $50,000 | $416.67 | $216.67 | $12,699 |
| $60,000 | $500 | $299.99 | $15,950 |
| $75,000 | $625 | $424.99 | $20,827 |
| $100,000 | $833.33 | $633.32 | $28,955 |
| $150,000 | $1,250 | $1,049.99 | $45,213 |

The 10% guideline is strict on purpose: it assumes you're also saving for retirement and emergencies. If you're debt-free and already saving well, a higher percentage can still be affordable.

## How the loan term changes the price

The same $425 payment on a $75,000 income buys more car with a longer loan, but the interest climbs quickly:

| Term | Car price | Loan | Total interest |
|---|---|---|---|
| 36 months | $17,133 | $13,662 | $1,637.07 |
| 48 months | $20,827 | $17,577 | $2,822.65 |
| 60 months | $24,253 | $21,209 | $4,290.17 |
| 72 months | $27,433 | $24,580 | $6,019.36 |

## How your interest rate changes the price

Your rate depends mostly on your credit score and whether the car is new or used, and it changes how far the same payment stretches. On a $75,000 income with the default assumptions ($425 a month for the loan, $5,000 down, 48 months):

| APR | Car price you can afford | Total interest |
|---|---|---|
| 5% | $21,654 | $1,945.16 |
| 7.5% | $20,827 | $2,822.65 |
| 10% | $20,052 | $3,642.79 |
| 14% | $18,916 | $4,847.17 |

Because the payment is capped, a higher rate shows up as a cheaper car rather than a bigger bill. Getting pre-approved by a bank or credit union before you shop tells you which row you're in.

## How strict should your budget be?

The 10% guideline is deliberately conservative. Raising the share of income you allow for car costs increases the price quickly:

| Share of gross income for car costs | Monthly car budget | Car price |
|---|---|---|
| 10% | $625.00 | $20,827 |
| 15% | $937.50 | $33,019 |
| 20% | $1,250.00 | $45,213 |

Spending 15% or more can work if you have no other debts, already save for retirement and have an emergency fund, or if the car replaces other transport costs. If you're also saving for a home, keep car costs low: lenders count the car payment in your debt-to-income ratio, and every $100 of car payment reduces the mortgage you can qualify for.

## Why the down payment matters so much

Cash up front raises your budget dollar for dollar, roughly speaking, and lowers the risk of owing more than the car is worth. With the same $425 payment:

| Down payment | Car price | Down payment as share of price |
|---|---|---|
| $0 | $16,110 | 0% |
| $5,000 | $20,827 | 24% |
| $10,000 | $25,544 | 39.1% |

With nothing down, the loan covers the sales tax and fees too, so you'd owe more than the car is worth from the day you drive it home. The 20% down guideline exists to avoid exactly that.

## New, used or leased?

- **New cars** cost more but come with warranties, the latest safety features and often subsidized financing. They lose value fastest in the first few years.
- **Used cars** a few years old have already taken that early drop in value, so your money buys more car, although loan rates are usually higher and repairs come sooner.
- **Leasing** gives lower monthly payments on a new car, but you don't own anything at the end, and mileage limits and wear charges apply. It suits people who want a new car every few years and drive predictable miles.

Whichever you choose, this calculator's price is what you can afford to buy. For a lease, compare the monthly lease payment plus insurance and running costs with the same monthly budget.

## Should you pay cash instead?

Paying cash avoids interest entirely and makes the car yours from day one. In the default scenario, financing $17,577 over four years costs $2,822.65 in interest, money that stays in your pocket if you can pay cash.

Cash isn't automatically the best choice, though:

- **Keep your emergency fund intact.** Draining savings to avoid a 6% or 7% loan leaves you exposed to the next surprise expense, which often ends up on a credit card at 20% or more.
- **Compare the loan rate with what your cash earns.** If a manufacturer offers 0% or 1.9% financing and your savings earn 4%, financing and keeping the cash can come out slightly ahead, as long as you'd really leave that money untouched.
- **A cheaper car beats a cheaper loan.** If you can't pay cash for the car you want, a less expensive one you can afford outright, or with a small loan, is often the strongest financial choice.

A middle path many buyers use is to put a large down payment toward the car, keep three to six months of expenses in savings, and finance the rest over a short term.

## Costs to budget for beyond the payment

The loan payment is usually less than half of what a car really costs to own. Before settling on a price, estimate the rest:

- **Insurance:** varies widely by model, age, ZIP code and driving record. A sporty or expensive car can cost far more to insure than a practical one at the same price, so get quotes before you buy.
- **Fuel or charging:** your monthly miles divided by the car's miles per gallon, times the local fuel price.
- **Maintenance and repairs:** tires, brakes and servicing on any car, and larger repairs once the warranty ends.
- **Registration and taxes:** annual registration fees, and in some states a yearly personal property tax on vehicles.
- **Depreciation:** a new car typically loses a large share of its value in the first few years. It isn't a monthly bill, but it's the biggest cost of owning a newer car.

## Key terms

- **20/4/10 rule:** 20% down, a loan of 4 years or less, and car costs under 10% of gross income.
- **Out-the-door price:** the price plus sales tax, title, registration and dealer fees.
- **Trade-in equity:** what your current car is worth minus what you still owe on it.
- **Upside down (negative equity):** owing more on a car than it's worth.
- **APR:** the yearly cost of the loan including required fees.
