## How to use this pay raise calculator

Enter your current pay, choose how you're paid, and type the raise as a percentage, or tap **$** to enter a dollar amount instead. Results update as you type.

1. **Current pay.** Your hourly rate, paycheck, monthly pay or annual salary, before tax.
2. **Raise.** A percentage, or dollars per pay period. Switching between % and $ converts the value for you.
3. **Inflation.** Starts at the latest 12-month change in the Consumer Price Index from the Bureau of Labor Statistics. Change it to compare with a different year.

## Worked example: is a 3.5% raise enough?

Jordan earns $58,000 a year and is offered a 3.5% raise. Prices rose 3% over the year.

- **Raise:** $58,000 × 3.5% = **$2,030 a year**, for a new salary of $60,030
- **Per month:** $169.17 more before tax; **per biweekly paycheck:** $78.08
- **To keep up with prices:** $58,000 × 3% = $1,740 of the raise just keeps up with inflation
- **Real raise:** (1.035 ÷ 1.03) − 1 = **0.49%**

Jordan's pay buys only about half a percent more than last year. If Jordan negotiates 5% instead, the raise is $2,900 a year ($241.67 a month), and the real raise grows to 1.94%, roughly four times as much.

## The formulas in plain English

**New pay = current pay × (1 + raise %)**

**Raise % = (new pay − old pay) ÷ old pay × 100**

**Real raise = (1 + raise) ÷ (1 + inflation) − 1**

The real raise uses division rather than simple subtraction (5% − 3% = 2%) because prices and pay both grow by percentages; the difference is small for low rates but grows with high inflation. The **Show the math** panel above runs through each step with your numbers.

## How much is a 3%, 4% or 5% raise?

Extra pay per year and per month, before tax:

| Salary | 3% raise | 4% raise | 5% raise |
|---|---|---|---|
| $40,000 | $1,200 ($100/mo) | $1,600 ($133.33/mo) | $2,000 ($166.67/mo) |
| $50,000 | $1,500 ($125/mo) | $2,000 ($166.67/mo) | $2,500 ($208.33/mo) |
| $60,000 | $1,800 ($150/mo) | $2,400 ($200/mo) | $3,000 ($250/mo) |
| $75,000 | $2,250 ($187.50/mo) | $3,000 ($250/mo) | $3,750 ($312.50/mo) |
| $100,000 | $3,000 ($250/mo) | $4,000 ($333.33/mo) | $5,000 ($416.67/mo) |

## Why one extra percent matters more than it looks

Raises compound. Each year's raise is calculated on a salary that already includes every earlier raise, so a small difference in the percentage grows into a large difference in pay.

Take Jordan's $58,000 salary again and compare two careers over the next ten years: one with 3% raises every year, one with 4%.

- **After 10 years at 3%:** about **$77,947** a year.
- **After 10 years at 4%:** about **$85,854** a year, nearly $7,900 more every year from then on.
- **Total earned over the ten years:** about $684,852 versus $724,208, a gap of **$39,356**.

Because future raises, retirement contributions and employer matches are all percentages of your salary, a stronger raise now also lifts every one of them. That's why negotiating a starting salary or an annual raise is often worth more than it seems in the moment.

## How to use the result in a raise conversation

Numbers make a request concrete. Before a review, use the calculator to work out three figures:

1. **Your break-even raise:** the percentage that just matches inflation. Below it, your pay buys less than last year.
2. **Your target raise:** what you're asking for, in percent and in dollars per year.
3. **The market rate:** what similar roles pay. The Bureau of Labor Statistics publishes pay by occupation, and job listings increasingly include salary ranges.

Ask in annual dollars as well as percent: "$3,000 a year" is easier to discuss than "5%". If the employer can't move on salary, ask about a one-time bonus, extra paid time off or a higher retirement match; the [salary to hourly calculator](/finance/salary-to-hourly-calculator/) shows what paid days off are worth per hour worked.

## How much of a raise you actually keep

A raise is taxed at your marginal rate, the rate on your top dollars of income, plus Social Security and Medicare. So you keep less than the headline figure, but you never take home less because of a raise.

For a single filer earning $60,000, paid every two weeks, with no 401(k) and no state income tax, a 4% raise to $62,400 adds:

- **$2,400 a year** before tax,
- **$1,928.40 a year** after federal income tax and FICA, which is 80% of the raise,
- **$74.17 per paycheck.**

In a state with an income tax, or with a 401(k) percentage that rises with your pay, you'll keep a little less in cash, although the 401(k) part is still your money. Run both salaries through the [paycheck calculator](/finance/paycheck-calculator/) to see your own figures.

## Real raise at different inflation rates

What a raise is worth depends on inflation over the same period. On a $60,000 salary:

| Raise | Inflation | Real raise | Needed to keep up | Ahead of inflation |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 3% | 2.0% | 0.98% | $1,200 | $600 |
| 3% | 3.7% | −0.68% | $1,800 (more than the raise) | $0 |
| 5% | 3.7% | 1.25% | $2,220 | $780 |
| 8% | 3.7% | 4.15% | $2,220 | $2,580 |

A 3% raise feels like progress, but in a year with 3.7% inflation it's a small pay cut in real terms. That's why it's worth knowing the current inflation rate before a pay review.

## Changing jobs vs staying

Job changes often come with bigger pay increases than annual raises, and because raises compound, a one-time jump keeps paying off. Compare two paths for someone earning $60,000:

- **Stay:** 3% raises every year.
- **Switch:** a new job at $66,000 (a 10% increase), then the same 3% raises.

Over the next five years, staying earns about $318,548 and switching about $350,403, a difference of **$31,855**, and the gap keeps growing every year after that. A job change isn't always the right move, but if your raises consistently trail the market, it's worth knowing what the jump would be worth.

## Raises for hourly workers

For hourly pay, enter your rate and choose *per hour*. A raise of $1.50 on $20 an hour is 7.5%, worth $3,120 a year at 40 hours a week. If your hours vary, the yearly figure scales with them: at 30 hours a week, the same raise is worth $2,340.

## Cost-of-living raises vs merit raises

Some employers split their pay increases into two parts. A **cost-of-living adjustment** aims to keep your pay level with prices and is often the same percentage for everyone. A **merit raise** rewards performance and varies by person. If your employer gives only one number, compare it with inflation: anything above the inflation rate is the merit part, the real increase in what your work earns.

Government benefits work the same way. Social Security payments rise each year by a COLA tied to the CPI-W, a version of the Consumer Price Index, so beneficiaries keep pace with prices without any merit component.

## A raise also raises your savings

Retirement contributions set as a percentage of pay grow automatically with each raise. If you put 6% of a $60,000 salary into a 401(k), a 4% raise adds $144 a year to your contributions, and an employer matching 50% of that adds another $72, without any change on your part.

You can go further by raising your contribution rate at the same time as your pay. Increasing from 6% to 7% when you get the raise still leaves you with more take-home pay than before, and the extra 1% compounds for decades.

## When raises usually happen

Many employers set raises once a year, often tied to performance reviews or the start of a fiscal year, and budget a pool for them months in advance. Asking a few weeks before budgets are set gives your manager the best chance to argue for more. Promotions, new responsibilities and competing offers are the other moments when larger increases are most likely. Once a raise is agreed, confirm the new rate and its effective date in writing.

## Key terms

- **Merit raise:** an increase based on performance, usually given yearly.
- **Cost-of-living adjustment (COLA):** an increase meant to offset inflation.
- **Nominal raise:** the raise in dollars or percent, before inflation.
- **Real raise:** the raise after inflation, measuring the change in purchasing power.
- **Consumer Price Index (CPI):** the government's main measure of how prices change, published monthly by the Bureau of Labor Statistics.

## What to do with your result

A raise is gross pay, so check what actually reaches your account by running the old and new salary through the [paycheck calculator](/finance/paycheck-calculator/). To compare an hourly offer with a salary, use the [salary to hourly calculator](/finance/salary-to-hourly-calculator/).

One of the easiest ways to make a raise count is to save part of it before you get used to spending it. Raising your 401(k) contribution by even 1% can add a surprising amount by retirement; the [retirement calculator](/finance/retirement-calculator/) shows how much. For a shorter-term goal, the [savings goal calculator](/finance/savings-goal-calculator/) turns the extra into a plan.

If you're weighing a raise at your current job against an offer elsewhere, compare the whole package rather than the percentage alone: health coverage, retirement match, paid time off and commute all change what the new pay is really worth.
