## How to use this emergency fund calculator

Enter your essential monthly costs and how many months you want to cover, and the calculator shows your target. Add what you've saved and what you can put aside each month to see how long it takes to get there.

1. **Essential monthly expenses.** The bills you'd keep paying if your income stopped. Look at the last two or three months of bank and card statements to get a realistic figure.
2. **Months to cover.** 3 to 6 is the usual range; see below for how to choose.
3. **Already saved.** Money set aside specifically for emergencies, not your checking account float.
4. **Monthly saving.** An amount you can sustain every month.
5. **Savings rate.** It starts at the current 3-month Treasury bill yield, a benchmark for high-yield savings accounts.
6. **More options.** A goal date, to see the monthly amount needed to finish by then.

## Worked example: a six-month fund for a family

A household spends about $4,200 a month on essentials: rent, utilities, groceries, insurance, car payments and childcare. They want six months covered, have $3,500 saved and can add $500 a month to a high-yield savings account paying 4%.

- **Target:** $4,200 × 6 = **$25,200**
- **Covered now:** $3,500 ÷ $4,200 = **0.8 months**
- **Still to save:** **$21,700**
- **Time to reach the target:** **40 months**, about 3 years and 4 months
- **To finish in 3 years instead:** save **$556.60** a month

Interest helps: without it, the same $500 a month would take 44 months. Here's how the target and timeline change with different coverage:

| Months covered | Target | Time at $500 a month | Monthly saving to finish in 3 years |
|---|---|---|---|
| 3 | $12,600 | 18 months | $226.63 |
| 6 | $25,200 | 40 months | $556.60 |
| 9 | $37,800 | 61 months | $886.57 |
| 12 | $50,400 | 81 months | $1,216.54 |

## How many months should you save?

Three to six months is a guideline, not a rule. Move toward the higher end, or beyond, if:

- **You're the only earner** in your household, or you have children or other dependents.
- **Your income is irregular:** commission, freelance, seasonal or gig work.
- **Your job would take a long time to replace:** specialized roles, senior positions, or industries prone to layoffs.
- **You have health issues** or older family members who may need help.
- **You own a home,** especially an older one, where large repairs are likely.

Three months may be enough if you have two stable incomes, little debt, good disability insurance and in-demand skills. Retirees often keep a year or more of spending in cash, so they don't have to sell investments after a market drop.

## Emergency fund targets by monthly expenses

| Essential expenses per month | 3 months | 6 months |
|---|---|---|
| $2,500 | $7,500 | $15,000 |
| $3,500 | $10,500 | $21,000 |
| $5,000 | $15,000 | $30,000 |
| $7,500 | $22,500 | $45,000 |

The figures look large, which is why most people build them gradually. The first $1,000 matters most: according to the Federal Reserve's annual survey of household finances, a significant share of adults couldn't cover an unexpected $400 expense using cash or its equivalent.

## How the calculation works

- **Target** = essential monthly expenses × months of coverage.
- **Months covered now** = what you've saved ÷ essential monthly expenses.
- **Time to reach the target:** each month, the balance earns one month of interest (compounded daily at the rate you entered) and your deposit is added, until the balance reaches the target.
- **Monthly saving for a goal date** uses the same growth, solved for the deposit that reaches the target exactly on time, rounded up to the cent.

The **Show the math** panel shows each step with your numbers.

## How to build your fund faster

- **Start with a milestone.** Aim for $1,000 or one month of expenses first. Reaching it quickly builds momentum and covers many common surprises.
- **Automate it.** Schedule a transfer for the day after payday, so the money moves before you can spend it.
- **Save windfalls.** Tax refunds, bonuses and gifts can fill a fund much faster than monthly saving alone.
- **Use a separate account.** Keeping the fund at a different bank from your checking account makes it less tempting to dip into for non-emergencies.
- **Trim temporarily.** A few months of tighter spending, or selling things you don't use, can jump-start the fund.

How much faster? In the example, saving $200 a month takes 89 months, $500 takes 40 months and $1,000 takes 21 months.

## Where to keep it

An emergency fund has one job: to be there, at full value, the day you need it. That points to:

- **High-yield savings accounts** at FDIC-insured banks or NCUA-insured credit unions, which often pay several times the national average.
- **Money market accounts** at banks, which are also insured and may offer check-writing.
- **A small amount in checking** for same-day needs.

Avoid investments that can drop in value, such as stocks, and long-term CDs with penalties for early withdrawal. Once the fund is complete, money beyond it can go to longer-term goals where higher returns make sense.

## Should you invest your emergency fund?

It's tempting, especially when markets are rising, but the risk is timing: emergencies such as layoffs often arrive during recessions, exactly when stock prices tend to be down. Having to sell investments at a 20% or 30% loss to pay the rent turns one problem into two. Keeping the core fund in insured savings gives up a little return in exchange for certainty. If your fund is well above six months, you might keep the first several months in savings and the remainder in conservative, easy-to-sell investments.

## Using and refilling the fund

If you have to use the fund, that's what it's for. Pay for the emergency in cash rather than on a card, then pause other extra savings briefly and refill it before moving on. Re-run this calculator after any big change in your expenses, such as a new home, a new baby or paying off a car, because your target moves with your costs.

## Examples for different situations

- **A single renter with a stable salary** and $2,800 a month of essentials might aim for 3 to 4 months: $8,400 to $11,200.
- **A family with one earner, two children and a mortgage** at $5,500 a month should aim for at least 6 months, $33,000, and possibly more given home repairs.
- **A freelancer** whose income swings from month to month, with $3,200 of essentials, might keep 9 to 12 months, $28,800 to $38,400, and treat the fund as a buffer that smooths irregular income as well as covering emergencies.
- **A retiree** spending $4,000 a month beyond Social Security might keep 12 months, $48,000, in cash or short-term savings, so a market downturn doesn't force them to sell investments at a loss.

## How insurance and your emergency fund work together

Insurance protects against large, rare costs; an emergency fund covers the deductibles and the smaller surprises. Make sure the fund at least covers your health insurance out-of-pocket maximum, or a good part of it, and your car and home insurance deductibles. Disability insurance matters too: if an illness or injury stopped your income for months, a fund alone may not be enough. A health savings account, if you're eligible, can serve as a dedicated medical emergency fund with tax advantages.

## If an emergency comes before the fund is ready

If you face a big expense before your fund is complete, choose the least costly option you have. Ask the provider about a payment plan, which is often interest-free for medical bills. A credit union's small personal loan usually costs far less than a credit card cash advance or a payday loan. Use credit cards only as a short bridge, and avoid borrowing from a retirement account if you can, since withdrawals can trigger taxes and penalties and lose years of growth. Afterwards, rebuild the fund before resuming other extra savings.

## Emergency fund vs sinking funds

Not every unexpected-feeling expense is an emergency. Car registration, insurance premiums, holiday gifts and annual subscriptions come every year; they're just irregular. Setting money aside for them in separate **sinking funds**, by dividing each yearly cost by 12 and saving that amount monthly, keeps your emergency fund for real emergencies. The [savings goal calculator](/finance/savings-goal-calculator/) works out the monthly amount for each.

## Key terms

- **Emergency fund:** cash set aside for unexpected, necessary expenses or a loss of income.
- **Essential expenses:** the costs you'd keep paying in a crisis.
- **Sinking fund:** savings for a known, irregular expense.
- **High-yield savings account:** an insured savings account paying well above the national average rate.
- **Liquidity:** how quickly money can be used without losing value.
