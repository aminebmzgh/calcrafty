## How to use this inflation calculator

Choose **Compare two years** to find what an amount from one year is worth in another, or **Project the future** to see what today's prices could become.

1. **Amount.** Any dollar figure: a price, a salary, savings or a historical cost.
2. **From year and to year.** Any years from 1913 to the current year. The current year uses the latest monthly CPI, so results stay up to date during the year.
3. **Future mode.** Enter an expected inflation rate (it starts at the latest 12-month change in the CPI) and the number of years ahead.

The table under the result shows what the same amount from each decade since 1920 is worth in your chosen year.

## Worked examples

**A salary from 1990.** A $50,000 salary in 1990 had the same buying power as **$123,200.65** in 2025. Prices rose 146.4% over those 35 years, an average of 2.61% a year. Anyone earning less than that today, in nominal dollars, can buy less than a $50,000 earner could in 1990.

**$100 from 2000.** $100 in 2000 equals **$186.96** in 2025: total inflation of 86.96%, or 2.53% a year.

**The recent surge.** $100 in 2020 equals **$124.39** in 2025. Prices rose 24.39% in five years, an average of 4.46% a year, far faster than the decade before.

**A dollar from 1913.** The CPI starts in 1913, when $1 had the buying power of about **$32.58** in 2025.

## The inflation formula in plain English

**Value in later year = amount × CPI(later year) ÷ CPI(earlier year)**

**Total inflation = (CPI(later) ÷ CPI(earlier) − 1) × 100**

**Average yearly inflation = (CPI(later) ÷ CPI(earlier))^(1 ÷ years) − 1**

For the future: **future cost = today's amount × (1 + inflation rate)^years**, and **today's value of a future amount = future amount ÷ (1 + inflation rate)^years**.

The Consumer Price Index is a number, set at 100 for the average of 1982 to 1984, that tracks the cost of a broad basket of consumer goods and services. Only the ratio between two years matters. The **Show the math** panel shows the CPI values used for your result.

| Decade | Average per year | Total over the decade |
|---|---|---|

| 1960s | 2.76% | 31.28% |
| 1970s | 7.82% | 112.25% |
| 1980s | 4.72% | 58.55% |
| 1990s | 2.80% | 31.79% |
| 2000s | 2.39% | 26.63% |
| 2010s | 1.73% | 18.69% |

The 1970s stand out: prices more than doubled in ten years, driven by oil shocks and loose monetary policy, until the Federal Reserve raised interest rates sharply in the early 1980s. The 2010s had the lowest inflation of any decade on record since the 1950s.

## What inflation does to your money over time

Inflation compounds, just like interest. Here's what $1,000 of today's prices could cost in the future, and what $1,000 then would be worth in today's dollars:

| Inflation | Years | Future cost of $1,000 of today's prices | $1,000 then, in today's dollars |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2% | 10 | $1,218.99 | $820.35 |
| 3% | 10 | $1,343.92 | $744.09 |
| 3% | 20 | $1,806.11 | $553.68 |
| 3% | 30 | $2,427.26 | $411.99 |
| 4% | 20 | $2,191.12 | $456.39 |

At 3% a year, prices double in about 24 years. For retirement planning, that means income that looks comfortable in today's dollars needs to be much larger in the future; the [retirement calculator](/finance/retirement-calculator/) builds this in automatically.

## When prices fell: deflation in the 1930s

Inflation isn't guaranteed. During the Great Depression, prices fell sharply: the annual average CPI dropped from 17.158 in 1929 to 12.933 in 1933, a decline of about 24.6% in four years. Falling prices sound appealing, but deflation came with collapsing wages, mass unemployment and debts that became harder to repay as incomes shrank. That experience is one reason the Federal Reserve now aims for low, steady inflation of about 2% rather than zero.

## Inflation and interest rates

Inflation and interest rates move together. When inflation rises, the Federal Reserve typically raises its policy rate to cool spending, and rates on mortgages, car loans, credit cards and savings accounts follow. That's why savings accounts and CDs paid much more in 2023 and 2024 than in the 2010s.

What matters for savers is the **real interest rate**: the rate you earn minus inflation. A savings account paying 4% when inflation is 3% has a real return of about 1% before tax. In the late 1970s, savings rates were high, but inflation was even higher, so savers lost buying power. For borrowers, the reverse is true: a fixed-rate mortgage becomes cheaper in real terms when inflation rises, because the payment stays the same while incomes and prices grow.

## Protecting your money from inflation

- **Keep cash for short-term needs, not long-term goals.** An emergency fund belongs in safe, available savings, but money you won't need for many years usually needs investments that can outgrow inflation.
- **Look at inflation-protected investments.** Treasury Inflation-Protected Securities (TIPS) and Series I savings bonds are issued by the US government and adjust with the CPI.
- **Invest for long horizons.** Over long periods, diversified stock investments have historically grown faster than inflation, though with ups and downs along the way.
- **Negotiate your pay.** A raise that matches inflation only keeps you level; use the inflation rate as the floor in a salary conversation.
- **Lock in fixed costs when it makes sense.** Fixed-rate loans and longer leases can protect you from rising prices.

## How official programs adjust for inflation

Many government figures are indexed to price measures each year, so they keep pace with inflation automatically. Federal income tax brackets, the standard deduction and retirement account contribution limits are adjusted using the chained CPI-U, a variant that accounts for shoppers switching between products when prices change. Social Security benefits rise each year by a cost-of-living adjustment based on the CPI-W, the index for urban wage earners and clerical workers. Because these adjustments use slightly different indexes, they can differ a little from the CPI-U figures in this calculator.

## Common questions this calculator answers

- **Comparing salaries across time:** is a job offer today really better than what a parent earned decades ago?
- **Understanding historical prices:** what a price from an old newspaper, family story or history book would mean in today's money.
- **Valuing an inheritance or settlement:** how much a sum from years ago has lost or kept in real terms.
- **Checking rent or price increases:** whether a landlord's or supplier's increase is above or below general inflation.
- **Planning for the future:** what a goal like college or retirement might cost by the time you need the money.

## How the CPI is measured

The Bureau of Labor Statistics collects about 80,000 prices every month from stores, service providers, rental housing and online sellers across the country, covering food, housing, apparel, transportation, medical care, recreation, education and more. Each category is weighted by how much urban households spend on it. Housing costs, mostly rent and the equivalent rent of owned homes, make up roughly a third of the index.

A few things to keep in mind:

- **It's an average.** Your personal inflation rate depends on what you buy. Someone who rents in a fast-growing city or spends heavily on health care may face more inflation than the index shows.
- **Quality changes are adjusted for.** When a product improves, BLS tries to separate the price of the improvement from pure inflation.
- **There are other measures.** The Federal Reserve targets 2% inflation measured by a different index, the PCE price index, which usually runs a little below the CPI.

## Data notes

Past years use the annual average CPI-U, the mean of the twelve monthly index values. BLS did not publish an index for October 2025, because data collection stopped during the federal government shutdown, so the 2025 figure here averages the 11 published months. The current year uses the most recent monthly index in this calculator's data.

## Key terms

- **Inflation:** a general rise in prices, which reduces what each dollar buys.
- **Consumer Price Index (CPI):** the government's main measure of consumer prices.
- **Purchasing power:** the amount of goods and services a sum of money can buy.
- **Real vs nominal:** nominal amounts are in the dollars of their time; real amounts are adjusted for inflation.
- **Deflation:** a general fall in prices, as in the early 1930s.
- **Disinflation:** inflation slowing down while prices still rise, as happened in 2023 and 2024.
