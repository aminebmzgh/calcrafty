## How to use this tip calculator

1. **Bill amount.** The total from the check, usually before tax.
2. **Tip.** The percentage you want to leave.
3. **Splitting between.** How many people are sharing the bill equally.
4. **Round up.** Tick the box to round each person's share up to the next whole dollar, which is convenient for cash and adds a little to the tip.

The result shows the tip, the total and each person's share, along with a table of the tip, total and per-person amount at 10%, 15%, 18%, 20%, 22% and 25%.

## The formula

**Tip = bill × tip % ÷ 100**

**Total = bill + tip**

**Each person pays = total ÷ number of people**

When the total doesn't divide evenly, each share is rounded up to the next cent so the bill is covered. With rounding to the dollar turned on, each share is rounded up to the next whole dollar, and the extra goes to the tip.

## Worked example: dinner for two

The bill is $85, you're leaving 18%, and two people are splitting it.

- **Tip:** $85 × 0.18 = **$15.30**.
- **Total:** $85 + $15.30 = **$100.30**.
- **Each person:** $100.30 ÷ 2 = **$50.15**.

With rounding up turned on, each person pays **$51**, the total is $102, and the tip becomes $17, exactly **20%** of the bill.

## Calculating a tip in your head

- **10%:** move the decimal point one place left. On $47.80, that's $4.78.
- **20%:** double 10%. On $47.80, that's $9.56, or about $9.50.
- **15%:** take 10% and add half of it. On $47.80: $4.78 + $2.39 = $7.17.
- **18%:** take 20% and subtract a tenth of that 20%. On $47.80: $9.56 − $0.96 = $8.60.
- **25%:** divide by 4. On $48, that's $12.

Rounding the bill to a convenient number first makes it easier: on $47.80, think of $48, and a 20% tip of about $9.60. For cash, rounding the final total up to a whole dollar keeps things simple for everyone.

## Splitting the bill fairly

An even split is simplest, but it isn't always fair when people ordered very different amounts. Some options:

- **Split evenly** when orders were similar. This calculator does that.
- **Pay for what you ordered** and add the same tip percentage to each person's subtotal. Each person can enter their own share as the bill, with 1 person, to see their tip.
- **Split shared items evenly** and pay separately for drinks or expensive dishes.

When a group of four has a $142.60 bill with a 20% tip, the tip is $28.52 and the total $171.12, or **$42.78** each. Rounded up to the dollar, it's **$43** each: $172 in total, and a tip of $29.40, or about 20.6%.

## Tipping on the pre-tax or post-tax amount

Most etiquette advice says tipping on the pre-tax amount is acceptable, because the tax isn't part of the service. Many people tip on the total for simplicity. The difference is usually small: on a $60 meal with $5.10 of tax, a 20% tip is $12.00 on the pre-tax amount and $13.02 on the total.

Enter the pre-tax subtotal from your receipt as the bill to tip on that amount, then add the tax to the total yourself, or enter the total to tip on everything.

## Tipping on discounts, coupons and gift cards

When part of the bill is covered by a discount, coupon or gift card, etiquette guides generally suggest tipping on the amount **before** the discount, because the server did the same work. For an $80 meal with a $20-off coupon, you pay $60, but a 20% tip on the full $80 is $16, compared with $12 on the discounted $60. Enter the original amount as the bill to see the full-value tip, then subtract the discount from the total yourself.

The same applies to "comped" items the restaurant took off the bill, and to meals paid for with gift cards or loyalty points.

## Cash or card

Tipping on a card is convenient and leaves a record. Some servers prefer cash tips, since they receive them right away rather than in a later paycheck. Either way, the tip is income that should be reported. If you're splitting a bill and some people are paying in cash, rounding each share up to the dollar, as this calculator can do, avoids fiddling with coins and slightly increases the tip.

## Tip screens at the counter

Card readers at coffee shops, takeout counters and other quick-service places increasingly suggest a tip, often with preset options of 15%, 20% or 25%. Tipping in these situations has traditionally been optional, and it still is: you can choose a custom amount, a small fixed tip or no tip. If you do tip, the calculator works the same way; enter the bill and your chosen percentage.

## When service is poor

A tip rewards service, but servers often depend on tips for much of their income, and problems in the kitchen or with staffing may not be the server's fault. If service was genuinely poor, many people still leave a reduced tip, around 10% to 15%, and speak to a manager about the problem. Leaving no tip at all is usually reserved for serious issues.

## Large groups

Restaurants often add an automatic gratuity, commonly around 18% to 20%, for parties of six or eight or more, because a large table takes more work and ties up a section. Read the menu or ask when booking. If no gratuity is added, tip as you would normally; splitting the bill evenly with this calculator is the quickest way to settle up.

## Service charges and automatic gratuity

Some restaurants add a service charge or automatic gratuity, often for large groups, at certain events, or as a standard policy. Check the bill before tipping so you don't tip twice. A service charge may or may not go to the staff, so it's reasonable to ask how it's distributed. If you received exceptional service, you can still add a little extra.

## How much to tip in common situations

Tipping customs vary by service and region, and they've shifted in recent years. Broadly, in the United States:

- **Sit-down restaurants:** 15% to 20% of the pre-tax bill, with 20% common for good service.
- **Bartenders:** about $1 to $2 per drink, or 15% to 20% of the tab.
- **Food delivery:** around 15% to 20%, with a few dollars as a minimum for small orders.
- **Takeout and counter service:** optional; many people leave a small amount or round up.
- **Hair and personal services:** around 15% to 20% of the service price.
- **Taxis and rideshares:** about 15% to 20% of the fare.

Our guide [how much to tip](/guides/how-much-to-tip/) goes into each situation, including hotels, movers and tipping outside the U.S.

## Tipping outside the United States

Tipping customs differ a lot from country to country. In much of Europe, service is often included in menu prices, and leaving a small amount or rounding up is common rather than a fixed percentage. In Japan, tipping is generally not expected and can even cause confusion. In other places, a service charge is added to the bill automatically. Check local customs before you travel; the percentage table in this calculator still helps when you decide to tip.

## Budgeting for tips

Tips add up, especially if you eat out or order delivery often. A $15 delivery order with a $3 tip, three times a week, is about $468 a year in tips alone, on top of the food. When you plan a budget for dining out, include the tip and tax so the real cost isn't a surprise. For a meal that costs $50 before tax, plan on roughly $60 to $65 once tax and a 20% tip are added, depending on your local sales tax.

## Tips and taxes for workers

For people who receive tips, they're income. Under IRS rules, employees who receive $20 or more in cash and card tips in a month must report them to their employer, who withholds income and payroll taxes. For tax years 2025 through 2028, a federal deduction lets eligible workers in occupations that customarily receive tips deduct up to $25,000 of qualified tips a year from federal taxable income, with the deduction phasing out at higher incomes. Tips are still subject to Social Security and Medicare taxes. If you earn tips, the [paycheck calculator](/finance/paycheck-calculator/) helps estimate take-home pay, and the [percentage calculator](/math/percentage-calculator/) handles other percentage questions.

## Key terms

- **Tip (gratuity):** an extra amount paid for service, usually a percentage of the bill.
- **Service charge:** a fee added to the bill by the business, which may or may not go to staff.
- **Automatic gratuity:** a tip added to the bill automatically, common for large parties.
- **Pre-tax subtotal:** the cost of food and drinks before sales tax.
- **Split check:** dividing a restaurant bill between several people, evenly or by what each ordered.
- **Tip reporting:** an employee telling their employer about tips received, so taxes can be withheld.
