## How to use this refinance calculator

Enter your current loan, the new offer and your plans, and the calculator compares the two paths month by month. It shows how much the payment changes, when the refinance pays for itself and whether it actually saves money over the time you'll keep it.

1. **Current loan.** Your remaining balance, interest rate and years left, all on your latest mortgage statement.
2. **New interest rate.** The rate starts at this week's Freddie Mac average for a 30-year fixed loan; use the rate on your Loan Estimate if you have one.
3. **New loan term.** 30 years gives the lowest payment; a term that matches your remaining years keeps your payoff date the same.
4. **Closing costs.** The total from your Loan Estimate.
5. **Years you'll keep the loan.** Be realistic: if you might move or refinance again, use that horizon.
6. **More options.** Tick the box if the closing costs will be added to the new loan instead of paid at closing.

The figures compare principal and interest only. Property tax and insurance don't change when you refinance.

## Worked example: $280,000 at 7.25% with 26 years left

A homeowner owes $280,000 at 7.25%, with 26 years left and a payment of $1,996.52. A lender offers 6.25% with $5,600 in closing costs, and they expect to stay about 8 years. Here's how the four common terms compare:

| New term | New payment | Monthly change | True break-even | Ahead after 8 years | Lifetime result |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 30 years | $1,724.01 | −$272.51 | 25 months | $14,037.97 | −$3,326.74 |
| 25 years | $1,847.07 | −$149.45 | 24 months | $17,501.40 | +$63,191.03 |
| 20 years | $2,046.60 | +$50.08 | 23 months | $23,116.96 | +$126,131.47 |
| 15 years | $2,400.78 | +$404.26 | 22 months | $33,085.02 | +$185,173.49 |

The 30-year loan has the biggest monthly saving, but because it adds four years of payments, it costs $3,326.74 more over its full life than simply keeping the current loan. The 25-year loan keeps roughly the same payoff date and saves $63,191. The 20- and 15-year options raise the payment yet still come out ahead quickly, because every extra dollar pays down principal at the lower rate.

## The two break-even calculations

**The classic break-even** divides closing costs by monthly savings: $5,600 ÷ $272.51 = 20.6, rounded up to **21 months** for the 30-year option. It's quick, but it treats every dollar of savings as profit.

**The true break-even** asks a better question: at what month are you better off, counting everything you've paid plus what you still owe? A new 30-year loan pays down principal more slowly than a loan you've already been paying for years, so part of the lower payment is simply slower repayment. Counting that, the 30-year refinance doesn't pull ahead until **month 25**.

For shorter terms the true break-even comes sooner than the classic one, or exists even when the payment rises: the 20-year option has no classic break-even at all, since its payment is higher, yet it's ahead from month 23. This calculator always shows the true break-even, with the classic version in **Show the math**.

## How the rate drop changes the answer

For a $300,000 balance at 7.5% with 27 years left, refinancing into a new 30-year loan with $6,000 of closing costs, kept for 7 years:

| New rate | Monthly savings | Classic break-even | True break-even | Ahead after 7 years |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 7.0% | $166.29 | 37 months | 53 months | $2,925.28 |
| 6.5% | $266.00 | 23 months | 25 months | $13,493.62 |
| 6.0% | $363.55 | 17 months | 17 months | $24,018.77 |
| 5.5% | $458.83 | 14 months | 13 months | $34,493.22 |

A half-point drop barely pays within seven years, while a full point is comfortably worth it. With smaller drops, the gap between the classic and true break-even is widest, so the shortcut is most misleading exactly when the decision is closest.

## How long you keep the loan matters most

Closing costs are paid on day one; the savings arrive month by month. For the 30-year option in the worked example, the result after selling or refinancing again:

| Keep the new loan for | Result vs not refinancing |
|---|---|
| 2 years | −$134.67 |
| 3 years | +$2,481.59 |
| 5 years | +$7,439.33 |
| 8 years | +$14,037.97 |
| 10 years | +$17,738.24 |

Leaving within two years loses money. If there's a real chance you'll move soon, a smaller or no-closing-cost offer may be the better choice even at a slightly higher rate.

## Paying closing costs upfront vs rolling them in

Rolling the $5,600 into the 30-year loan raises the balance to $285,600 and the payment to $1,758.49, about $34 more than paying the costs upfront. The true break-even moves from 25 to 29 months, and the 8-year result falls from $14,037.97 to $11,387.55, because you pay interest on the costs for as long as you keep the loan. Rolling costs in makes sense when cash is better used elsewhere, such as keeping an emergency fund intact, but it isn't free.

## When refinancing isn't worth it

The calculator can show a lower payment even when refinancing is a poor choice. Be cautious when:

- **You'll move or refinance again within the true break-even period.** You'd pay the costs without collecting enough savings.
- **The savings come only from a longer term.** A lower payment from stretching the loan out isn't a saving; check the lifetime result.
- **You're far into your current loan.** With 10 or 15 years left, most of each payment already goes to principal. Restarting a 30-year schedule moves you back to interest-heavy payments.
- **Your credit or income has changed.** The quoted rate assumes you qualify for it; a lower score or higher debt-to-income ratio can mean a higher rate or extra fees.
- **The loan has a prepayment penalty.** Rare on modern mortgages, but check your current note.

## Should you pay points to lower the rate?

Lenders often let you pay discount points, usually 1% of the loan per point, for a lower rate. Points add to the closing costs, so they push the break-even later. Enter the higher closing costs and the lower rate as one scenario and save it with **Add to compare**, then enter the no-points offer as another. If you expect to keep the loan longer than the point where the two lines cross, the points are worth paying; if not, take the higher rate with lower costs.

## Rate-and-term vs cash-out refinancing

This calculator models a **rate-and-term** refinance, where the new loan replaces the old balance. A **cash-out** refinance borrows more than you owe and pays you the difference, which raises the balance and usually comes with a slightly higher rate. It can make sense for a major renovation or to replace high-interest debt, but it turns that money into long-term mortgage debt secured by your home. To borrow a smaller amount without giving up a low first-mortgage rate, compare a home equity line of credit instead.

## What you'll need to apply

Having these ready speeds up a refinance: your latest mortgage statement, two recent pay stubs, the last two years of W-2s or tax returns, recent bank and retirement account statements, your homeowners insurance details, and photo ID. Self-employed borrowers usually also need profit-and-loss statements. Most lenders order a new appraisal, although some streamline programs skip it.

## The formulas in plain English

- **Payments:** both loans use the standard mortgage formula, M = P × r(1 + r)ⁿ ÷ ((1 + r)ⁿ − 1), where r is the annual rate ÷ 12 and n is the number of months.
- **Monthly savings:** current payment − new payment.
- **True break-even:** the first month in which (everything paid on the current loan + its remaining balance) ≥ (everything paid on the new loan + its remaining balance + upfront costs).
- **Lifetime result:** interest left on the current loan − interest on the new loan − closing costs.

Both schedules are calculated month by month in exact decimal, with interest rounded to the cent as lenders do.

## Key terms

- **Rate-and-term refinance:** a new loan that changes the rate, the term or both, without taking cash out.
- **Closing costs:** fees for the new loan, typically 2% to 5% of the amount borrowed.
- **Break-even point:** the month when the refinance has paid for itself.
- **Term reset:** starting a new amortization schedule, which can extend the payoff date.
- **Loan Estimate:** the standard three-page disclosure every lender must give you within three business days of applying.
