## How to use this CD calculator

Enter your deposit, the APY and the term, and the calculator shows the balance at maturity. It also works out how much you keep after tax and inflation, and what an early withdrawal would cost.

1. **Deposit.** The amount you'll put in when you open the CD.
2. **APY.** The annual percentage yield the bank advertises. It's the number to compare between CDs and savings accounts.
3. **Term.** CDs typically run from 3 months to 5 years. Longer terms used to pay more, but when short-term rates are high, shorter CDs can pay as much or more.
4. **Tax rate and inflation.** Your combined federal and state rate on interest, and the inflation rate (it starts at the latest 12-month change in the Consumer Price Index).
5. **More options.** Enter a month to cash out early and the penalty in months of interest from your account agreement.

## Worked example: $25,000 in an 18-month CD

A saver puts $25,000 into an 18-month CD at 4.40% APY. They're in the 22% federal bracket with a 2% state tax (24% in total), and inflation is running at 3%.

- **Balance at maturity:** 25,000 × 1.044^1.5 = **$26,668.02**
- **Interest earned:** **$1,668.02**
- **After 24% tax:** **$1,267.70**
- **Real return after tax and inflation:** about **0.34% a year**
- **Equivalent APR with daily compounding:** 4.306%

The real return shows what the CD really does: it protects the deposit and roughly keeps pace with prices after tax, but it doesn't grow your buying power much. That's what CDs are for: safety and a known result, not long-term growth.

If the saver needed the money after 9 months, the bank's penalty of 6 months' interest would be $538.28. They'd have earned $820.54 by then, so they'd walk away with **$25,282.26**, still ahead, but with most of the interest gone.

## The CD formulas in plain English

**Balance at maturity = deposit × (1 + APY)^(months ÷ 12)**

The APY already includes compounding, so you don't need to know whether the bank compounds daily or monthly to work out the result. That only matters for converting APY to the underlying interest rate:

**APR = n × ((1 + APY)^(1/n) − 1)**, where n is 365 for daily compounding, 12 for monthly, 4 for quarterly and 1 for annual.

The **early withdrawal penalty** is usually stated as a number of months of simple interest: **deposit × APR ÷ 12 × months of penalty**. Some banks calculate it on the amount withdrawn rather than the full deposit, so check your agreement. The **Show the math** panel shows each step with your numbers.

## What CDs earn by term

$10,000 at sample rates (check current offers; rates change often):

| Term | Sample APY | Balance at maturity | Interest |
|---|---|---|---|
| 6 months | 4.50% | $10,222.52 | $222.52 |
| 1 year | 4.25% | $10,425.00 | $425.00 |
| 2 years | 4.00% | $10,816.00 | $816.00 |
| 3 years | 3.90% | $11,216.22 | $1,216.22 |
| 5 years | 3.80% | $12,049.99 | $2,049.99 |

When short-term rates are higher than long-term ones, as in this sample, the yield curve is said to be inverted. A short CD earns more per year now, but you'll have to reinvest at whatever rate is available when it matures. A long CD locks in today's rate for longer, which pays off if rates fall.

## How early withdrawal penalties can eat your principal

Penalties are fixed when you open the CD, but how much they hurt depends on when you withdraw. On a $10,000, 5-year CD at 4.25% APY:

| Penalty | Cash out after | Interest earned | Penalty | You get back |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 3 months of interest | 3 months | $104.60 | $104.06 | $10,000.54 |
| 6 months of interest | 3 months | $104.60 | $208.12 | $9,896.48 |
| 12 months of interest | 6 months | $210.29 | $416.24 | $9,794.05 |
| 12 months of interest | 24 months | $868.06 | $416.24 | $10,451.82 |

Breaking a CD in its first months can return less than you deposited, which surprises many savers who think of CDs as risk-free. Deposit insurance protects you if the bank fails; it doesn't protect you from a penalty you agreed to. Read the penalty terms before opening, and keep your emergency fund in a savings account instead.

## Taxes and the real return

CD interest is taxed as ordinary income each year it's credited, even before the CD matures, unless you hold the CD inside an IRA. That can turn a decent-looking yield into a small real return. On a 1-year, 4.25% CD with 3% inflation:

| Tax rate on interest | Real return after tax and inflation |
|---|---|
| 12% | 0.72% |
| 22% | 0.31% |
| 32% | −0.11% |

For higher earners, Treasury bills and notes, whose interest is exempt from state and local income tax, can sometimes beat a CD with a slightly higher APY. Holding CDs in a traditional or Roth IRA avoids the yearly tax altogether.

## Building a CD ladder

A CD ladder spreads money across several maturities so part of it becomes available regularly. With $50,000, you might buy five $10,000 CDs maturing in 1, 2, 3, 4 and 5 years. Each year, one CD matures; you either use the money or reinvest it in a new 5-year CD at the top of the ladder.

After the first few years, every rung is a 5-year CD earning the higher long-term rate, yet $10,000 still comes due every year. A ladder reduces the risk of locking everything in just before rates rise, and of having nothing available when you need it. The same idea works on a shorter scale: four 3-, 6-, 9- and 12-month CDs give you access to a quarter of the money every three months.

## CDs vs other safe places for cash

| Option | Rate | Access to money | Protection |
|---|---|---|---|
| High-yield savings account | Variable, can change anytime | Anytime | FDIC or NCUA insured |
| CD | Fixed for the term | Penalty before maturity | FDIC or NCUA insured |
| Money market fund | Variable | Usually within a day | Not insured; invests in short-term debt |
| Treasury bills | Fixed to maturity (4 to 52 weeks) | Sell before maturity at market price | Backed by the US government; no state income tax |
| I bonds | Fixed rate plus inflation adjustment | Locked for 1 year; penalty before 5 years | Backed by the US government |

A CD makes the most sense when you have a known date for the money, such as a tuition payment or a home purchase next year, and want a guaranteed result. For money you might need suddenly, the flexibility of a savings account is usually worth the slightly lower rate.

## How to choose a CD

1. **Match the term to when you'll need the money.** A CD that matures a month after your purchase date is no use.
2. **Compare APYs across banks and credit unions.** Online banks and credit unions often pay noticeably more than large branch banks.
3. **Read the early withdrawal penalty.** Between two similar rates, a smaller penalty is worth a lot of flexibility.
4. **Check the minimum deposit and the renewal terms.** Some CDs renew automatically into a lower-rate product.
5. **Stay within insurance limits,** spreading large balances across institutions if needed.

## What happens when a CD matures

At maturity you can withdraw the money, move it elsewhere or let it renew. Most banks give a grace period of about a week or two; if you do nothing, the CD usually rolls into a new one of the same term at the bank's current rate, which may be lower than its best promotional offers. The interest you earned can typically be withdrawn without penalty at maturity, and some CDs let you take interest out monthly along the way, although that means it no longer compounds.

## Types of CDs

- **Traditional CD:** fixed rate, fixed term, penalty for early withdrawal.
- **No-penalty CD:** lets you withdraw after a short initial period without a penalty, usually at a lower rate.
- **Bump-up CD:** lets you raise the rate once or twice if the bank's rates go up.
- **Jumbo CD:** for large deposits, often $100,000 or more, sometimes at a slightly higher rate.
- **Brokered CD:** bought through a brokerage account and tradable before maturity; selling early can mean a gain or a loss depending on rates, instead of a fixed penalty.

## Key terms

- **APY (annual percentage yield):** the yearly yield including compounding.
- **Maturity:** the date the CD ends and you can withdraw without a penalty.
- **Grace period:** a short window after maturity, often 7 to 10 days, to withdraw or change terms before the CD renews automatically.
- **Early withdrawal penalty:** the cost of taking money out before maturity.
- **FDIC/NCUA insurance:** federal protection for deposits up to $250,000 per depositor, per insured institution, per ownership category.
