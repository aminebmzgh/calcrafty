## How to use this overtime calculator

1. **Hourly rate.** Your regular pay per hour before tax.
2. **Overtime rule.** Choose **Federal** for the 40-hour weekly rule that applies in most states, or **California** for its daily overtime and double-time rules.
3. **Hours.** For the federal rule, enter your total hours for the workweek. For California, enter how many hours you work each day and how many days; 7 means seven consecutive days.
4. **Multiplier (More options).** 1.5 is the legal minimum. Change it if your contract pays more, such as double time for every overtime hour.

The result shows your weekly pay before tax, split into regular, overtime and (for California) double-time pay, what you'd earn in a year at this schedule, and your average pay per hour worked. The **Show the math** panel lists each step.

## The formula

**Overtime rate = regular rate × 1.5**

**Weekly pay = regular hours × rate + overtime hours × rate × 1.5 + double-time hours × rate × 2**

Under the federal rule, regular hours are the first 40 in the week and overtime hours are everything above that. Under California's rule, hours are split day by day first, then any regular hours above 40 in the week become overtime, without counting any hour twice.

## Worked example: 50 hours at $25

- **Regular pay:** 40 hours × $25 = **$1,000**.
- **Overtime rate:** $25 × 1.5 = **$37.50**.
- **Overtime pay:** 10 hours × $37.50 = **$375**.
- **Weekly pay:** $1,000 + $375 = **$1,375** before tax.
- **Yearly, at this schedule every week:** $1,375 × 52 = **$71,500**.
- **Average per hour worked:** $1,375 ÷ 50 = **$27.50**.

## What extra hours are worth

At $25 an hour under the federal rule:

| Hours in the week | Overtime hours | Weekly pay | Yearly at this schedule | Average per hour |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 40 | 0 | $1,000.00 | $52,000 | $25.00 |
| 45 | 5 | $1,187.50 | $61,750 | $26.39 |
| 50 | 10 | $1,375.00 | $71,500 | $27.50 |
| 55 | 15 | $1,562.50 | $81,250 | $28.41 |
| 60 | 20 | $1,750.00 | $91,000 | $29.17 |

Each overtime hour is worth $37.50 before tax, 50% more than a regular hour. Working 60 hours every week would raise yearly pay by $39,000, but long hours have a cost too: fatigue, safety, and the time you give up. Use the yearly figure as a guide, not a plan.

## Federal rules: the Fair Labor Standards Act

The Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA) is the federal law behind overtime. Its main rules:

- **Who it covers:** non-exempt employees, which includes nearly all hourly workers.
- **The threshold:** hours over 40 in a **workweek**, a fixed, recurring period of seven consecutive days that your employer sets. It doesn't have to run Monday to Sunday.
- **The rate:** at least 1.5 times the **regular rate**, which includes most non-discretionary bonuses, shift differentials and commissions, not just the base hourly rate.
- **No averaging:** each workweek stands alone. Working 30 hours one week and 50 the next still earns 10 hours of overtime in the second week.
- **No daily overtime:** a 12-hour day doesn't earn overtime under federal law if the week stays at or under 40 hours.
- **Comp time instead of pay:** private employers can't substitute time off for overtime pay. Some public-sector employers can.

## When a bonus changes your overtime rate

Overtime must be based on the **regular rate**, which includes most non-discretionary pay such as production bonuses, attendance bonuses, shift differentials and commissions. This calculator uses your hourly rate, so if you earned a bonus, the true overtime pay is a little higher.

Example: you earn $20 an hour, work 50 hours, and receive a $100 production bonus for the week.

- **Straight-time pay:** 50 hours × $20 = $1,000, plus the $100 bonus = $1,100.
- **Regular rate:** $1,100 ÷ 50 hours = **$22 an hour**.
- **Overtime premium:** the extra half of the regular rate for the 10 overtime hours, 10 × $11 = **$110**.
- **Total pay:** $1,100 + $110 = **$1,210**.

If the bonus were left out of the regular rate, the premium would be 10 × $10 = $100, and you'd be underpaid by $10. Discretionary bonuses (paid at the employer's sole choice, not promised in advance), gifts and expense reimbursements are excluded from the regular rate.

## Salaried, but still non-exempt

Some salaried employees are entitled to overtime: anyone who earns less than the federal salary threshold, or whose duties don't meet an exemption test. For a non-exempt employee paid a fixed salary for a 40-hour week, the regular rate is the weekly salary ÷ 40. A $1,000-a-week salary gives a $25 regular rate, so 10 overtime hours earn $375, the same as the hourly example above.

## California rules: daily overtime and double time

California is the best-known state with daily overtime. For non-exempt employees:

- **Time and a half** for hours over 8 in a workday, and for hours over 40 in a workweek.
- **Double time** for hours over 12 in a workday.
- **Seventh consecutive day:** time and a half for the first 8 hours, double time after 8.

Here's how the same hours compare at $20 an hour:

| Schedule | Federal pay | California pay | Why it differs |
|---|---|---|---|
| 4 days × 10 hours (40 h) | $800 | $880 | California pays 2 overtime hours each day |
| 5 days × 10 hours (50 h) | $1,100 | $1,100 | Both count 10 overtime hours |
| 6 days × 9 hours (54 h) | $1,220 | $1,220 | Both count 14 overtime hours, by different routes |
| 5 days × 13 hours (65 h) | $1,550 | $1,600 | California pays 5 of the hours at double time |
| 7 days × 8 hours (56 h) | $1,280 | $1,280 | Both count 16 overtime hours |

California's daily rule doesn't always pay more. It makes the biggest difference when long days come in a short week, like a four-day, 40-hour schedule, or when days run past 12 hours. In the 6 × 9 row, the federal rule counts 54 − 40 = 14 overtime hours; California counts 6 daily overtime hours plus 8 regular hours that push the week past 40, which is also 14. No hour is ever paid twice.

Some other states also have daily overtime, including Alaska, Colorado and Nevada (for some workers), and some set overtime rules for particular industries. Where state and federal law differ, the rule that's better for the worker applies. Check your state's labor department for the details.

## Is overtime worth it after tax?

Overtime pay is taxed like the rest of your wages. To see what you'd actually keep, enter your higher weekly gross in the [paycheck calculator](/finance/paycheck-calculator/). Two things to know:

- **Withholding can look high.** Payroll systems often withhold as though every paycheck were that large all year, so a big overtime check may have more tax taken out. The difference is reconciled when you file your return.
- **The federal overtime deduction.** For tax years 2025 through 2028, a deduction of up to $12,500 a year ($25,000 for married couples filing jointly) applies to the premium portion of qualified overtime pay, the extra half in time and a half. It phases out at higher incomes, applies to federal income tax only, and doesn't reduce Social Security or Medicare tax.

## Exempt vs non-exempt

Not everyone is entitled to overtime. Under the FLSA, an employee is exempt only if they're paid a salary of at least $684 a week ($35,568 a year) **and** their main job duties are executive, administrative or professional, as defined in the regulations. Some states, including California, New York and Washington, set higher salary thresholds. Being paid a salary, or having "manager" in your job title, doesn't by itself make you exempt.

If you're unsure, look at your duties rather than your title, and check with your state labor department or the U.S. Department of Labor's Wage and Hour Division.

## Tips for tracking overtime

- **Keep your own record of hours,** including start and end times and unpaid breaks. Our [time card calculator](/everyday/time-card-calculator/) totals a week from your clock-in and clock-out times.
- **Know your workweek.** Ask your employer when it starts and ends, since overtime is counted within that fixed period.
- **Check your pay stub** for the overtime hours and rate, and that the overtime rate includes any bonuses or shift differentials. Our guide on [how to read a pay stub](/guides/how-to-read-a-pay-stub/) explains each line.
- **Report errors quickly.** Unpaid overtime claims under federal law generally must be filed within two years, or three for willful violations.

## Key terms

- **Non-exempt:** an employee covered by overtime rules.
- **Exempt:** an employee excluded from overtime rules because of their salary level and job duties, such as many managers and professionals.
- **Overtime premium:** the extra half-time on top of straight-time pay; it's the part covered by the 2025–2028 federal overtime deduction.
- **Workweek:** the fixed seven-day period over which overtime is counted.
- **Regular rate:** the hourly rate overtime is based on, including most bonuses and differentials.
- **Time and a half:** 1.5 times the regular rate.
- **Double time:** twice the regular rate.
- **Comp time:** time off instead of overtime pay, generally allowed only for public-sector employers in the U.S.
