  - q: What is my ideal weight for my height?
    a: There's no single ideal weight, but formulas give a reference point. For a woman who is 5 feet 6 inches, the Robinson, Miller, Devine and Hamwi formulas give 129 to 135 pounds, and a healthy BMI corresponds to about 115 to 154 pounds. Any weight within the healthy BMI range is considered healthy for most adults.
  - q: Which ideal weight formula is most accurate?
    a: None is "most accurate," because they weren't built to define a healthy weight for individuals. The Devine formula (1974) was created to dose medication and is still the most widely used in hospitals. Robinson and Miller (both 1983) re-fitted the idea to life-insurance height-weight tables. The spread between them is a reminder that a range is more meaningful than a single number.
  - q: How is ideal body weight calculated?
    a: Each formula starts from a base weight at 5 feet and adds a fixed amount per inch above that. The Devine formula, for example, is 50 kg plus 2.3 kg per inch over 5 feet for men, and 45.5 kg plus 2.3 kg per inch for women. A man who is 5 feet 10 inches gets 50 + 2.3 × 10 = 73 kg, about 161 pounds.
  - q: Do these formulas work for people under 5 feet?
    a: Not well. They were designed for heights of 5 feet and over, and extending them below that gives very low numbers. For shorter adults, the healthy BMI range is a better reference, and a doctor or dietitian can give more personal guidance.
  - q: Does ideal weight account for muscle or body frame?
    a: No. The formulas use only height and sex, so a muscular person or someone with a large frame may be perfectly healthy well above them. Body fat percentage and waist size give a better sense of whether your weight is healthy for you.
  - q: Why do doctors use ideal body weight?
    a: Mainly for clinical calculations. Some medications and ventilator settings are based on ideal body weight rather than actual weight, because drugs distribute differently in fat and lean tissue. That's the purpose the Devine formula was originally designed for.

## How to use this ideal weight calculator

Pick your units and sex, enter your height, and the calculator shows four classic ideal body weight formulas alongside the healthy weight range from BMI. Adding your current weight puts it on the same chart for comparison.

1. **Units.** Pounds and feet/inches or kilograms and centimeters; switching converts what you've entered.
2. **Sex.** Each formula has separate versions for men and women.
3. **Height.** Measure without shoes. The formulas are designed for heights of 5 feet (152 cm) and over.
4. **Current weight.** Optional; it's only used for the comparison chart.

## Worked example: a woman who is 5 feet 6 inches

A woman who is 5 feet 6 inches is 6 inches over 5 feet. Plugging that into each formula:

| Formula | Calculation | Ideal weight |
|---|---|---|
| Robinson (1983) | 49 + 1.7 × 6 = 59.2 kg | about 130 lb |
| Miller (1983) | 53.1 + 1.36 × 6 = 61.3 kg | about 135 lb |
| Devine (1974) | 45.5 + 2.3 × 6 = 59.3 kg | about 131 lb |
| Hamwi (1964) | 45.5 + 2.2 × 6 = 58.7 kg | about 129 lb |

The average is about 131 pounds. The healthy BMI range for her height is much wider: about **115 to 154 pounds**.

For a man who is 6 feet 1 inch, the formulas spread further apart, from about 164 pounds (Miller) to 183 pounds (Hamwi), while the healthy BMI range is roughly 140 to 189 pounds. The taller you are, the more the formulas disagree, because they add different amounts per inch.

## The formulas in plain English

All four use the same shape: **a base weight at 5 feet, plus a fixed amount for each inch over 5 feet**, with separate numbers for men and women.

- **Robinson:** men 52 kg + 1.9 kg per inch; women 49 kg + 1.7 kg per inch
- **Miller:** men 56.2 kg + 1.41 kg per inch; women 53.1 kg + 1.36 kg per inch
- **Devine:** men 50 kg + 2.3 kg per inch; women 45.5 kg + 2.3 kg per inch
- **Hamwi:** men 48 kg + 2.7 kg per inch; women 45.5 kg + 2.2 kg per inch

The healthy BMI range uses a different approach: weight = BMI × height², with BMI values of 18.5 and 24.9. Calcrafty converts heights and weights with exact factors (1 inch = 2.54 cm, 1 pound = 0.45359237 kg). The **Show the math** panel shows each formula with your height.

## Where these formulas came from

The Hamwi formula appeared in 1964 as a quick rule of thumb for diabetes diet planning. Devine published his in 1974 to calculate doses of the antibiotic gentamicin, and it spread through hospital pharmacy. In 1983, Robinson and Miller each produced versions fitted to the Metropolitan Life Insurance height and weight tables. A review in the Annals of Pharmacotherapy (2000) traced these origins and noted that none was designed to set personal weight goals.

That history explains both their usefulness and their limits. They're consistent and easy to compute, which suits clinical calculations, but they ignore body composition, frame size, age and ethnicity.

## How to use the result

Treat the formulas as a rough reference and the healthy BMI range as the broader target. If your weight is within the BMI range, you're generally considered to be at a healthy weight even if you're above or below the formula values. If you're outside the range, small changes help: losing 5% to 10% of body weight improves blood pressure, blood sugar and cholesterol for many people.

## Key terms

- **Ideal body weight (IBW):** a weight estimated from height and sex by a formula, used mainly in medicine.
- **Healthy weight range:** the weights that give a BMI of 18.5 to 24.9 at your height.
- **Frame size:** skeletal build; people with larger frames often weigh more at a healthy body fat level.
- **Lean body weight:** weight minus fat mass, sometimes used instead of ideal weight for dosing.
