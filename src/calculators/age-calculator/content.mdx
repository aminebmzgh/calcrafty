## How to use this age calculator

Enter a date of birth and the calculator shows the exact age today in years, months and days. To find someone's age on a particular date, past or future, fill in **Age on this date** as well.

1. **Date of birth.** Use the date picker or type the date.
2. **Age on this date.** Leave it empty for today, or pick any date to see the age on that day.

You'll also see the age expressed as total months, weeks, days and hours, the day of the week you were born, and a countdown to the next birthday.

## Worked example: born May 15, 1990

On November 1, 2026, someone born on May 15, 1990 is:

- **36 years, 5 months and 17 days** old
- **437 months** and 17 days
- **1,902 weeks** and 5 days
- **13,319 days**, or about 319,656 hours

Here's how the years, months and days are counted:

1. The most recent birthday was May 15, 2026: that's **36 years**.
2. Counting forward month by month, June 15, July 15, August 15, September 15 and October 15 are five more monthly anniversaries: **5 months**.
3. From October 15 to November 1 is **17 days**, because October has 31 days.

They were born on a **Tuesday**, and their next birthday, when they turn 37, is Saturday, May 15, 2027, **195 days** away.

## How age is calculated in plain English

The calculator works with calendar dates rather than clock times, so time zones and daylight saving time never shift the answer.

- **Years and months:** it finds the largest number of whole calendar months you can add to the birth date without passing the target date. Twelve months make a year.
- **Days:** the remaining days from that last monthly anniversary to the target date.
- **Short months:** adding a month to January 31 lands on February 28 (or 29 in a leap year), so month counts never skip or produce negative days.
- **Total days:** the plain difference between the two dates, counting every leap day.

Leap years follow the Gregorian rules: every year divisible by 4 is a leap year, except years divisible by 100, unless they're also divisible by 400. That's why 2000 was a leap year but 1900 wasn't.

## Age milestones

Many rules depend on an exact age on a specific date. Common US examples:

- **18:** voting age and legal adulthood in most states.
- **21:** the minimum legal drinking age.
- **26:** the age until which young adults can usually stay on a parent's health plan.
- **50:** retirement plan catch-up contributions become available.
- **59½:** penalty-free withdrawals from most retirement accounts.
- **62:** the earliest age to claim Social Security retirement benefits.
- **65:** Medicare eligibility for most people.
- **67:** full Social Security retirement age for people born in 1960 or later.

Enter the relevant date in **Age on this date** to check eligibility precisely.

## Ages in other units and systems

The same span of time can be described several ways, and the right one depends on the question.

- **Completed years** are what forms, laws and eligibility rules almost always mean by "age." You don't turn 18 until the 18th anniversary of your birth date.
- **Age in months** is common for babies and toddlers, because development changes quickly. Pediatric growth charts, for example, are plotted by month for the first years of life.
- **Age in days** is used for newborns, in some medical and research settings, and for fun milestones: your 10,000th day arrives when you're about 27 years and 5 months old.
- **Age in weeks** is the standard for pregnancy and early infancy.

Other cultures count differently. Under the traditional East Asian system, a baby is one year old at birth and everyone gains a year at the New Year; South Korea officially switched to international age counting for legal and administrative purposes in 2023.

For legal questions, check the exact wording of the rule. Some laws say "at least 18 years of age," some say "under 21 on the date of the event," and a few count age differently for people born on February 29. Entering the relevant date in **Age on this date** shows exactly where you stand.

Your exact age is calculated entirely in your browser; the dates you enter are never sent anywhere.

## Leap-day birthdays

People born on February 29 only see their birth date on the calendar once every four years, so when do they turn a year older in other years? This calculator counts the birthday on **February 28** in common years, because adding a whole number of years to February 29 lands on the last day of February.

For someone born on February 29, 2000:

| Date | Age shown |
|---|---|
| February 27, 2025 | 24 years, 11 months, 29 days |
| February 28, 2025 | **25 years**, 0 months, 0 days |
| March 1, 2025 | 25 years, 0 months, 1 day |

Not every rule works this way. Some laws and organizations treat March 1 as the birthday in a common year, which can matter for a driving test, a drinking age or a benefit that starts on a birthday. When it matters, check the specific rule and enter the date it names in **Age on this date**.

## Finding the age difference between two people

To find the exact gap between two people's ages, enter the older person's birth date as the birth date and the younger person's birth date in **Age on this date**. For someone born March 3, 1985 and someone born August 20, 1992, the difference is **7 years, 5 months and 17 days**, or 2,727 days. The same trick works for any two dates: an anniversary, a company's founding or how long a pet has been part of the family.

## Fun age milestones

Beyond birthdays, some people celebrate round numbers of days or seconds. For someone born May 15, 1990:

- **10,000 days old:** September 30, 2017, at about 27 years and 4½ months.
- **1 billion seconds old:** about 11,574 days, or 31.7 years, after birth.
- **20,000 days old:** February 15, 2045, at about 54 years and 9 months.
- **25,000 days old:** October 25, 2058, at about 68½ years.

Enter your birth date and look at the total days in the result to see how close you are to your own next milestone.

## Why age calculators sometimes disagree

If two calculators give slightly different answers, the difference usually comes down to two choices. The first is how they handle month ends: going from January 31 to February 28 can be counted as a full month or as 28 days. The second is whether they count by calendar dates or by elapsed time in hours, which time zones and daylight saving time can shift by a day near midnight. This calculator uses calendar dates and whole calendar months throughout, which matches how birthdays and legal ages are normally counted.

## Calculating age in a spreadsheet

Excel and Google Sheets can work out age too. The common formula is **DATEDIF(birth date, today's date, "Y")** for completed years, with "YM" for the extra months. Its "MD" option, for leftover days, can give wrong or negative results around the ends of months, and Microsoft documents it as unreliable. For an exact years-months-days answer, this calculator avoids that problem by counting calendar months directly.

## School and eligibility cutoff dates

Many age rules depend on your age on a fixed date rather than today. Kindergarten is a common example: most US states require a child to be 5 years old by a cutoff date, often around September 1, though the exact date varies by state. Sports leagues, scholarships and insurance policies often use similar cutoffs. Enter the cutoff date in **Age on this date** to see whether someone qualifies.

## Key terms

- **Chronological age:** time elapsed since birth, as calculated here.
- **Leap year:** a year with 366 days, adding February 29.
- **Gregorian calendar:** the calendar used in most of the world, with its leap year rules.
- **Monthly anniversary:** the same day number in a later month.
