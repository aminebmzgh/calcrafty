## How to use this car CO2 calculator

1. **Miles per year.** Check your odometer, or a service record from a year ago, for the miles you drive in a typical year. The U.S. average is roughly 11,500 miles.
2. **Fuel economy.** Enter your car's combined miles per gallon. You'll find the official rating on fueleconomy.gov or the window sticker; your real-world figure, from fill-up records, is even better.
3. **Fuel.** Gasoline or diesel.
4. **Fuel price (More options).** Optional, for the yearly fuel cost.

The result shows your car's yearly CO2 emissions in metric tons and pounds, the grams per mile and per month, how it compares with the EPA's typical passenger vehicle, and what 10 mpg better fuel economy would save.

## The formula

**Gallons per year = miles ÷ mpg**

**CO2 per year = gallons × emission factor**

The emission factors come from the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency:

- **Gasoline:** 8,887 grams of CO2 per gallon.
- **Diesel:** 10,180 grams of CO2 per gallon.

Dividing by 1,000,000 converts grams to metric tons (1 metric ton = 1,000 kg, about 2,205 lb).

## Worked example: a 25 mpg car

You drive 11,500 miles a year in a gasoline car that averages 25 mpg.

- **Fuel:** 11,500 ÷ 25 = **460 gallons**.
- **CO2:** 460 × 8,887 g = 4,088,020 g = **4.09 metric tons**, about 9,013 lb.
- **Per mile:** 8,887 ÷ 25 = **355 grams**.
- **Compared with the typical vehicle:** 4.09 ÷ 4.6 = **89%**.

At 35 mpg, the same driving would emit 2.92 metric tons, about 1.17 metric tons less each year.

## How fuel economy changes emissions

For 11,500 miles of gasoline driving:

| Fuel economy | Gallons a year | CO2 a year | CO2 per mile |
|---|---|---|---|
| 15 mpg | 766.7 | 6.81 t | 592 g |
| 20 mpg | 575.0 | 5.11 t | 444 g |
| 22.2 mpg (EPA typical) | 518.0 | 4.60 t | 400 g |
| 25 mpg | 460.0 | 4.09 t | 355 g |
| 30 mpg | 383.3 | 3.41 t | 296 g |
| 40 mpg | 287.5 | 2.56 t | 222 g |
| 50 mpg | 230.0 | 2.04 t | 178 g |

The savings from better fuel economy shrink as mpg rises: going from 15 to 20 mpg saves 1.70 tons a year, while going from 40 to 50 mpg saves 0.52 tons. That's because emissions depend on gallons per mile, not miles per gallon. Replacing a very inefficient vehicle often cuts more CO2 than upgrading an already efficient one.

## How distance changes emissions

For a 25 mpg gasoline car:

| Miles a year | CO2 a year |
|---|---|
| 5,000 | 1.78 t |
| 10,000 | 3.55 t |
| 15,000 | 5.33 t |
| 20,000 | 7.11 t |

Emissions rise in direct proportion to distance. Every 1,000 miles in this car adds about 355 kg of CO2.

## Comparing two cars

If you're choosing between vehicles, run the calculator for each with the same yearly miles. For 12,000 miles a year:

- **A 20 mpg SUV** uses 600 gallons and emits **5.33 metric tons** of CO2, with about $1,920 of fuel at $3.20 a gallon.
- **A 35 mpg sedan** uses 342.9 gallons and emits **3.05 metric tons**, with about $1,097 of fuel.

The sedan cuts emissions by 2.28 tons a year and saves about 257 gallons, worth roughly $823 a year at that fuel price. Over a typical ownership period of several years, those differences add up, which is worth weighing alongside the purchase price.

## Your commute's share

Commuting is often the largest single part of a household's driving. A 30-mile round trip, five days a week for 50 weeks, is 7,500 miles a year. In a 25 mpg car, that's 300 gallons and **2.67 metric tons** of CO2, about $960 of fuel. Working from home one day a week would cut that by a fifth; carpooling with one other person roughly halves each person's share.

## Finding your real fuel economy

The official mpg rating is measured in standardized tests, and real-world driving often differs, especially in cold weather, in heavy traffic or with a lot of short trips. To find your actual figure:

1. **Fill the tank completely** and note the odometer reading.
2. **Drive normally** until the tank is low.
3. **Fill up again** and note the gallons added and the new odometer reading.
4. **Divide the miles driven by the gallons added.**

Average several tanks for a reliable figure, then enter it here. Many cars also show average mpg on the dashboard, which is usually close but not exact.

## City and highway driving

Most gasoline cars use more fuel in stop-and-go city traffic than on the highway, because they waste energy accelerating from stops and idling. Hybrids reverse that pattern to some extent, since they recover energy when braking and can run on electric power at low speeds. If you mainly drive in town, use the city rating; if your miles are mostly highway, use the highway rating; for a mix, the combined rating is a good estimate.

## Why a gallon of gas makes more than its weight in CO2

A gallon of gasoline weighs a little over 6 lb, yet burning it produces about 19.6 lb of CO2. The extra mass comes from the air. Gasoline is mostly carbon and hydrogen; when it burns, each carbon atom combines with two oxygen atoms from the air to form carbon dioxide. Carbon has an atomic mass of about 12 and CO2 about 44, so every pound of carbon in the fuel becomes about 3.7 lb of CO2.

## Gasoline, diesel, hybrids and electric cars

- **Diesel** contains more carbon per gallon, so it emits more CO2 per gallon, but diesel engines often travel farther on each gallon. Compare cars by grams per mile, not per gallon.
- **Hybrids** use the same fuel, so the calculator works for them directly; their higher mpg is what lowers emissions.
- **Plug-in hybrids** split their driving between electricity and gasoline. Enter only the gasoline-powered miles to estimate their tailpipe emissions.
- **Fully electric cars** have no tailpipe emissions. Their footprint comes from generating the electricity they use, which varies a lot depending on how your local grid produces power, and from manufacturing the battery. This calculator covers tailpipe CO2 only.

## Estimating an electric car the same way

You can compare an electric car using the same logic, with electricity in place of gasoline:

**CO2 per year = miles × kWh per mile × grams of CO2 per kWh of electricity**

The car's efficiency, in kWh per mile or miles per kWh, is on its fueleconomy.gov listing. The grams of CO2 per kWh depend on where your electricity comes from: grids that rely heavily on coal emit far more per kWh than those powered mostly by hydro, nuclear, wind or solar. Your utility, or the EPA's data on regional power grids, can give you a figure for your area. Charging from home solar panels lowers it further.

## Seasonal changes

Fuel economy usually drops in winter. Cold engines run less efficiently until they warm up, winter fuel blends contain slightly less energy, and heating, defrosting and lower tire pressure all add load. Very hot weather with heavy air-conditioning use has a smaller effect. If you calculate from a single summer tank, your yearly emissions will be a little higher than the result suggests.

## Ways to cut your driving emissions

- **Drive less:** combine errands, carpool, or use transit, walking or cycling for short trips.
- **Drive smoothly:** rapid acceleration and hard braking waste fuel, and fuel economy usually falls at high highway speeds.
- **Limit idling:** an idling engine burns fuel while going nowhere.
- **Maintain the car:** keep tires inflated to the recommended pressure, and follow the maintenance schedule.
- **Lighten the load:** remove roof racks and heavy items you don't need, which add drag and weight.
- **Choose an efficient vehicle** when it's time to replace your car; check its rating on fueleconomy.gov.

## Putting the number in context

The EPA's typical passenger vehicle emits about 4.6 metric tons of CO2 a year. Driving is one of the largest parts of many households' carbon footprints, alongside home heating and cooling, electricity, food and flights. Tracking the number over time, as you change how much and what you drive, is the most practical way to see progress.

## Key terms

- **CO2 (carbon dioxide):** the main greenhouse gas from burning fossil fuels.
- **Emission factor:** the amount of CO2 released per unit of fuel burned.
- **Metric ton:** 1,000 kilograms, about 2,205 pounds.
- **mpg:** miles per gallon, a measure of fuel economy.
- **Tailpipe emissions:** gases released directly from a vehicle's exhaust.
- **Combined mpg:** a weighted average of city and highway fuel economy, used for the official rating.
- **Greenhouse gas:** a gas that traps heat in the atmosphere, such as carbon dioxide, methane and nitrous oxide.
- **Carbon footprint:** the total greenhouse gas emissions caused by a person, household, activity or product, usually given in metric tons of CO2 equivalent.
