## How to use this savings goal calculator

Enter a goal, what you've saved and either a deadline or a monthly amount. The calculator works in both directions: it tells you the monthly deposit that hits a deadline, or the time it takes at the amount you can afford.

1. **Savings goal and saved so far.** For example a house deposit, an emergency fund or a car.
2. **I want to know.** Choose **How much to save** if you have a deadline, or **How long it takes** if you have a budget.
3. **Deadline or monthly amount.** Years until you need the money, or what you can put away each month.
4. **Interest rate.** The default is the current 3-month Treasury bill yield, a benchmark for what high-yield savings accounts and money market funds pay. Use your account's rate for an exact plan.

## Worked example: a $60,000 house deposit in four years

A couple wants $60,000, 20% down on a $300,000 home, in four years. They have $8,000 saved and a high-yield savings account paying 4%.

- **Save each month:** **$973.94**, about $224.76 a week
- **You deposit:** $46,749.12 over 48 months
- **Interest earned:** $5,250.97
- **Balance at the deadline:** $60,000.09

Without interest the same goal would need $1,083.34 a month, so the 4% rate saves them about $109 a month. If $973.94 is more than they can manage, switching to **How long it takes** at $800 a month shows they'd reach $60,000 in 4 years and 10 months, earning $6,409.57 in interest along the way.

## The savings formula in plain English

Your goal is reached by two things growing together: the money you already have, and the deposits you add each month. The calculator solves for the deposit that makes them add up to exactly the goal.

**PMT = (goal − saved × (1 + m)ⁿ) × m ÷ ((1 + m)ⁿ − 1)**

- **m** is the monthly interest rate. Savings accounts usually compound daily, so m = (1 + rate ÷ 365)^(365 ÷ 12) − 1.
- **n** is the number of months until the deadline.
- If the rate is 0%, it's simply (goal − saved) ÷ n.

The monthly amount is rounded up to the cent, so following the plan always reaches the goal. In "How long it takes" mode, the calculator adds a month of interest and your deposit until the balance reaches the goal.

## Key terms

- **Sinking fund:** money set aside regularly for a known future expense.
- **Emergency fund:** savings for unexpected costs such as a job loss or a car repair.
- **High-yield savings account:** an insured savings account paying well above the average rate, usually online.
- **APY:** the yearly yield including compounding; the rate to compare between accounts.

## Building an emergency fund step by step

An emergency fund is the savings goal most people should hit first. It covers job loss, medical bills or a broken-down car without new debt, and it's what makes every other goal safer.

**Work out the target.** Add up essential monthly costs: rent or mortgage, utilities, groceries, insurance, transportation and minimum debt payments. Many guides suggest three to six months of those costs. For someone with $3,500 of monthly essentials, that's $10,500 to $21,000.

**See how long it takes.** Switch the calculator to **How long it takes**, enter the target and what you can save. At $400 a month in a 4% account, $10,500 takes 2 years and 2 months, and $21,000 takes 4 years and 1 month, with $1,655.93 of interest helping along the way.

**Start smaller if you need to.** A first milestone of $500 or $1,000 covers many common surprises. Reaching it quickly builds the habit, and you can raise the target once you get there.

**Keep it separate and easy to reach.** A high-yield savings account at a different bank from your checking account keeps the money out of sight but available within a day or two. Make sure the account is FDIC or NCUA insured.

**Automate and add windfalls.** A recurring transfer on payday does most of the work; tax refunds, bonuses and side income can top it up.

**Refill it after you use it.** That's what it's for. Once you've drawn on it, pause other goals briefly and rebuild it before moving on.

**Use sinking funds for known costs.** Car insurance, holidays, annual subscriptions and car registration aren't emergencies, but they can feel like one. Dividing each yearly cost by 12 and saving that amount monthly in a separate pot keeps your emergency fund for real emergencies.
