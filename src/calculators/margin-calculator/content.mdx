faq:
  - q: "How do I calculate profit margin?"
    a: "Subtract the cost from the selling price to get the profit, then divide the profit by the selling price and multiply by 100. An item that costs $45 and sells for $75 makes $30 of profit, and $30 ÷ $75 = 40% gross margin."
  - q: "What is the difference between margin and markup?"
    a: "Both start from the same profit, but margin divides it by the selling price and markup divides it by the cost. With a $45 cost and a $75 price, the $30 profit is a 40% margin and a 66.67% markup. Margin is always the smaller number for a profitable sale."
  - q: "How do I find the selling price for a target margin?"
    a: "Divide the cost by one minus the margin as a decimal. For a 60% margin on a $6.80 cost, the price is $6.80 ÷ (1 − 0.60) = $6.80 ÷ 0.40 = $17.00. Multiplying the cost by 1.60 instead would give only a 37.5% margin."
  - q: "What markup gives a 50% margin?"
    a: "A 100% markup, which means doubling the cost. Retailers call this keystone pricing. A 25% margin needs a 33.33% markup, a 40% margin needs 66.67%, and a 60% margin needs 150%."
  - q: "What is a good profit margin?"
    a: "It depends on the industry. Grocery stores often run gross margins in the 25% to 30% range, apparel and specialty retail often 40% to 60%, and software far higher. Compare yourself with businesses like yours, and remember that gross margin has to cover rent, wages and every other overhead cost."
  - q: "Can a margin be more than 100%?"
    a: "No. Margin is profit as a share of the price, and profit can't be larger than the price unless the cost is negative. Markup has no upper limit: an item that costs $2 and sells for $10 has a 400% markup but an 80% margin."
  - q: "Is gross margin the same as net margin?"
    a: "No. Gross margin subtracts only the direct cost of the goods sold. Net margin subtracts everything: rent, payroll, marketing, card fees, shipping supplies, interest and taxes. A shop with a 60% gross margin can easily have a net margin under 15%."

## How to use this margin calculator

Pick the question you're asking, enter two numbers and the calculator fills in the rest. Every mode shows the gross margin, the markup and the profit per unit together, so you can see both percentages side by side.

1. **Cost and price.** You know what an item costs you and what you sell it for, and you want the margin and markup.
2. **Cost and target margin.** You know your cost and want a certain margin, so the calculator finds the price.
3. **Cost and markup.** You add a fixed percentage on top of cost, as many wholesalers and contractors do, and want the price and the margin that results.
4. **Price and target margin.** The market sets the price, and you want the most you can pay for the item and still hit your margin. Buyers use this when negotiating with suppliers.

Under **More options**, enter the number of units sold to see total revenue and total gross profit. The table below the result shows the price, profit and equivalent markup for margins from 10% to 70% at your cost, which is a quick way to see where a price point lands.

## The formulas

Everything starts with gross profit per unit:

**Profit = selling price − cost**

**Gross margin = profit ÷ selling price × 100**

**Markup = profit ÷ cost × 100**

To find a price from a margin, rearrange the margin formula:

**Price = cost ÷ (1 − margin)**

To find a price from a markup:

**Price = cost × (1 + markup)**

And the two percentages convert directly, with no cost or price needed:

**Markup = margin ÷ (1 − margin)** and **margin = markup ÷ (1 + markup)**, both as decimals.

Prices are rounded to the cent. Because of that rounding, the margin on the final price can differ from your target in the fourth decimal place: a 35% margin on a $60 cost gives $92.31, which is actually a 35.0016% margin.

## Worked example: pricing a handmade candle

A candle maker adds up the cost of one candle: wax, wick, fragrance oil, a glass jar and a label come to **$6.80**. They want a **60% gross margin** to leave room for fees, packaging and their time.

- **Price:** $6.80 ÷ (1 − 0.60) = $6.80 ÷ 0.40 = **$17.00**.
- **Profit per candle:** $17.00 − $6.80 = **$10.20**.
- **Check the margin:** $10.20 ÷ $17.00 = 60%.
- **Markup:** $10.20 ÷ $6.80 = **150%**.

Selling 400 candles a month brings in $6,800 of revenue and **$4,080** of gross profit.

The common mistake is to multiply the cost by 1.6, thinking that adds "60%". That's a 60% markup, giving a price of $10.88 and a margin of only 37.5%. On 400 candles, it leaves $1,632 of gross profit instead of $4,080.

## From gross margin to net margin

Gross profit isn't what the business keeps. Out of the candle maker's $4,080 a month come the expenses that don't belong to any single candle:

| Monthly expense | Amount |
|---|---|
| Market booth fees | $300 |
| Shipping boxes and packing material | $450 |
| Card processing, about 3% of $6,800 | $204 |
| **Total overhead** | **$954** |

That leaves $3,126 before the owner's pay and income tax, a **net margin of about 46%** on $6,800 of sales. The more overhead a business carries, the higher the gross margin it needs. That's why retailers with expensive storefronts aim for much higher gross margins than an online seller working from home.

## Margin and markup at a glance

The same profit can be described two ways. Use this table to translate a markup a supplier quotes into the margin you'll actually earn, or the other way round.

| Gross margin | Markup needed | Price on a $10 cost |
|---|---|---|
| 10% | 11.11% | $11.11 |
| 20% | 25% | $12.50 |
| 25% | 33.33% | $13.33 |
| 30% | 42.86% | $14.29 |
| 40% | 66.67% | $16.67 |
| 50% | 100% | $20.00 |
| 60% | 150% | $25.00 |
| 70% | 233.33% | $33.33 |
| 75% | 300% | $40.00 |
| 80% | 400% | $50.00 |

As margins get higher, the markup needed grows very quickly. Going from a 70% margin to an 80% margin means going from a 233% markup to a 400% markup. Our guide [margin vs markup](/guides/margin-vs-markup/) explains the difference in more depth, with examples from retail, restaurants and contracting.

## How discounts eat into margin

A discount comes straight out of profit, so it cuts the margin much harder than the headline percentage suggests. Take an item that costs $45 and normally sells for $75, a 40% margin with $30 of profit.

- At **20% off**, the price falls to $60. Profit drops to $15, half of what it was, and the margin falls to **25%**.
- To earn the same $30 of gross profit at the sale price, you'd need to sell **twice as many** units.

Before running a promotion, enter the discounted price in this calculator to see the margin you'd be left with. The [discount calculator](/math/discount-calculator/) works out the sale price, including stacked coupons.

## Working back from the market price

Often you don't get to choose the price: customers already know what a product sells for. In that case, the useful question is how much you can afford to pay for it. That's the "price and target margin" mode.

A boutique owner sees that scarves like the ones they want to stock sell for about **$48**. They need a **55% gross margin** to cover their rent and staff.

- **Maximum cost:** $48 × (1 − 0.55) = $48 × 0.45 = **$21.60**.
- A supplier quotes **$24.00**. At that cost, the margin would be ($48 − $24) ÷ $48 = **50%**, five points short.

The owner now has a clear number to negotiate with: ask for $21.60, look for a cheaper supplier, or accept a lower margin on this line and make it up elsewhere. Buyers at larger retailers work the same way, starting from the shelf price and the margin the category has to earn.

## Margins for service businesses and contractors

Contractors, caterers and other service businesses often quote with a markup on their costs instead of a margin, because it's easy to apply to a list of materials and labor. The catch is that the margin they earn is always lower than the markup they add.

A remodeling contractor estimates a job at **$3,200** in materials and direct labor and adds a **25% markup**:

- **Price:** $3,200 × 1.25 = **$4,000**.
- **Gross profit:** $800.
- **Margin:** $800 ÷ $4,000 = **20%**.

If the business needs a 25% margin to cover its office, vehicles, insurance and estimating time, it has to add a 33.33% markup instead, quoting $4,266.67. Run your own numbers through the "cost and markup" mode to see what your usual markup really earns.

## Key terms

- **Cost (cost of goods sold, COGS):** the direct cost of the item: what you paid a supplier, or the materials and direct labor to make it. The IRS uses cost of goods sold to work out a business's gross profit.
- **Selling price:** what the customer pays, before sales tax.
- **Gross profit:** selling price minus cost, per unit or in total.
- **Gross margin (profit margin):** gross profit as a percentage of the selling price.
- **Markup:** gross profit as a percentage of the cost.
- **Keystone pricing:** doubling the wholesale cost, a 100% markup and a 50% margin.
- **Net margin:** what's left after all expenses, as a percentage of revenue.

## What to do with your result

- **Compare with your target.** If your margin is lower than you need, either the price is too low or the cost is too high. Run the "price and target margin" mode to see the most you can pay a supplier.
- **Check every channel.** Selling through a marketplace, a wholesaler or a reseller usually means giving up part of the price. Enter the price you actually receive, not the list price.
- **Include all direct costs.** Shipping you pay to receive stock, packaging that goes out with every order and payment fees per sale all belong in the cost if you want a true per-unit margin.
- **Think in dollars too.** A 70% margin on a $4 item is $2.80 of profit; a 25% margin on a $400 item is $100. Both percentages and dollar profit matter.
- **Revisit prices when costs change.** If a supplier raises your cost from $45 to $50 and you keep the $75 price, your margin falls from 40% to 33.3%.

## Common mistakes

1. **Using markup and margin as if they were the same.** A 30% markup is only a 23.08% margin. Confusing them is the single most common pricing error in small businesses.
2. **Adding a margin to the cost.** You can't multiply by (1 + margin); you have to divide by (1 − margin).
3. **Leaving out costs.** A margin calculated on the product cost alone, without inbound shipping or packaging, looks better than it is.
4. **Ignoring returns and spoilage.** If one in twenty items is returned or thrown away, your effective cost per item sold is higher than the purchase price.
5. **Judging only by percentage.** A higher margin with much lower sales can earn less total profit. Use the units field to compare total gross profit.
