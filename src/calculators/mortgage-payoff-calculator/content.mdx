## How to use this mortgage payoff calculator

This calculator starts from where your loan is today, not from the day you took it out. That makes it useful years into a mortgage, after a refinance or when a loan has had extra payments already.

1. **Current loan balance.** The unpaid principal from your latest statement.
2. **Interest rate.** Your loan's note rate. The APR includes fees and is slightly higher; don't use it here.
3. **Monthly principal and interest.** Your payment without escrow. If your statement shows $2,300 including $500 of taxes and insurance, enter $1,800.
4. **Extra each month.** What you could add to principal every month.
5. **More options.** A one-time lump sum paid now, and a goal such as "paid off in 15 years" to see the monthly extra you'd need.

The headline shows how long until you're mortgage-free and how much sooner that is. Below it are the interest saved and the interest you'd still pay. The table compares extra payments from $100 to $1,000 a month, and the schedule shows every remaining payment.

## How the payoff is calculated

Each month the calculator charges interest on the balance and applies your payment:

1. **Interest** = balance × annual rate ÷ 12, rounded to the cent.
2. **Principal** = payment + extra − interest.
3. **New balance** = balance − principal.

It repeats until the balance reaches zero. The same steps run twice: once with your current payment and once with the extra payments, and the difference is the interest and time saved. If the payment doesn't cover a month's interest, the balance would never fall, and the calculator tells you so.

## Worked example: $250,000 left at 6.5%

A homeowner owes $250,000 at 6.5% and pays $1,800 a month in principal and interest. This month, $1,354.17 of that is interest (250,000 × 0.065 ÷ 12), and only $445.83 reduces the balance.

At the current payment, the loan is paid off in **21 years 7 months**, with **$215,023.43** of interest still to pay. Adding **$200 a month**:

- Paid off in **17 years 6 months**, 4 years 1 month sooner.
- Interest falls to $168,494.33, saving **$46,529.10**.

Here's what different extra payments do for the same loan:

| Extra a month | Paid off in | Interest | Saved |
|---|---|---|---|
| None | 21 years 7 months | $215,023.43 | — |
| $100 | 19 years 3 months | $188,697.34 | $26,326.09 |
| $250 | 16 years 9 months | $160,028.64 | $54,994.79 |
| $500 | 13 years 9 months | $128,334.96 | $86,688.47 |
| $1,000 | 10 years 3 months | $92,578.13 | $122,445.30 |

The savings grow faster than the extra payment. $100 a month saves about $26,000, while $500 saves almost $87,000, because each dollar of principal removed early stops being charged interest for many years.

## Your rate decides how much an extra payment is worth

The same $200 a month is worth far more on an expensive loan. For a $250,000 balance with 25 years left, paying the scheduled amount plus $200:

| Rate | Scheduled payment | Paid off in | Interest saved |
|---|---|---|---|
| 3.5% | $1,251.56 | 20 years | $27,669.07 |
| 5% | $1,461.48 | 19 years 10 months | $44,431.41 |
| 6.5% | $1,688.02 | 19 years 6 months | $64,927.56 |
| 8% | $1,929.54 | 19 years 2 months | $89,719.55 |

At 8%, the extra $200 saves more than three times as much as at 3.5%. If you have a low-rate mortgage from a few years ago, the case for paying it down early is weaker; if your rate is high, it's one of the best low-risk uses of spare money.

## The earlier you start, the more you save

Where you are in the loan matters as much as the amount. On a $300,000 30-year mortgage at 6.5% ($1,896.20 a month), here's what $200 extra a month does depending on when you start:

| Start paying $200 extra | Balance then | Time saved | Interest saved |
|---|---|---|---|
| From the first payment | $300,000.00 | 6 years 11 months | $103,450.19 |
| After 5 years | $280,833.26 | 5 years | $66,945.03 |
| After 10 years | $254,329.14 | 3 years 5 months | $39,658.94 |
| After 20 years | $166,997.98 | 1 year 3 months | $8,381.16 |

Late in a loan most of each payment already goes to principal, so extra payments have less interest left to save. Starting early, even with a smaller amount, usually beats waiting to pay more later.

## Paying off by a target date

Under More options, enter a goal and the calculator finds the extra monthly payment that gets you there. For the same $250,000 balance:

| Goal | Extra needed a month | Total monthly payment |
|---|---|---|
| Paid off in 15 years | $377.77 | $2,177.77 |
| Paid off in 10 years | $1,038.70 | $2,838.70 |

A common goal is to be mortgage-free by retirement. Count the years until you plan to stop working and enter that as the goal.

## Lump sums

A one-time payment comes off the balance before the next month's interest is charged. Paying **$20,000** now on the example loan, with no monthly extra, finishes it in 18 years 3 months instead of 21 years 7 months and saves **$52,479.81**. A lump sum often comes from a bonus, an inheritance or the sale of an asset.

Before sending a large amount, ask your servicer how to make sure it's applied to principal. Most servicers have a box on the payment form or an option in their app. Some will **recast** the loan afterward, recalculating a lower monthly payment on the smaller balance while keeping the same payoff date, usually for a fee of a few hundred dollars.

## Biweekly payments

Paying half your mortgage every two weeks means 26 half-payments, or 13 full payments, a year instead of 12. That extra payment goes to principal. You get almost exactly the same result by adding one-twelfth of your payment each month: for an $1,800 payment, that's $150 extra.

On the example loan, $150 a month pays it off in 18 years 4 months instead of 21 years 7 months and saves **$37,042.46**. Some companies sell biweekly plans for a setup fee; you don't need one. Add the extra yourself each month, or check whether your servicer offers biweekly payments for free.

## Should you pay off your mortgage early?

Every extra dollar earns a guaranteed return equal to your mortgage rate, with no risk. That's attractive when rates are 6% or more, and it brings the security of owning your home outright. But money in your house is hard to get back if you need it. Consider these first:

- **Emergency savings.** Three to six months of expenses in cash. Use the [emergency fund calculator](/finance/emergency-fund-calculator/) to set a target.
- **Higher-interest debt.** Credit cards and personal loans usually cost far more than a mortgage. The [debt payoff calculator](/finance/debt-payoff-calculator/) compares the snowball and avalanche methods.
- **Retirement matching.** An employer's 401(k) match beats any mortgage rate.
- **Tax effects.** If you itemize deductions, mortgage interest may be deductible, which lowers the real cost of the loan. Most households now take the standard deduction, so this matters less than it used to.

If your rate is low, for example 3%, a high-yield savings account or Treasury bills may pay more than the mortgage costs, and keeping the cash can come out ahead. If your rate is higher than what safe savings earn, paying extra usually wins.

## Other ways to pay off sooner

- **Refinance to a shorter term.** A 15-year loan usually has a lower rate than a 30-year loan, so you pay less interest on two fronts. The payment is higher and fixed, though; see the [refinance calculator](/finance/refinance-calculator/).
- **Round up your payment.** Paying $1,900 instead of $1,800 is an extra $100 a month that's easy to forget about.
- **Put raises toward the loan.** When your income rises, adding part of the raise to your payment keeps your budget steady while the loan shrinks.
- **Use windfalls.** Tax refunds and bonuses are natural lump sums.

## Common mistakes

- **Using the payment including escrow.** Taxes and insurance don't reduce the balance, so including them makes the payoff look faster than it is.
- **Using the original loan amount.** If you've had the mortgage for years, your balance is lower than what you borrowed. Use today's balance.
- **Draining savings for a lump sum.** You can't easily borrow back what you've put into your home. Keep an emergency fund.
- **Not telling the servicer it's for principal.** An extra payment can be held as a prepaid future payment instead of reducing the balance.
- **Forgetting an adjustable rate.** On an ARM, the rate and payment change at each reset, so the payoff date moves too. Rerun the calculator after every adjustment with the new rate and payment.

## Key terms

- **Unpaid principal balance:** what you still owe, not counting interest that hasn't been charged yet.
- **Payoff amount:** the exact amount to close the loan on a given date, including interest up to that day.
- **Principal and interest (P&I):** the part of the payment that repays the loan.
- **Escrow:** money collected with the payment for property tax and homeowners insurance.
- **Recast:** recalculating the payment after a large principal payment, keeping the same end date.
- **Prepayment penalty:** a fee for paying off a loan early, now rare on mortgages.
