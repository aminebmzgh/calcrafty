## How to use this debt payoff calculator

List your debts, add the extra amount you can pay each month, and the calculator runs both payoff methods side by side. It shows when you'll be debt-free, how much interest each method costs and the order your debts get paid off.

1. **Your debts.** For each one, enter a name, the current balance, the APR and the minimum monthly payment from your statement. Add up to 10 debts.
2. **Extra payment.** The amount you can pay each month beyond all the minimums. Even $50 makes a difference.
3. **Read both results.** The avalanche column targets the highest APR first; the snowball column targets the smallest balance first. The one with lower interest is marked.

The calculation assumes no new charges, fixed rates and that you keep paying the same total each month.

## Worked example: $14,300 across three debts

Someone owes $900 on a store card at 26.99% (minimum $35), $7,400 on a Visa at 24.49% (minimum $185) and $6,000 on a personal loan at 11.5% (minimum $198). They can add $300 a month, for a total of $718.

**Avalanche (highest rate first):**
- Store card cleared in month 3, Visa in month 20, personal loan in month 24
- **Debt-free in 2 years, total interest $2,792.76**

**Snowball (smallest balance first):**
- Store card cleared in month 3, personal loan in month 14, Visa in month 25
- **Debt-free in 2 years 1 month, total interest $3,414.02**

The avalanche saves **$621.26** because it attacks the 24.49% Visa before the 11.5% loan. The snowball, though, wipes out a second debt six months sooner (month 14 instead of 20), which can help motivation. Paying only the minimums would take 7 years and cost $9,699.03 in interest.

When the smallest debt also has the highest rate, the two methods give identical results. For example, with a $4,200 card at 22.99%, a $9,800 car loan at 7.5% and a $14,500 student loan at 5.5%, both finish in 3 years 3 months with $3,415.50 of interest.

## How the payoff is calculated in plain English

Each month, three things happen in order:

1. **Interest** is added to every balance: balance × APR ÷ 12, rounded to the cent.
2. **Minimums** are paid on every debt that still has a balance.
3. **Everything left** of your monthly budget (the extra, plus minimums freed up by debts already paid off) goes to the target debt. If it's more than the target owes, the rest moves to the next debt in line.

The target order is the only difference between the methods: highest APR first for avalanche, smallest balance first for snowball. The calculator repeats this until every balance reaches zero, or warns you if your payments don't cover the interest.

## How much the extra payment matters

The extra amount you add each month matters far more than which method you pick. Here are the same three debts ($14,300 in total) with different amounts added to the $418 of combined minimums:

| Extra per month | Avalanche: time | Avalanche: interest | Snowball: time | Snowball: interest |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| $0 (minimums rolled over) | 53 months | $7,507.99 | 53 months | $7,507.99 |
| $100 | 37 months | $4,773.15 | 38 months | $5,258.58 |
| $200 | 29 months | $3,496.73 | 30 months | $4,131.69 |
| $300 | 24 months | $2,792.76 | 25 months | $3,414.02 |
| $500 | 18 months | $2,020.30 | 19 months | $2,546.01 |

Two things stand out. First, even with no extra money at all, simply rolling each cleared debt's minimum into the next one finishes in 53 months instead of 7 years and saves about $2,200 compared with paying minimums forever. Second, going from $0 to $100 extra saves more than $2,700, while the choice between avalanche and snowball is worth a few hundred dollars. Find the extra money first, then choose the method.

## Should you consolidate instead?

A debt consolidation loan replaces several debts with one fixed payment. It saves money only if its rate, including fees, is meaningfully lower than the rates you're paying now, and only if you keep paying as much as before.

Suppose a lender offers $14,300 at 11% for 36 months. The required payment is $468.16, which is tempting because it's lower than the $718 you're paying now. But at that payment the loan costs $2,553.91 in interest over three years, about the same as the avalanche plan, which finishes a year sooner. If you keep paying $718 a month on the loan, it's gone in 23 months with $1,562.26 of interest, about $1,230 less than the avalanche.

Fees change the picture. With a 5% origination fee deducted from the loan, you'd need to borrow $15,053 to receive $14,300. At $718 a month, that takes 24 months and costs $1,740.59 in interest plus the $753 fee, about $300 less than the avalanche, a much thinner advantage. Run any offer through the [APR calculator](/finance/apr-calculator/) first, and close or freeze the paid-off cards so the balances don't return.

## How paying off debt affects your credit score

Paying down debt usually helps your credit score, and quickly. Under the widely used FICO model, payment history and amounts owed are the two biggest factors, together making up about two-thirds of the score.

- **Credit utilization** (card balances divided by credit limits) is a large part of "amounts owed". Bringing it below about 30%, and ideally under 10%, often lifts a score within a billing cycle or two of the lower balances being reported.
- **On-time payments** matter most of all. Autopaying every minimum while you work the plan protects your history.
- **Closing paid-off cards** can backfire: it removes their limits, which raises utilization on the cards you still use. Unless a card has an annual fee, it's often better to keep it open and unused.
- **Paying off an installment loan** such as a car loan can cause a small, temporary dip, because you have one fewer active account. It's not a reason to keep paying interest.

## When to pay a debt out of order

Interest rate and balance aren't the only things that matter. Some debts deserve priority regardless of method:

- **Debts that can cost you essentials.** Rent or mortgage, car loans you need for work, and utilities come before unsecured debt.
- **Tax debts and court-ordered payments,** which can lead to liens, wage garnishment or legal penalties.
- **Promotional 0% balances about to expire,** especially "deferred interest" store cards that charge all the back interest if any balance remains when the promotion ends.
- **Federal student loans** usually come last: they often have lower rates and offer income-driven repayment and other protections that private debts don't.

## Key terms

- **Debt avalanche:** pay extra toward the highest-interest debt first.
- **Debt snowball:** pay extra toward the smallest balance first.
- **Rollover:** adding a cleared debt's minimum payment to the next target.
- **Minimum payment:** the least you must pay each month to stay current.
- **Debt-to-income ratio:** monthly debt payments ÷ gross monthly income; lenders use it to judge new credit.

## Making a payoff plan stick

The math decides how much interest you pay, but behavior decides whether you finish. A plan that's slightly more expensive but that you actually complete beats a perfect plan you abandon after three months.

**List everything first.** Pull balances, rates and minimums from your statements or your credit report, which you can get free from each of the three nationwide credit bureaus at AnnualCreditReport.com. Include medical bills, buy-now-pay-later plans and money owed to family if you intend to repay it.

**Set the extra amount realistically.** Look at your last two or three months of spending and pick an amount you can keep paying even in a month with surprise costs. It's better to start at $150 and raise it than to start at $400 and stop.

**Protect the plan with a small cushion.** Without any emergency savings, the next car repair goes back on a card. Many people keep a starter fund, often around $1,000, before throwing everything at debt.

**Automate the payments.** Set every minimum to autopay so you never miss one, then schedule the extra payment to the target debt right after payday.

**Use windfalls.** Tax refunds, bonuses and cash gifts can go straight to the target debt. Each lump sum pulls your debt-free date closer and cuts interest on every debt that's left.

**Track progress.** Watching the total fall is motivating. Re-run this calculator every few months with your new balances; you'll see the debt-free date move closer as balances fall faster than the original plan assumed, especially once the first debt is gone and its payment rolls over.

**Stop adding new debt.** Freezing cards (literally or by removing them from online stores) keeps the plan from leaking.
