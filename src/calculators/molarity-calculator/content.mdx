## How to use this molarity calculator

1. **Choose what to find.**
   - **Molarity:** you know how much solute you dissolved and the final volume.
   - **Mass to weigh:** you know the concentration and volume you want, and need to know how much to weigh out.
   - **Volume:** you have a set amount of solute and want a given concentration.
2. **Pick the compound.** Common compounds are listed with their molar masses. For anything else, choose **Other** and enter its molar mass in grams per mole.
3. **Enter the known values.** Mass in grams, volume in milliliters and molarity in moles per liter (M).

The result shows the answer, the number of moles and millimoles, and all three quantities together. **Show the math** lists each step.

## The formulas

**Molarity (M) = moles of solute ÷ liters of solution**

**Moles = mass (g) ÷ molar mass (g/mol)**

Combining them:

**M = mass ÷ (molar mass × liters)**

**Mass = M × liters × molar mass**

**Liters = mass ÷ (molar mass × M)**

Molarity is also called molar concentration or amount concentration, and its unit, mol/L, is written M.

## Worked example: 0.2 M salt solution

You dissolve 5.844 g of sodium chloride (NaCl, 58.44 g/mol) and make it up to 500 mL.

- **Moles:** 5.844 ÷ 58.44 = **0.1 mol**.
- **Liters:** 500 mL ÷ 1,000 = **0.5 L**.
- **Molarity:** 0.1 ÷ 0.5 = **0.2 M**.

## Worked example: how much to weigh out

You need 250 mL of 0.5 M glucose (180.16 g/mol).

- **Moles needed:** 0.5 × 0.25 = **0.125 mol**.
- **Mass:** 0.125 × 180.16 = **22.52 g**.

Weigh 22.52 g of glucose, dissolve it in about 200 mL of water, then add water to the 250 mL mark.

| 9 g of NaCl made up to 1 L (normal saline) | 58.44 g/mol | 0.154 M |
| 2 g of NaOH at 0.5 M | 40.00 g/mol | make up to 100 mL |

Normal saline, 0.9% NaCl by weight per volume, is 9 g per liter, which works out to about 0.154 M.

## Molar masses of common compounds

These are calculated from standard atomic weights (H 1.008, C 12.011, N 14.007, O 15.999, Na 22.990, Cl 35.45, K 39.098, Ca 40.078) and rounded to 0.01 g/mol:

| Compound | Formula | Molar mass |
|---|---|---|
| Sodium chloride | NaCl | 58.44 g/mol |
| Glucose | C₆H₁₂O₆ | 180.16 g/mol |
| Sucrose | C₁₂H₂₂O₁₁ | 342.30 g/mol |
| Sodium hydroxide | NaOH | 40.00 g/mol |
| Hydrogen chloride | HCl | 36.46 g/mol |
| Potassium chloride | KCl | 74.55 g/mol |
| Calcium chloride | CaCl₂ | 110.98 g/mol |
| Ammonium chloride | NH₄Cl | 53.49 g/mol |

**Hydrates** contain water in their crystal structure, which adds to the molar mass. Calcium chloride dihydrate, CaCl₂·2H₂O, is about 147.01 g/mol rather than 110.98. Check the label on the bottle and use the molar mass of the form you actually have.

## Making a solution accurately

1. **Calculate the mass** with the calculator.
2. **Weigh the solute** on a balance, using a weighing boat or paper.
3. **Dissolve it** in a beaker with somewhat less water than the final volume.
4. **Transfer** the solution to a volumetric flask, rinsing the beaker into the flask so none is lost.
5. **Make up to the mark** with water, so the bottom of the meniscus sits on the line, then cap and invert to mix.

Adding the solute to the full volume of water gives a slightly wrong concentration, because the solute itself takes up space.

## Diluting a stock solution

To make a weaker solution from a concentrated one, use the dilution equation:

**M₁ × V₁ = M₂ × V₂**

where M₁ and V₁ are the concentration and volume of the stock, and M₂ and V₂ are those of the dilute solution. To make 500 mL of 0.1 M from a 1 M stock: V₁ = 0.1 × 500 ÷ 1 = **50 mL** of stock, made up to 500 mL with water. The number of moles stays the same; only the volume changes.

## Molarity of concentrated acids from a bottle label

Concentrated reagents are usually labeled with a percentage by mass and a density, not a molarity. To convert:

**M = density (g/mL) × 1,000 × mass fraction ÷ molar mass**

Concentrated hydrochloric acid is typically about 37% HCl by mass with a density of about 1.19 g/mL. One liter weighs 1,190 g, of which 37% (440.3 g) is HCl. Dividing by 36.46 g/mol gives about **12.08 M**. To make 100 mL of 1 M HCl from it, you'd need 1 × 100 ÷ 12.08 ≈ **8.3 mL** of the concentrated acid, added slowly to water and made up to 100 mL. Always check the actual percentage and density on your bottle's label or safety data sheet, as they vary by grade.

## Molarity in reactions and titrations

Molarity connects volumes of solution to moles, which is what chemical equations work in. Multiply a solution's molarity by the liters used to get the moles that took part in the reaction.

In a titration, 25.0 mL of 0.100 M NaOH contains 0.100 × 0.0250 = **0.00250 mol** of NaOH. Sodium hydroxide reacts with hydrochloric acid one to one, so if it exactly neutralizes 20.0 mL of an HCl solution, that solution held 0.00250 mol of HCl, and its concentration is 0.00250 ÷ 0.0200 = **0.125 M**. When a reaction isn't one to one, multiply by the ratio from the balanced equation.

## Concentration of ions

When an ionic compound dissolves, it splits into ions, and each ion has its own concentration. A 0.1 M solution of sodium chloride contains 0.1 M Na⁺ and 0.1 M Cl⁻. A 0.1 M solution of calcium chloride, CaCl₂, contains 0.1 M Ca²⁺ but **0.2 M** Cl⁻, because each formula unit releases two chloride ions. This matters when you're preparing buffers, comparing osmotic strength, or working out how much of a specific ion is present.

## Common mistakes

- **Using milliliters as liters.** 250 mL is 0.25 L; forgetting to divide by 1,000 gives an answer 1,000 times too small.
- **Using the wrong molar mass,** especially for hydrates or for a compound with a similar name.
- **Adding solute to the final volume of water** instead of making the solution up to the final volume.
- **Rounding too early.** Keep at least four significant figures until the final answer.
- **Confusing molarity with molality** in problems that involve temperature changes or boiling and freezing points.

## Molarity, molality and other units

| Unit | Definition | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Molarity (M) | moles per liter of solution | Most common in labs; changes slightly with temperature |
| Molality (m) | moles per kilogram of solvent | Doesn't depend on temperature |
| Percent w/v | grams per 100 mL of solution | Used for saline and many reagents |
| Parts per million | mg per liter (for dilute water solutions) | Used for trace amounts |
| Normality (N) | equivalents per liter | Depends on the reaction; mostly replaced by molarity |

To convert percent w/v to molarity, multiply the grams per 100 mL by 10 to get grams per liter, then divide by the molar mass. For 0.9% NaCl: 9 g/L ÷ 58.44 = 0.154 M.

## Significant figures

The precision of a molarity can't be better than the measurements behind it. If you weigh 5.84 g on a balance that reads to 0.01 g and use a 500 mL volumetric flask, three significant figures (0.200 M) is a fair result. Weighing to 0.1 g would only justify two (0.20 M). The calculator shows more digits so you can round to suit your equipment, and it keeps full precision until the final step.

## Temperature and volume

Liquids expand slightly as they warm, so the same solution occupies a little more volume at a higher temperature and its molarity falls slightly. Volumetric glassware is calibrated at a stated temperature, commonly 20 °C, which is printed on the flask. For everyday lab work the effect is small, but for precise analytical work, prepare and use solutions at the calibration temperature, or use molality, which is based on mass and doesn't change with temperature.

## Labeling and storing solutions

Label every solution with its name, concentration, the date it was made and your initials. Store solutions in closed containers, away from light if they're sensitive to it, and remember that some, such as sodium hydroxide, slowly absorb carbon dioxide from the air and change concentration. Standardize them by titration before precise use.

## Safety

Concentrated acids and bases such as hydrochloric acid and sodium hydroxide are corrosive. Wear eye protection and gloves, add acid to water (never water to acid), and follow your lab's safety rules and the substance's safety data sheet.

## Key terms

- **Solute:** the substance being dissolved.
- **Solvent:** the liquid it's dissolved in, usually water.
- **Solution:** the mixture of solute and solvent.
- **Mole:** 6.022 × 10²³ particles, the SI unit of amount of substance.
- **Molar mass:** the mass of one mole of a substance, in grams per mole.
- **Volumetric flask:** a flask calibrated to hold an exact volume at its mark.
- **Stock solution:** a concentrated solution kept on hand and diluted to make working solutions.
- **Meniscus:** the curved surface of a liquid in a narrow container, read at its lowest point.
