## How to use this flooring calculator

1. **Room size.** Measure the length and width of the room in feet at the widest points, wall to wall.
2. **Extra area.** Add closets, alcoves or a hallway section in square feet. For an L-shaped room, enter the larger rectangle as the room and the smaller one here.
3. **Waste allowance.** This covers cuts at walls, pieces that break or have defects, and the patterns you'll need to match.
4. **Coverage per box.** It's printed on the box, usually in square feet. Planks and tiles are sold by the box, so you can't buy a partial one.
5. **Price per square foot.** Optional, for a cost estimate of the flooring itself.

The result shows the number of boxes to buy, the square footage you'll actually get, and the cost, plus the effective cost per square foot of floor once waste and full boxes are counted.

## The formula

**Floor area = length × width + extra area**

**Area to buy = floor area × (1 + waste %)**

**Boxes = area to buy ÷ coverage per box, rounded up**

**Cost = boxes × coverage per box × price per sq ft**

## Worked example: a 12 × 15 ft room

- **Floor area:** 15 × 12 = **180 sq ft**.
- **With 10% waste:** 180 × 1.10 = **198 sq ft**.
- **Boxes:** 198 ÷ 20 = 9.9, so **10 boxes**, giving 200 sq ft.
- **Cost at $3.50 per sq ft:** 200 × $3.50 = **$700**.

Because you pay for waste and whole boxes, the real cost per square foot of floor covered is $700 ÷ 180 = **$3.89**, not $3.50. Use that figure when comparing quotes or products.

## How much waste to allow

| Layout | Typical waste allowance |
|---|---|
| Straight planks in a simple rectangular room | 5% to 10% |
| Rooms with closets, angles or many doorways | 10% to 15% |
| Diagonal layouts | 15% |
| Herringbone or chevron | 15% to 20% or more |
| Tile, especially large-format or natural stone | 10% to 15% |

For the 12 × 15 ft room with 20 sq ft boxes:

| Waste allowance | Area to buy | Boxes | Cost at $3.50 |
|---|---|---|---|
| 5% | 189 sq ft | 10 | $700 |
| 10% | 198 sq ft | 10 | $700 |
| 15% | 207 sq ft | 11 | $770 |
| 20% | 216 sq ft | 11 | $770 |

Because boxes come in fixed sizes, small changes in the allowance often don't change the number of boxes at all. When the result is just over a whole box, as at 15%, it's worth checking your measurements before buying the extra box.

## Several rooms or a hallway

When the same flooring runs through connected spaces, add them up before applying the waste allowance and rounding to boxes. Calculating each room separately and rounding each one up usually buys more boxes than you need.

For example, the 12 × 15 ft bedroom plus a 3 × 10 ft hallway (30 sq ft) is 210 sq ft. With 10% waste that's 231 sq ft: **12 boxes**, or 240 sq ft, costing $840 at $3.50. Calculated separately, the bedroom needs 10 boxes and the hallway 2 (33 sq ft ÷ 20 = 1.65), which is also 12 here, but with more rooms the separate roundings add up.

## Box size makes a difference

Two products at the same price per square foot can cost different amounts because of their box sizes. For the 12 × 15 ft room at 10% waste and $3.50 per sq ft:

- **20 sq ft boxes:** 10 boxes, 200 sq ft, **$700**.
- **23.4 sq ft boxes:** 9 boxes, 210.6 sq ft, **$737.10**.

The larger boxes leave more spare flooring, which is useful for repairs but costs $37 more. When you're close to a whole number of boxes, compare the box sizes as well as the price.

## Which way to lay the planks

Plank direction doesn't change the area, but it does affect waste and how the room looks:

- **Along the longest wall** is the most common choice, and usually wastes the least.
- **Toward the main light source,** such as a large window, makes seams less visible.
- **Along the line of sight** from the main entrance makes a room look longer.
- **Diagonally** can make a small room look bigger, but needs more cuts and a higher waste allowance.

Plan the layout so the last row along the far wall isn't a thin sliver. Measure the room width, divide by the plank width, and if the last row would be very narrow, cut the first row down so both edge rows are similar.

## Stairs

Stairs are calculated differently from flat floors, because each step has a tread (the part you stand on) and a riser (the vertical face), and both are covered with separate pieces. Measure one tread's depth and width and one riser's height and width, multiply each pair, add them together and multiply by the number of steps. Stair nosing pieces are usually sold individually, one per step, so count them rather than estimating by area. Because stair cuts are fiddly and pieces are short, allow at least 15% to 20% waste.

## Metric conversion

One square foot is exactly 0.09290304 square meters, so the 180 sq ft room is about 16.72 m². Many tile and flooring products list both units on the box.

## Measuring a room

- **Measure at floor level,** wall to wall, in two or three places, and use the largest figure.
- **Include doorways** to where the new floor will stop, usually the middle of the door frame under the door.
- **Split irregular rooms into rectangles.** Calculate each one and add them, or use the extra-area field for smaller sections.
- **Don't subtract built-in cabinets** in a kitchen unless you're sure you won't move them. Floating floors usually stop at cabinets, but tile under appliances is common.
- **Draw a sketch** with the measurements, and take it to the store. It helps staff check your numbers and suggest the best direction to lay the floor.

## Laminate, vinyl, hardwood and tile

The arithmetic is the same for every type of flooring, but each has its own considerations:

- **Laminate and luxury vinyl plank (LVP):** usually sold in boxes of about 18 to 25 sq ft. Most are floating floors that click together over an underlayment. Leave the expansion gap the manufacturer specifies at the walls.
- **Engineered and solid hardwood:** boxes are often 20 sq ft or so. Wood needs to acclimate to the room's temperature and humidity before installation; follow the manufacturer's instructions on how long.
- **Tile:** sold by the box or piece. Small tiles waste less per cut, while large-format tiles waste more and need a very flat subfloor. Don't forget thinset, grout and spacers.
- **Carpet:** usually sold by the square yard (9 sq ft) in rolls 12 or 15 ft wide, so seams and roll width affect the amount more than a simple waste percentage. Ask the installer to measure.

## What else you'll need

Flooring is rarely the only cost. Depending on the product and your subfloor:

- **Underlayment** for floating floors, unless it's pre-attached.
- **Moisture barrier** over concrete subfloors.
- **Transition strips** where the new floor meets another surface.
- **Baseboards or quarter-round** to cover the expansion gap.
- **Thinset, grout and sealer** for tile.
- **Removal and disposal** of the old flooring.
- **Installation**, if you're not doing it yourself, usually priced per square foot.

For a concrete subfloor in a basement or garage, check that the slab is flat and dry before installing. If you're pouring new concrete, the [concrete calculator](/construction/concrete-calculator/) works out the volume.

## Buying tips

- **Buy all the boxes at once** and check that they share the same lot or dye number.
- **Mix planks from several boxes** as you install, so any color variation is spread across the floor.
- **Keep a spare box** for future repairs, stored flat in a dry place with the receipt and the product name.
- **Check the return policy.** Many stores accept unopened boxes, which makes buying an extra box low-risk.

## Key terms

- **Square foot:** a square 1 foot on each side; most flooring is priced and sold in square feet.
- **Waste allowance:** extra material for cuts, breakage and pattern matching.
- **Floating floor:** a floor that isn't glued or nailed down, such as most laminate and LVP.
- **Acclimation:** letting flooring adjust to the room's temperature and humidity before installation.
- **Expansion gap:** a small gap around the edges so the floor can expand and contract.
- **Lot number:** the production batch, which should match across all boxes.
- **Subfloor:** the structural layer under the finished floor, usually plywood or concrete.
