## How to use this temperature converter

1. **Enter the temperature.** Decimals and negative numbers are fine, such as −12.5 °F.
2. **Choose the scales.** Pick the scale you're converting from and the one you want: Fahrenheit (°F), Celsius (°C), kelvin (K) or Rankine (°R).
3. **Read the result.** The headline shows the converted temperature with the formula used. Below it are the temperature in kelvin and the same number converted as a temperature change, which works differently.
4. **Check the tables.** One shows your temperature on all four scales; the other lists reference points such as freezing and boiling water, body temperature and a moderate oven.

If you enter a temperature below absolute zero, the converter tells you, because no real temperature is that cold.

## The formulas

| From | To | Formula |
|---|---|---|
| Fahrenheit | Celsius | °C = (°F − 32) × 5/9 |
| Celsius | Fahrenheit | °F = °C × 9/5 + 32 |
| Celsius | Kelvin | K = °C + 273.15 |
| Kelvin | Celsius | °C = K − 273.15 |
| Fahrenheit | Kelvin | K = (°F − 32) × 5/9 + 273.15 |
| Kelvin | Rankine | °R = K × 9/5 |
| Fahrenheit | Rankine | °R = °F + 459.67 |

The converter turns every temperature into kelvin first, then into the scale you want, using these exact definitions. Results are shown to four decimal places.

## Worked example: 72 °F to Celsius

1. Subtract 32: 72 − 32 = 40.
2. Multiply by 5/9: 40 × 5 ÷ 9 = **22.22 °C**.

In kelvin, that's 22.22 + 273.15 = 295.37 K. The order matters: subtract first, then multiply. Multiplying first gives a wrong answer.

The other way, 22 °C to Fahrenheit: 22 × 9/5 = 39.6, plus 32 = **71.6 °F**.

## Oven temperatures

Recipes from the U.S. use Fahrenheit, most other countries use Celsius, and older British recipes use gas marks. The exact conversions are rarely round numbers, so cooks round to the nearest 5 or 10 degrees:

| Fahrenheit | Exact Celsius | Usually written | Gas mark |
|---|---|---|---|
| 325 °F | 162.8 °C | 160–170 °C | 3 |
| 350 °F | 176.7 °C | 180 °C | 4 |
| 375 °F | 190.6 °C | 190 °C | 5 |
| 400 °F | 204.4 °C | 200 °C | 6 |
| 425 °F | 218.3 °C | 220 °C | 7 |
| 450 °F | 232.2 °C | 230 °C | 8 |

Fan (convection) ovens cook faster, so recipes often suggest reducing the temperature by about 20 °C (around 25 °F) when using one. Oven thermostats are often off by 10 degrees or more anyway, so an inexpensive oven thermometer helps more than precise conversion. When you're also changing a recipe's size, the [recipe scaler](/food/recipe-scaler/) adjusts the ingredients; cooking times may need checking too.

## Food safety temperatures

Food safety guidance in the U.S. is given in Fahrenheit. These are the USDA's safe minimum internal temperatures, measured with a food thermometer in the thickest part:

| Food | Fahrenheit | Celsius |
|---|---|---|
| Poultry, including ground poultry | 165 °F | 73.9 °C |
| Ground beef, pork and lamb | 160 °F | 71.1 °C |
| Beef, pork, veal and lamb steaks, chops and roasts (then rest 3 minutes) | 145 °F | 62.8 °C |
| Fish and shellfish | 145 °F | 62.8 °C |
| Refrigerator | 40 °F or below | 4.4 °C or below |
| Freezer | 0 °F | −17.8 °C |

Bacteria grow fastest between 40 °F and 140 °F (4.4 °C to 60 °C), often called the danger zone, which is why leftovers should be refrigerated promptly. If a recipe or thermometer uses the other scale, convert exactly here rather than estimating, since a few degrees matter.

## Boiling point and altitude

Water boils at 212 °F (100 °C) only at sea-level air pressure. At higher altitudes the air pressure is lower and water boils at a lower temperature: roughly 1 °F lower for every 500 feet of elevation. At 5,000 feet, water boils at about 203 °F (95 °C). That's why pasta, beans and boiled eggs take longer in mountain towns, and why some baking recipes include high-altitude adjustments. A thermometer reading of 203 °F in boiling water at altitude is normal, not a faulty thermometer.

## Thermometer accuracy

A conversion is only as accurate as the reading. Digital thermometers are typically accurate to within about 1 °F (0.5 °C), and cheaper dial thermometers can be further off. To check a food thermometer, put it in a glass of ice water: it should read 32 °F (0 °C). Thermometers that show both scales are convenient, but check which scale is active before you read it.

## Converting a temperature change

A temperature **difference** converts without the 32 offset, because only the size of the degree matters:

- A Celsius degree equals 1.8 Fahrenheit degrees.
- A kelvin equals a Celsius degree, and a Rankine degree equals a Fahrenheit degree.

So a rise of 10 °C is a rise of 18 °F, and a 2 °C change in climate is a 3.6 °F change; 1.5 °C is 2.7 °F. Converting a change with the ordinary formula is a common mistake: treating a 10 °C rise as "50 °F" overstates it almost threefold. The converter shows both readings for any number you enter.

## Quick mental conversions

- **°C to °F:** double it and add 30. 20 °C → 70 °F (exact 68 °F); 30 °C → 90 °F (exact 86 °F).
- **°F to °C:** subtract 30 and halve. 80 °F → 25 °C (exact 26.7 °C).
- **Useful anchors:** 10 °C = 50 °F, 16 °C ≈ 61 °F, 28 °C ≈ 82 °F. The digits of 16 and 61, and 28 and 82, swap.

The shortcuts are good within a couple of degrees for everyday weather. Use the exact formula for cooking, science and medicine.

## Kelvin and Rankine

Kelvin is the SI unit of temperature, used in science and engineering. It starts at **absolute zero**, the lowest possible temperature, where particles have the least possible energy: 0 K = −273.15 °C = −459.67 °F. Kelvin has no degree sign ("300 K", not "300 °K") and no negative values. Since 2019 the kelvin has been defined by fixing the Boltzmann constant, a constant of nature, and the Celsius scale is defined from it as K − 273.15.

Rankine is the absolute scale with Fahrenheit-sized degrees: 0 °R is absolute zero and water freezes at 491.67 °R. It's used mainly in some U.S. engineering fields, such as thermodynamics and aerospace. Some examples on the absolute scales:

- Room temperature: 25 °C = 298.15 K.
- Liquid nitrogen boils at about 77 K (−196 °C, −321 °F).
- A hot summer day of 300 K is 26.85 °C or 80.33 °F.

## Body and weather temperatures

Normal body temperature is often given as 98.6 °F (37 °C), but healthy readings vary by about a degree between people, by time of day and by where it's measured. A reading of 100.4 °F (38 °C) or higher is commonly treated as a fever in adults. For weather, 0 °C (32 °F) is the freezing point of water, 20 °C (68 °F) is mild, 30 °C (86 °F) is hot, and 40 °C (104 °F) is dangerously hot.

## A short history of the scales

Daniel Gabriel Fahrenheit proposed his scale in 1724, setting 0 °F near the coldest temperature he could make with a salt and ice mixture. Anders Celsius proposed a 100-step scale between the freezing and boiling points of water in 1742, though in his original version water boiled at 0 and froze at 100; it was reversed soon after. Lord Kelvin proposed an absolute scale in 1848, and William Rankine proposed its Fahrenheit-degree counterpart in 1859. Today the United States and a few other countries use Fahrenheit for daily life, while almost everywhere else uses Celsius.

## Common mistakes

- **Multiplying before subtracting.** For Fahrenheit to Celsius, subtract 32 first. 72 × 5/9 − 32 gives 8 °C, not the correct 22.2 °C.
- **Adding 273 instead of 273.15.** The difference is small, but for science homework and lab work the exact figure matters.
- **Writing "degrees kelvin".** Kelvin temperatures are written without the degree sign: 300 K.
- **Converting a change like a reading.** A rise of 5 °C is a rise of 9 °F, not 41 °F.
- **Rounding too early.** Keep the decimals until the last step, then round once.

## Key terms

- **Celsius (°C):** water freezes at 0 °C and boils at 100 °C at sea level.
- **Fahrenheit (°F):** water freezes at 32 °F and boils at 212 °F at sea level.
- **Kelvin (K):** the SI temperature unit, starting at absolute zero.
- **Rankine (°R):** an absolute scale with Fahrenheit-sized degrees.
- **Absolute zero:** 0 K, −273.15 °C or −459.67 °F, the coldest possible temperature.
- **Temperature difference:** a change in temperature, converted without the scales' offsets.
