## How to use this salary to hourly calculator

Enter what you're paid and choose the period: per hour, per day, per week, every two weeks, twice a month, per month or per year. The calculator converts it to every other period at once. It works in both directions: pick *per year* to find your hourly wage, or *per hour* to find your annual salary.

1. **Pay and period.** For example, $75,000 per year or $25 per hour.
2. **Hours and days per week.** 40 and 5 for a standard full-time job.
3. **More options.** *Paid weeks per year* stays at 52 for a salaried job; lower it if you're paid hourly and take unpaid weeks off. *Paid days off* shows what you earn per hour you actually work.

## Worked example: comparing a salaried offer with an hourly job

Maya has two offers. One pays a $62,000 salary with 10 paid holidays and 11 vacation days. The other pays $30 an hour, 40 hours a week, with no paid time off.

- **Salaried offer:** $62,000 ÷ 2,080 hours = **$29.81 an hour**.
- **Hourly offer:** $30 × 2,080 = **$62,400 a year**, but only if she works all 52 weeks. Every unpaid day off costs $240.

On paper the hourly job pays $400 more. But the salaried job pays for 21 days off: 168 hours. Dividing $62,000 by the 1,912 hours she'd actually work gives **$32.43 per hour worked**. If Maya plans to take the same three or so weeks off either way, the salaried job pays more.

## The formulas in plain English

**Hourly rate = annual salary ÷ (hours per week × weeks per year)**

**Annual salary = hourly rate × hours per week × weeks per year**

Every other period comes from the annual figure: monthly is ÷ 12, semimonthly ÷ 24, biweekly ÷ 26 and weekly ÷ 52. The **Show the math** panel above shows each step with your numbers.

## What a simple conversion leaves out

Converting a salary to an hourly rate is a good first comparison, but an hourly figure doesn't capture everything a job is worth.

**Overtime.** Hourly, non-exempt employees must be paid at least 1.5 times their regular rate for hours over 40 in a workweek under the Fair Labor Standards Act. At $25 an hour, five hours of overtime a week adds $187.50, for $1,187.50 a week or **$61,750 a year**, compared with $52,000 for a 40-hour week. Most salaried employees are exempt and earn the same pay however many hours they work, so a salary that looks higher can work out lower per hour if the job routinely runs to 50 hours a week. Enter your real weekly hours to see it.

**Benefits.** Health insurance, retirement matches and paid leave are worth thousands of dollars a year. An employer that pays most of a family health plan or matches 5% of salary in a 401(k) can make a lower salary the better offer.

**Unpaid time.** Commutes, unpaid breaks and on-call time don't appear in the hours, but they still take your time. If a job requires an hour's commute each way, a 40-hour week is really 50 hours away from home.

## Overtime pay by hours worked

For hourly, non-exempt workers, every hour over 40 in a workweek is paid at 1.5 times the regular rate. Here's what that means at $25 an hour, if the same schedule holds all year:

| Hours per week | Weekly pay | Yearly pay |
|---|---|---|
| 40 | $1,000.00 | $52,000 |
| 45 | $1,187.50 | $61,750 |
| 50 | $1,375.00 | $71,500 |
| 55 | $1,562.50 | $81,250 |
| 60 | $1,750.00 | $91,000 |

Ten hours of overtime a week adds $19,500 a year, 37.5% more pay for 25% more hours. To convert an overtime-heavy schedule, enter your average weekly pay and choose *per week*; the calculator then gives the equivalent hourly figure across all your hours. Some states, including California, also require overtime after 8 hours in a single day. Shift differentials and holiday premiums work the same way: average them into your weekly pay.

## What long weeks do to a salaried rate

Most salaried employees are exempt from overtime, so working more hours lowers their effective hourly pay. On a $75,000 salary:

| Hours actually worked per week | Effective hourly rate |
|---|---|
| 40 | $36.06 |
| 45 | $32.05 |
| 50 | $28.85 |
| 55 | $26.22 |

A salaried job that routinely runs to 50 hours a week pays the same per hour as a $60,000 job at 40 hours. When comparing offers, ask about typical hours and enter them in the **Hours per week** field rather than assuming 40.

## Converting a contractor or freelance rate

Comparing a 1099 contract rate with an employee's wage takes more than the 2,080-hour formula, because contractors cover costs employers normally pay:

- **Self-employment tax.** Contractors pay both halves of Social Security and Medicare, 15.3% on most of their net earnings, instead of the employee's 7.65%. On $90,000 of net income that's about $12,717.
- **Unbilled time.** Finding clients, invoicing and admin aren't billable. Many freelancers bill 1,200 to 1,600 hours a year, not 2,080.
- **No paid time off or benefits.** Holidays, vacation, health insurance and retirement matches all come out of the contractor's own pocket.

A common rule of thumb is that a contract rate needs to be roughly 1.25 to 1.5 times an employee's hourly wage to come out even, and more for short or unpredictable projects. For example, $50 an hour for 1,800 billable hours is $90,000. The employer half of Social Security and Medicare alone takes about $6,358 more than an employee earning $90,000 would pay, before health insurance, retirement savings or any unpaid weeks between projects.

## Part-time, seasonal and irregular schedules

The calculator works for any regular schedule. Enter your usual weekly hours, and lower **Paid weeks per year** under More options for work that isn't year-round:

- **Part-time:** $18 an hour for 25 hours a week is $23,400 a year.
- **Seasonal:** $22 an hour, 40 hours a week for 30 weeks, is $26,400.
- **School-year jobs:** many school staff are paid for about 40 weeks; some districts spread that pay over 12 months, so the monthly check is lower than the weekly rate suggests.

If your hours change from week to week, add up a few months of pay, divide by the number of weeks, and enter that average as weekly pay.

## Hourly vs salaried: other differences

Pay per hour isn't the only difference between the two:

- **Overtime.** Hourly workers are usually paid for every extra hour; most salaried workers aren't.
- **Predictability.** A salary pays the same every period. Hourly pay can drop when shifts are cut, or rise with extra hours.
- **Benefits.** Full-time salaried roles more often include health insurance, retirement matches and paid leave, although many hourly jobs offer them too.
- **Flexibility.** Hourly work makes it easy to be paid for exactly what you work; salaried work often allows more flexibility in when you work.

Being paid a salary doesn't automatically make you exempt from overtime. Under federal rules, exemption depends on your duties and on earning at least a minimum salary, and some states set higher thresholds. If you're salaried but your work doesn't meet the tests, you may still be owed overtime; the Department of Labor's Wage and Hour Division explains the rules.

## Key terms

- **Gross pay:** pay before taxes and deductions.
- **2,080 hours:** a standard full-time year (40 × 52), used by most employers. Federal agencies use 2,087.
- **Exempt and non-exempt:** salaried "exempt" employees aren't paid overtime under the Fair Labor Standards Act; "non-exempt" employees get 1.5 times their rate after 40 hours a week.
- **Biweekly:** every two weeks, 26 paychecks a year (27 in some years).
- **Semimonthly:** twice a month, 24 paychecks a year.
