## How to use this student loan calculator

1. **Loan balance.** Your total federal Direct Loans when repayment begins, including any unpaid interest that was added to the balance. Your servicer's website or StudentAid.gov shows it.
2. **Interest rate.** Each loan has its own fixed rate. If you have several, use the weighted average: multiply each balance by its rate, add the results and divide by the total balance. A consolidation loan uses the same weighted average, rounded up to the next one-eighth of a percent.
3. **Repayment plan.** Pick the plan you want to see. The table under the results compares all four fixed-payment plans at once, with who can use each.
4. **Extra payments.** Under More options, add an amount you'd pay every month on top of the required payment.

The headline shows the monthly payment (a range for the Graduated plan), with the total interest, total paid and the first month's interest below. The schedule shows every payment.

## The fixed-payment plans

| Plan | Term | Payment | Who can use it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Standard | 10 years | Level | Loans first disbursed before July 1, 2026 |
| Graduated | 10 years | Starts low, rises every 2 years | Loans first disbursed before July 1, 2026 |
| Extended | 25 years | Level (fixed option) | Before July 1, 2026, with more than $30,000 in Direct Loans |
| Tiered Standard | 10 to 25 years by balance | Level | Loans first disbursed on or after July 1, 2026 |

The Tiered Standard term depends on your balance when you enter repayment:

| Balance | Term | Example payment at 6.52% |
|---|---|---|
| Under $25,000 | 10 years | $227.30 on $20,000 |
| $25,000 to $49,999 | 15 years | $305.27 on $35,000 |
| $50,000 to $99,999 | 20 years | $448.05 on $60,000 (or $504.48 at 8.07%) |
| $100,000 or more | 25 years | $776.46 on $100,000 at 8.07% |

For new loans, the other option is the income-based Repayment Assistance Plan (RAP). Older loans can also use Income-Based Repayment (IBR). Our guide to [student loan repayment plans](/guides/student-loan-repayment-plans/) explains how to choose.

## Worked example: $35,000 at 6.52%

A borrower leaves school with $35,000 of undergraduate loans at 6.52%. The first month's interest is $190.17 (35,000 × 0.0652 ÷ 12). Here's what each fixed plan costs:

| Plan | Monthly payment | Term | Total interest |
|---|---|---|---|
| Standard | $397.77 | 10 years | $12,733.13 |
| Graduated | $228.75 rising to $686.20 | 10 years | $16,207.59 |
| Extended | $236.76 | 25 years | $36,027.99 |
| Tiered Standard | $305.27 | 15 years | $19,949.23 |

The Standard plan is the cheapest by far. Extended cuts the payment by $161 a month but nearly triples the interest. If these were new loans after July 1, 2026, the Tiered Standard plan would put this balance on a 15-year term, costing $7,216.10 more interest than a 10-year schedule would. You can still pay it off in 10 years by paying $93 extra each month, since there's no penalty for paying ahead.

## How the Graduated plan steps up

Federal rules set three limits for the Graduated plan: it lasts up to 10 years, each payment at least covers the interest that accrues, and no payment is more than three times any other. Servicers set the exact schedule. This calculator estimates it with payments that rise by the same percentage every two years, five steps in all:

| Years | Monthly payment on $35,000 at 6.52% |
|---|---|
| 1–2 | $228.75 |
| 3–4 | $301.05 |
| 5–6 | $396.21 |
| 7–8 | $521.44 |
| 9–10 | $686.20 |

The plan suits people who expect their income to rise steadily, such as early-career professionals. It costs $3,474.46 more interest than Standard, because the balance falls slowly in the first years while payments are low. Your servicer's numbers may differ by a few dollars; check your repayment disclosure.

## Graduate school balances

Graduate loans carry higher rates and larger balances, which makes the plan choice matter more. For $60,000 at 8.07%:

| Plan | Monthly payment | Total interest |
|---|---|---|
| Standard (10 years) | $730.19 | $27,622.19 |
| Graduated | $427.01 rising to $1,281.06 | $35,592.59 |
| Tiered Standard (20 years) | $504.48 | $61,075.86 |
| Extended (25 years) | $465.88 | $79,759.28 |

A new $60,000 graduate balance would go on the 20-year tier, costing more than double the interest of a 10-year payoff. Adding $200 a month to that tiered payment finishes in 10 years 7 months and saves $31,690.63. At $100,000 and 8.07%, the 25-year tier costs $776.46 a month and $132,937.01 in interest, against $1,216.98 a month and $46,037.19 in interest over 10 years.

## Paying extra

Federal student loans have no prepayment penalty. Any extra goes first to interest that has accrued since your last payment, then to principal. For $35,000 at 6.52% on the Standard plan:

| Extra a month | Paid off in | Interest saved |
|---|---|---|
| $50 | 8 years 7 months | $2,047.48 |
| $100 | 7 years 5 months | $3,516.84 |
| $200 | 5 years 11 months | $5,490.83 |

Tell your servicer to apply extra payments to principal and not to advance your due date; otherwise they may treat the money as an early payment of next month's bill. If you have several loans, putting the extra on the highest-rate loan first saves the most.

## Working out your weighted average rate

Most borrowers have a loan for each school year, each with its own rate. To enter one rate here, weight each rate by its balance. For three loans:

| Loan | Balance | Rate | Balance × rate |
|---|---|---|---|
| First year | $5,500 | 5.50% | 30,250 |
| Second year | $6,500 | 6.53% | 42,445 |
| Third year | $7,500 | 6.52% | 48,900 |
| **Total** | **$19,500** | | **121,595** |

The weighted average is 121,595 ÷ 19,500 = **6.24%**. A Direct Consolidation Loan would combine them at that average rounded up to the next one-eighth of a percent, 6.25%. Consolidating doesn't lower your rate; it simplifies payments and can change which plans you qualify for, so check the rules before you consolidate older loans.

## If you can't make the payment

Missing payments on federal loans has serious consequences, but there are options before that happens:

- **Change plans.** You can usually switch to a plan with a lower payment, including an income-based plan, by applying through StudentAid.gov.
- **Deferment or forbearance.** These pause or reduce payments for a while, for reasons such as unemployment, economic hardship or going back to school. Interest usually keeps accruing on unsubsidized loans.
- **Talk to your servicer early.** It's free, and it's far easier to fix before a payment is missed.

A loan is delinquent the day after a missed payment and goes into default after 270 days. Default can lead to wage garnishment, seizure of tax refunds and damaged credit, and you lose access to most repayment options until the loan is brought back into good standing.

## Interest on student loans

Federal student loans charge simple daily interest: the annual rate ÷ 365.25 × the balance, for each day. Like most planning tools, this calculator uses the rate ÷ 12 per month, which gives the same total within a few dollars over a typical schedule. Things that change your real balance:

- **Interest during school and grace.** Unsubsidized and PLUS loans accrue interest while you're in school and during the six-month grace period. Paying it as it accrues keeps the balance from growing.
- **Capitalization.** When unpaid interest is added to the principal, you start paying interest on interest. Under current rules this happens mainly when certain deferments end or you leave some plans.
- **Fees.** Federal loans charge an origination fee taken from each disbursement (about 1% for Direct Loans and about 4% for PLUS), so you receive slightly less than you owe.

## Standard plan or a longer one?

Choose the longest term you need for a payment you can afford, then pay extra whenever you can. A lower required payment protects you in a tight month, while extra payments get you to the Standard plan's cost when money allows. Check the payment against your budget: the [paycheck calculator](/finance/paycheck-calculator/) shows your take-home pay, and lenders look at your [debt-to-income ratio](/finance/debt-to-income-ratio-calculator/) when you apply for a mortgage or car loan later.

If you have private student loans, or several debts at once, the [debt payoff calculator](/finance/debt-payoff-calculator/) compares paying the highest rate first with paying the smallest balance first.

## Common mistakes

- **Using the amount borrowed instead of the current balance.** Interest that accrued during school may have been added, so you can owe more than you borrowed. Use the balance on your servicer's site.
- **Comparing plans by payment only.** The lowest payment is almost always the most expensive plan overall. Look at the total interest column too.
- **Forgetting private loans.** Private student loans follow their lender's terms, not federal plans. Enter them separately, with the lender's rate and term, using the [loan calculator](/finance/loan-calculator/).
- **Letting extra payments advance the due date.** Unless you ask for them to go to principal, extra payments may simply prepay next month's bill.

## Key terms

- **Direct Loan:** a federal student loan made by the U.S. Department of Education, including subsidized, unsubsidized, PLUS and consolidation loans.
- **Servicer:** the company that collects payments and manages your federal loans.
- **Capitalization:** adding unpaid interest to the principal balance.
- **Weighted average rate:** the combined rate of several loans, weighted by balance.
- **Tiered Standard plan:** the fixed plan for loans first disbursed on or after July 1, 2026, with a term set by the balance.
- **Repayment Assistance Plan (RAP):** the income-based plan for new loans, with payments based on income and family size.
