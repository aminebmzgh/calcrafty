  - q: "What is the difference between APR and interest rate?"
    a: "The interest rate is the cost of borrowing the money itself. The APR adds the lender's required fees, such as origination fees and points, and spreads them over the loan term as a yearly rate. With no fees, the two are the same; the bigger the fees, the further the APR rises above the rate."
  - q: "How is APR calculated?"
    a: "Lenders follow Regulation Z: the APR is the rate at which the present value of your payments equals the amount financed, which is the loan amount minus prepaid fees. On $20,000 at 7% for 60 months with a $600 fee, the payment is $396.02 and you really receive $19,400, which works out to an APR of 8.29%."
  - q: "Which fees are included in APR?"
    a: "Finance charges the lender requires as a condition of the loan: origination and underwriting fees, discount points, mortgage broker fees and prepaid interest, plus mortgage insurance on home loans. Third-party costs you'd pay anyway, such as appraisal, title insurance, credit reports and government recording fees, are generally excluded for mortgages."
  - q: "Is a lower APR always better?"
    a: "Not always. APR assumes you keep the loan for its full term. Fees are paid once, so if you repay or refinance early, they cost more per year than the APR shows. A mortgage with points can have the lower APR over 30 years but cost more if you sell after five."
  - q: "Why does a short loan with fees have such a high APR?"
    a: "The same fee is spread over fewer months. A $600 fee on a $20,000 loan at 7% gives an APR of 12.76% over 12 months but 8.29% over 60 months. That's why APR matters most when comparing short personal loans and cash advances."
  - q: "Is APR the same as APY?"
    a: "No. APR is used for loans and doesn't include compounding within the year. APY (annual percentage yield) is used for savings and does include compounding, so a savings account paying 5% compounded monthly has an APY of about 5.12%."

## How to use this APR calculator

Enter the loan amount, the interest rate and the term, then add the lender's fees. The APR updates as you type, along with the monthly payment, the amount you actually receive and the total cost of borrowing.

1. **Loan amount.** The amount on the loan offer.
2. **Interest rate.** The note rate, before fees. It's the rate used to work out your payment.
3. **Loan term.** In months: 36 for three years, 60 for five, 360 for a 30-year mortgage.
4. **Fees and points.** The charges the lender requires to give you the loan: origination or underwriting fees and discount points. On mortgages, leave out third-party costs such as the appraisal and title insurance.
5. **How the fees are paid.** Most personal loans deduct the fee from the money you receive; choose *Upfront or deducted*. If the fee is added to your balance, choose *Added to the loan*.

## Worked example: two personal loan offers

A borrower needs $15,000 for 36 months and gets two offers:

- **Offer A:** 9.99% interest with a 5% origination fee ($750) deducted from the loan.
- **Offer B:** 11.49% interest with no fee.

| Detail | Offer A | Offer B |
|---|---|---|
| Interest rate | 9.99% | 11.49% |
| Fee | $750 | $0 |
| Money received | $14,250 | $15,000 |
| Monthly payment | $483.94 | $494.57 |
| Finance charge | $3,171.84 | $2,804.52 |
| **APR** | **13.55%** | **11.49%** |

Offer A has the lower rate and the lower payment, but you receive $750 less and pay $367 more to borrow. Its APR of 13.55% shows that straight away. The APR is the only number that puts both offers on the same footing.

## How lenders calculate APR

The method comes from the federal Truth in Lending Act and its Regulation Z, so every lender in the US uses the same one.

1. **Amount financed** = loan amount − prepaid finance charges. If fees are added to the balance instead, the amount financed is the loan amount and the payment is worked out on the larger balance.
2. **Payment** = the standard loan payment at the interest rate: M = P × r(1 + r)ⁿ ÷ ((1 + r)ⁿ − 1), where r is the rate ÷ 12.
3. **APR** = the yearly rate at which the present value of all the payments equals the amount financed. There's no direct formula for it, so it's found numerically, the same way a spreadsheet's RATE function works.

The **Show the math** panel above runs through all three steps with your numbers. Regulation Z lets a lender's disclosed APR be off by at most 1/8 of a percentage point on a regular loan, which is why APRs are usually quoted to two decimals.

## Why the term changes the APR

Fees are paid once, but the APR spreads them over the whole loan. The shorter the loan, the more each year has to carry. Here's a $20,000 loan at 7% with the same $600 fee:

| Term | Monthly payment | APR |
|---|---|---|
| 12 months | $1,730.53 | 12.76% |
| 24 months | $895.45 | 10.03% |
| 36 months | $617.54 | 9.07% |
| 60 months | $396.02 | 8.29% |
| 84 months | $301.85 | 7.95% |

## When APR misleads: points on a mortgage

APR assumes you keep the loan to the end. Most people sell or refinance a mortgage long before 30 years, and that's where APR can point the wrong way.

Compare two $300,000, 30-year mortgages:

- **With points:** 6.5% plus $6,000 in points and fees. Payment $1,896.20, APR **6.70%**.
- **Without points:** 6.75% with no points. Payment $1,945.79, APR **6.75%**.

Over 30 years, paying points wins. But if you sell or refinance after five years, the $6,000 is spread over only 60 payments, and the real yearly cost of the first loan is about **6.99%**, more than the 6.75% loan without points. After three years it's about 7.26%. Before paying points, work out how many months of lower payments it takes to earn the fee back, and be honest about how long you'll keep the loan.

## Key terms

- **APR (annual percentage rate):** the yearly cost of credit including required fees, calculated under Regulation Z.
- **Note rate:** the interest rate in the loan contract, used to calculate the payment.
- **Amount financed:** the credit you actually receive: the loan amount minus prepaid finance charges.
- **Finance charge:** everything the credit costs you: all interest plus the fees counted in the APR.
- **Discount points:** an upfront payment to lower the interest rate, usually 1% of the loan per point.
- **Origination fee:** a lender's charge for processing the loan, common on personal loans (often 1% to 10%).

## What to do with your result

Use the APR to compare offers for the same amount and term, and look at the finance charge to see the cost in dollars. If a lender quotes only a rate, ask for the APR; on mortgages and most consumer loans it must appear on your disclosures.
