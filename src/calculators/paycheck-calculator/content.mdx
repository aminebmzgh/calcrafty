faq:
  - q: How much of my paycheck goes to taxes?
    a: For most workers, between 15% and 30% of gross pay goes to taxes. Everyone pays 7.65% in Social Security and Medicare. Federal income tax depends on your bracket after the standard deduction, and state tax ranges from 0% in states like Texas and Florida to over 9% at higher incomes in states like California. A single filer earning $68,000 with a 4% state rate pays about 18.7% in total.
  - q: What is the standard deduction for 2026?
    a: The 2026 standard deduction is $16,100 for single filers and married people filing separately, $32,200 for married couples filing jointly, and $24,150 for heads of household, according to IRS Revenue Procedure 2025-32. It's subtracted from your income before federal tax brackets are applied, which is why the first part of your pay is effectively tax-free.
  - q: How do I convert my hourly wage to an annual salary?
    a: Multiply your hourly wage by your weekly hours and then by 52 weeks. At $22.50 an hour and 40 hours a week, that's $22.50 × 40 × 52 = $46,800 a year. To go the other way, divide an annual salary by 2,080 (40 hours × 52 weeks), so a $68,000 salary works out to $32.69 an hour.
  - q: Does contributing to a 401(k) reduce my take-home pay?
    a: Yes, but by less than you contribute. Traditional 401(k) contributions come out before federal and most state income tax, so part of each dollar you save would have gone to taxes anyway. In our example, a 6% contribution of $156.92 per paycheck lowers take-home pay by only $131.82, because federal and state income tax drop too. Social Security and Medicare still apply to 401(k) contributions.
  - q: Why is my first paycheck of the year different from this estimate?
    a: This calculator estimates your annual tax and spreads it evenly across the year. Real paychecks can differ because of W-4 adjustments, bonuses withheld at a flat 22%, mid-year raises, the Social Security wage cap ($184,500 in 2026) being reached late in the year, or benefits that start partway through. Over a full year, your withholding should land close to this estimate if your W-4 has no adjustments.
  - q: What is the Additional Medicare Tax?
    a: It's an extra 0.9% Medicare tax on wages above $200,000. Employers must start withholding it once your wages for the year pass $200,000, whatever your filing status. Married couples filing jointly don't owe it until combined wages pass $250,000, so some of it may come back as a refund when you file.

## How to use this paycheck calculator

Pick salary or hourly, enter your pay, and the calculator shows your take-home pay per paycheck instantly. Filing status, pay frequency and your 401(k) percentage make the biggest difference to the result, so set those first.

1. **Pay type.** Enter an annual salary, or switch to **Hourly** and enter your wage and weekly hours. The results also show the equivalent hourly rate or annual salary.
2. **Paid and filing status.** Biweekly pay (26 paychecks) is the most common schedule in the US. Use the filing status you'll put on your tax return.
3. **401(k) contribution.** Enter the percentage you contribute to a traditional 401(k) or 403(b). Roth contributions are after-tax; leave them out here and subtract them from the result.
4. **State income tax.** Enter your effective state rate, or 0 if you live in a state with no income tax. State pages with exact brackets are linked below the calculator.
5. **Pre-tax benefits.** Under **More options**, add yearly health, dental and vision premiums plus any HSA or FSA payroll contributions.

## Worked example: a $68,000 salary paid every two weeks

A single filer earns $68,000 a year, is paid biweekly, puts 6% into a 401(k), pays $2,400 a year in pre-tax health premiums and lives in a state with a 4% flat income tax. Here's one paycheck:

| Line | Per paycheck | Per year |
|---|---|---|
| Gross pay | $2,615.38 | $68,000.00 |
| Federal income tax | −$200.09 | −$5,202.40 |
| Social Security (6.2%) | −$156.43 | −$4,067.20 |
| Medicare (1.45%) | −$36.58 | −$951.20 |
| State income tax (4%) | −$94.65 | −$2,460.80 |
| 401(k) (6%) | −$156.92 | −$4,080.00 |
| Health premiums | −$92.31 | −$2,400.00 |
| **Take-home pay** | **$1,878.40** | **$48,838.40** |

Federal taxable income is $68,000 − $4,080 − $2,400 − $16,100 = $45,420. The first $12,400 is taxed at 10% ($1,240) and the remaining $33,020 at 12% ($3,962.40), for $5,202.40 in federal tax. Even though this worker is "in the 12% bracket," their federal tax is only 7.7% of gross pay.

Social Security and Medicare are figured on $65,600, because the health premiums are exempt from FICA but the 401(k) contribution isn't. Without the 401(k), take-home pay would be $2,010.22, so saving $156.92 per paycheck only costs $131.82 of spending money.

## How take-home pay is calculated

Take-home pay is gross pay minus four things: pre-tax deductions, federal income tax, FICA (Social Security and Medicare) and state or local tax. Federal income tax is progressive: each slice of income is taxed at its own rate.

1. **Gross pay** = salary, or hourly wage × hours per week × 52.
2. **Federal taxable income** = gross − 401(k) − pre-tax benefits − standard deduction.
3. **Federal tax** = the sum of each bracket slice × its rate (10%, 12%, 22%, 24%, 32%, 35%, 37%).
4. **Social Security** = 6.2% of wages up to $184,500; **Medicare** = 1.45% of all wages, plus 0.9% above $200,000.
5. **Take-home pay** = gross − all of the above, divided by the number of paychecks.

This matches how IRS Publication 15-T's percentage method works for a W-4 with no adjustments: it annualizes your pay, applies the standard deduction and brackets, then spreads the tax over your pay periods. The **Show the math** panel above runs these steps with your numbers.

## Key terms

- **Gross pay:** what you earn before anything is taken out.
- **Net pay (take-home pay):** what's deposited in your account.
- **Marginal tax rate:** the rate on your last dollar of income. A raise is taxed at this rate.
- **Effective tax rate:** total taxes divided by gross pay; usually far lower than the marginal rate.
- **FICA:** Social Security and Medicare payroll taxes, 7.65% combined for employees.
- **Section 125 (cafeteria plan):** lets health premiums, HSA and FSA contributions come out before income tax and FICA.

## What to do with your result

Build your budget on take-home pay, not salary. A common rule of thumb is to keep housing under about 28% of gross income; check what that means for a home with the [mortgage affordability calculator](/finance/mortgage-affordability-calculator/), and see the full payment with the [mortgage calculator](/finance/mortgage-calculator/).

If you're not contributing enough to get your full employer 401(k) match, raising your contribution is usually the best return available, and as the example shows, it costs less take-home pay than it saves. The [retirement calculator](/finance/retirement-calculator/) shows what those contributions grow into. Put part of each paycheck toward a target with the [savings goal calculator](/finance/savings-goal-calculator/), and if debt payments are squeezing your budget, compare snowball and avalanche plans with the [debt payoff calculator](/finance/debt-payoff-calculator/).
