## How to use this paycheck calculator

Pick salary or hourly, enter your pay, and the calculator shows your take-home pay per paycheck instantly. Filing status, pay frequency and your 401(k) percentage make the biggest difference to the result, so set those first.

1. **Pay type.** Enter an annual salary, or switch to **Hourly** and enter your wage and weekly hours. The results also show the equivalent hourly rate or annual salary.
2. **Paid and filing status.** Biweekly pay (26 paychecks) is the most common schedule in the US. Use the filing status you'll put on your tax return.
3. **401(k) contribution.** Enter the percentage you contribute to a traditional 401(k) or 403(b). Roth contributions are after-tax; leave them out here and subtract them from the result.
4. **State income tax.** Enter your effective state rate, or 0 if you live in a state with no income tax. State pages with exact brackets are linked below the calculator.
5. **Pre-tax benefits.** Under **More options**, add yearly health, dental and vision premiums plus any HSA or FSA payroll contributions.

## Worked example: a $68,000 salary paid every two weeks

A single filer earns $68,000 a year, is paid biweekly, puts 6% into a 401(k), pays $2,400 a year in pre-tax health premiums and lives in a state with a 4% flat income tax. Here's one paycheck:

| Line | Per paycheck | Per year |
|---|---|---|
| Gross pay | $2,615.38 | $68,000.00 |
| Federal income tax | −$200.09 | −$5,202.40 |
| Social Security (6.2%) | −$156.43 | −$4,067.20 |
| Medicare (1.45%) | −$36.58 | −$951.20 |
| State income tax (4%) | −$94.65 | −$2,460.80 |
| 401(k) (6%) | −$156.92 | −$4,080.00 |
| Health premiums | −$92.31 | −$2,400.00 |
| **Take-home pay** | **$1,878.40** | **$48,838.40** |

Federal taxable income is $68,000 − $4,080 − $2,400 − $16,100 = $45,420. The first $12,400 is taxed at 10% ($1,240) and the remaining $33,020 at 12% ($3,962.40), for $5,202.40 in federal tax. Even though this worker is "in the 12% bracket," their federal tax is only 7.7% of gross pay.

Social Security and Medicare are figured on $65,600, because the health premiums are exempt from FICA but the 401(k) contribution isn't. Without the 401(k), take-home pay would be $2,010.22, so saving $156.92 per paycheck only costs $131.82 of spending money.

## How take-home pay is calculated

Take-home pay is gross pay minus four things: pre-tax deductions, federal income tax, FICA (Social Security and Medicare) and state or local tax. Federal income tax is progressive: each slice of income is taxed at its own rate.

1. **Gross pay** = salary, or hourly wage × hours per week × 52.
2. **Federal taxable income** = gross − 401(k) − pre-tax benefits − standard deduction.
3. **Federal tax** = the sum of each bracket slice × its rate (10%, 12%, 22%, 24%, 32%, 35%, 37%).
4. **Social Security** = 6.2% of wages up to $184,500; **Medicare** = 1.45% of all wages, plus 0.9% above $200,000.
5. **Take-home pay** = gross − all of the above, divided by the number of paychecks.

This matches how IRS Publication 15-T's percentage method works for a W-4 with no adjustments: it annualizes your pay, applies the standard deduction and brackets, then spreads the tax over your pay periods. The **Show the math** panel above runs these steps with your numbers.

## How filing status changes your paycheck

Filing status sets your standard deduction and the width of each tax bracket, so it can change take-home pay by thousands of dollars on the same salary. On $90,000 with no 401(k) or state tax:

| Filing status | Federal income tax | Take-home per biweekly paycheck |
|---|---|---|
| Single | $10,970 | $2,774.81 |
| Head of household | $7,548 | $2,906.42 |
| Married filing jointly (one earner) | $6,440 | $2,949.04 |

If you and a spouse both work, withholding as "married filing jointly" on both W-4s without checking the box in Step 2 usually withholds too little, because each employer assumes its paycheck is the household's only income. The IRS Tax Withholding Estimator, or the two-earner worksheet on Form W-4, fixes that.

## Why one paycheck can differ from the next

Even with a steady salary, paychecks aren't always identical:

- **The Social Security cap.** Social Security tax stops once your wages for the year reach $184,500. On a $240,000 salary paid biweekly, that happens on the 20th paycheck, and each check after that is about $572 larger until January.
- **Three-paycheck months.** With biweekly pay there are 26 paychecks a year, so two months have three. Budgeting on two paychecks a month leaves those as a bonus.
- **Benefit changes.** New health premiums at open enrollment, or hitting a 401(k) contribution limit, change the amount withheld.
- **Bonuses and overtime.** Supplemental wages are often withheld at a flat 22% federal rate, which may be more or less than your actual bracket.

## Pre-tax deductions cost less than they look

Money that goes to a traditional 401(k), HSA or FSA comes out before income tax, so each dollar you set aside lowers your take-home pay by less than a dollar.

On a $75,000 salary (single, biweekly, no state tax):

- **Contributing 10% to a 401(k)** puts $7,500 a year into retirement savings but reduces take-home pay by only $5,850, from $61,592.50 to $55,742.50. The 22% federal bracket covers the rest.
- **Putting $4,400 into an HSA** through payroll, the 2026 limit for self-only coverage, reduces take-home pay by just $3,095.40, because HSA payroll contributions also skip Social Security and Medicare tax.

That's why increasing a pre-tax contribution is often the cheapest way to save: the tax break pays part of it for you. Roth contributions work the other way round. They don't lower today's tax, but withdrawals in retirement are tax-free.

## How to check your withholding

If your refunds are large, or you owe at tax time, your W-4 doesn't match your situation. To fix it:

1. Compare this calculator's federal tax with the federal withholding on a recent pay stub, scaled to a full year.
2. If withholding is much higher, you're lending the government money interest-free; if it's lower, you may owe and could face a penalty.
3. Submit a new Form W-4 to your employer. Use Step 3 for child tax credits, Step 4(a) for other income and Step 4(c) for extra withholding.
4. Recheck after a marriage, a new job, a second job or a baby.

## Key terms

- **Gross pay:** what you earn before anything is taken out.
- **Net pay (take-home pay):** what's deposited in your account.
- **Marginal tax rate:** the rate on your last dollar of income. A raise is taxed at this rate.
- **Effective tax rate:** total taxes divided by gross pay; usually far lower than the marginal rate.
- **FICA:** Social Security and Medicare payroll taxes, 7.65% combined for employees.
- **Section 125 (cafeteria plan):** lets health premiums, HSA and FSA contributions come out before income tax and FICA.

## How a raise changes your take-home pay

A raise is taxed at your marginal rate, not your average rate, and it never makes your whole paycheck smaller. Moving into a higher bracket only affects the dollars above the bracket's threshold.

In the worked example, a $5,000 raise from $68,000 to $73,000 increases take-home pay by $3,565.50 a year, or $137.13 per paycheck. That's 71% of the raise. The other 29% goes to the 12% federal bracket, 7.65% Social Security and Medicare, 4% state tax and the 6% 401(k) contribution, which still belongs to you.

Bonuses are often withheld differently: employers can withhold a flat 22% federal rate on supplemental wages up to $1 million. If that's more than your actual bracket, the difference comes back when you file your return.

## What to do with your result

Build your budget on take-home pay, not salary. A common rule of thumb is to keep housing under about 28% of gross income; check what that means for a home with the [mortgage affordability calculator](/finance/mortgage-affordability-calculator/), and see the full payment with the [mortgage calculator](/finance/mortgage-calculator/).

If you're not contributing enough to get your full employer 401(k) match, raising your contribution is usually the best return available, and as the example shows, it costs less take-home pay than it saves.
