## How to use this recipe scaler

1. **Recipe makes.** The number of servings, pieces or batches the original recipe produces.
2. **You want.** How many you'd like to make. Fractions work too: 0.5 batches halves a recipe.
3. **Ingredients.** Paste the ingredient list, one ingredient per line, each starting with its amount. Whole numbers (3 eggs), fractions (1/3 cup), mixed numbers (1 1/2 tsp), decimals (0.75 cup) and fraction characters (½, ¼, ¾, ⅓, ⅔, ⅛) are all understood. Lines without a number, such as "salt to taste", are kept as they are.

The result shows the scale factor and a table of every ingredient, original and scaled, rounded to the nearest kitchen fraction. Amounts that had to be rounded noticeably are marked ≈.

## The formula

**Scale factor = servings you want ÷ servings the recipe makes**

**New amount = original amount × scale factor**

The calculator then rounds each new amount to the nearest fraction you can actually measure: eighths (1/8, 1/4, 3/8, 1/2 and so on), sixths and thirds. It never rounds a real amount down to nothing; anything tiny becomes 1/8.

## Worked example: 4 servings to 6

A recipe for 4 servings scaled to 6 has a factor of 6 ÷ 4 = **1.5**.

| Ingredient | Original | × 1.5 | To measure |
|---|---|---|---|
| Flour | 2 cups | 3 | 3 cups |
| Baking powder | 1 1/2 tsp | 2.25 | 2 1/4 tsp |
| Salt | 1/2 tsp | 0.75 | 3/4 tsp |
| Eggs | 3 | 4.5 | 4 1/2 eggs |
| Sugar | 1/3 cup | 0.5 | 1/2 cup |
| Milk | 1 cup | 1.5 | 1 1/2 cups |

For the half egg, beat a fifth egg and add about half of it, roughly 1½ tablespoons.

## Halving and doubling

The same recipe halved (factor 0.5) and doubled (factor 2):

| Ingredient | Original | Halved | Doubled |
|---|---|---|---|
| Flour | 2 cups | 1 cup | 4 cups |
| Baking powder | 1 1/2 tsp | 3/4 tsp | 3 tsp (1 tbsp) |
| Salt | 1/2 tsp | 1/4 tsp | 1 tsp |
| Eggs | 3 | 1 1/2 | 6 |
| Sugar | 1/3 cup | 1/6 cup | 2/3 cup |
| Milk | 1 cup | 1/2 cup | 2 cups |

A sixth of a cup isn't on most measuring sets, but it's easy to make with spoons: **1/6 cup = 2 tablespoons + 2 teaspoons**.

## Kitchen measurement equivalents

When a scaled amount doesn't match your measuring cups, convert it to spoons:

| Amount | Equals |
|---|---|
| 1 tablespoon | 3 teaspoons |
| 1/8 cup | 2 tablespoons |
| 1/6 cup | 2 tablespoons + 2 teaspoons |
| 1/4 cup | 4 tablespoons |
| 1/3 cup | 5 tablespoons + 1 teaspoon |
| 1/2 cup | 8 tablespoons |
| 1 cup | 16 tablespoons (48 teaspoons) |

In metric terms, a U.S. cup is about 236.6 mL, a tablespoon about 14.8 mL and a teaspoon about 4.9 mL. Recipes from other countries may use different cups and spoons; an Australian tablespoon, for example, is 20 mL, so check the recipe's origin.

## What doesn't scale in a straight line

Ingredients usually scale exactly, but a few other things don't:

- **Pan size.** Doubling a cake batter needs about twice the pan area, not a pan twice as wide. An 8-inch square pan is 64 square inches; a 9 × 13-inch pan is 117, close to double. An 8-inch round is about 50 square inches and a 9-inch round about 64.
- **Baking time.** Batter in a deeper pan takes longer to bake through; the same depth in more pans takes about the same time. Start checking early.
- **Leavening and spices.** When multiplying a recipe several times over, some bakers use a little less than the full amount of baking soda, baking powder, salt and strong spices. For doubling, scaling exactly is usually fine.
- **Evaporation.** Soups, sauces and stews in a wider pot lose liquid faster, and a larger batch in the same pot loses it more slowly. Adjust the liquid by eye.
- **Pan sizes for frying and roasting.** Crowding a pan steams food instead of browning it, so cook larger batches in several rounds.

## Scaling to a different pan

Sometimes the goal isn't a number of servings but a pan you have. Use the pans' areas as the "servings": enter the original pan's area as **Recipe makes** and your pan's area as **You want**.

For a brownie recipe written for an 8-inch square pan (64 square inches) that you want to bake in a 9 × 13-inch pan (117 square inches), the factor is 117 ÷ 64 = **1.83**. Two cups of flour becomes about 3 2/3 cups, 1 cup of sugar about 1 7/8 cups, and 2 eggs about 3 2/3 eggs; in practice, use 4 eggs or 3 large eggs plus a yolk. The batter ends up the same depth, so the baking time stays close to the original. Check a few minutes early, since a larger pan holds heat differently.

For round pans, the area is 3.14 × radius²: a 9-inch round (radius 4.5 inches) is about 64 square inches, almost the same as an 8-inch square.

## Worked example: a bigger batch of cookies

A cookie recipe makes 12 cookies and you need 30, a factor of **2.5**:

- 2 1/4 cups flour becomes **5 5/8 cups**.
- 1 tsp baking soda becomes **2 1/2 tsp**.
- 1 cup butter becomes **2 1/2 cups** (5 sticks of 1/2 cup each).
- 3/4 cup sugar becomes **1 7/8 cups**, which you can measure as 1 3/4 cups plus 2 tablespoons.
- 2 eggs becomes **5 eggs**.

Cookies scale well because each one bakes the same way regardless of how many you make. Bake them in batches on cool baking sheets, and keep the cookie size the same as the original so the baking time doesn't change.

## Yeast doughs

Bread and pizza doughs scale well by weight. Yeast amounts can usually be scaled exactly for doubling. For much larger batches, many bakers reduce the yeast slightly, since a big mass of dough holds warmth and ferments faster. Kneading time grows with batch size, and a home mixer has a maximum dough capacity, so very large batches are often easier made in two lots.

## Weighing instead of measuring

For baking, weighing ingredients is more accurate than cups, and it makes scaling easier: multiply the grams by the scale factor and you're done, with no awkward fractions. A cup of flour can weigh anything from about 120 to 150 grams depending on how it's scooped, which is often why a scaled recipe turns out differently. If your recipe gives weights, paste them in and the calculator scales them the same way; a decimal like 187.5 g is easy to weigh on a kitchen scale.

## Liquid and dry measures

U.S. recipes use the same cup for liquid and dry ingredients, but different tools give the best results. Use a clear **liquid measuring cup** with a spout, read at eye level, for water, milk and oil. Use **dry measuring cups** filled to the top and leveled with a knife for flour, sugar and other dry ingredients. When you scale up, a larger liquid measure (2 or 4 cups) saves repeated filling, and when you scale down, a set of small spoons, including 1/8 teaspoon, makes tiny amounts accurate. Sticky ingredients such as honey slide out more easily from a lightly oiled measure.

## Writing down your scaled recipe

Once you've scaled a recipe you'll make again, write the new amounts down, along with the pan size, oven temperature and actual baking time that worked. Small changes such as an extra minute in the oven or a little less salt are easy to forget, and they're what turns a scaled recipe into a reliable one.

## Scaling for a crowd

When cooking for many people, decide first how many servings you need, allowing a little extra. Then:

- **Scale in stages.** Making a recipe four times is often easier as two double batches, especially for baking.
- **Check your equipment.** A pot, mixer bowl or oven may not hold a large batch at once.
- **Prep in advance.** Large quantities take longer to chop, mix and cook, so plan the time as well as the ingredients.
- **Watch the seasoning.** Taste and adjust salt and spices at the end rather than adding the full scaled amount at once.

## Tips for clean results

- **Start each line with the amount,** like "1 1/2 cups flour", not "flour, 1 1/2 cups".
- **Use a space in mixed numbers:** "1 1/2", not "11/2", which reads as eleven halves.
- **Leave ranges for later.** For "2 to 3 cloves garlic", enter "2 1/2 cloves garlic" or scale it by eye.
- **Round eggs sensibly.** For 4 1/2 eggs, use 4 large eggs plus a little extra liquid, or 5 eggs in a forgiving recipe like a frittata.

## Key terms

- **Scale factor:** the number every ingredient is multiplied by.
- **Yield:** the number of servings or pieces a recipe makes.
- **Leavening:** ingredients such as baking powder, baking soda and yeast that make baked goods rise.
- **Baker's percentage:** each ingredient's weight as a percentage of the flour's weight.
