## How to use this debt-to-income ratio calculator

Enter your income and monthly payments, and both ratios update as you type. Choose a loan type to see whether you meet its guideline and how large a housing payment would still fit.

1. **Gross annual income.** Before tax, for everyone on the application. Include steady income such as salary, regular overtime, self-employment profit and benefits.
2. **Monthly housing payment.** If you rent, use your rent. If you're applying for a mortgage, use the expected payment including property tax, homeowners insurance, mortgage insurance and HOA dues; the [mortgage calculator](/finance/mortgage-calculator/) gives that figure.
3. **Other debts.** The minimum required payment on each debt, not what you choose to pay.
4. **Compare with.** Conventional, FHA, VA or USDA guidelines.

## Worked example: a couple applying for a mortgage

A couple earns $96,000 a year together, which is $8,000 a month. The home they want would cost $2,100 a month with taxes and insurance. They also pay $380 on a car loan, $220 on student loans and $150 in credit card minimums.

- **Total monthly debts:** $2,100 + $380 + $220 + $150 = **$2,850**
- **Front-end ratio:** $2,100 ÷ $8,000 = **26.25%**
- **Back-end ratio (DTI):** $2,850 ÷ $8,000 = **35.63%**

Both ratios fit the conventional 28/36 guideline. The largest housing payment that keeps them at 36% is $8,000 × 36% − $750 = **$2,130**, so they have only $30 a month of room. Under FHA's 43% limit, the same income would support up to $2,690 for housing, although FHA's 31% housing guideline would cap it nearer $2,480.

Paying off the credit card balance before applying drops their DTI to 33.75%; paying off the car instead drops it to 30.88%.

## The formulas in plain English

**Front-end ratio = monthly housing payment ÷ gross monthly income × 100**

**Back-end ratio (DTI) = all monthly debt payments ÷ gross monthly income × 100**

When people say "DTI", they usually mean the back-end ratio. Lenders calculate it with the new housing payment included, so a DTI based on your current rent can change once a mortgage replaces it. The **Show the math** panel above runs through every step with your numbers.

## Lender guidelines by loan type

| Loan type | Housing (front-end) | All debts (back-end) | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Conventional | 28% | 36% | Fannie Mae allows up to 45% with manual underwriting and 50% through Desktop Underwriter |
| FHA | 31% | 43% | Higher ratios possible with compensating factors such as cash reserves |
| VA | none | 41% | Also requires enough residual income after all expenses |
| USDA | 29% | 41% | Rural housing program with income limits |

These are guidelines, not hard caps. Lenders weigh DTI together with your credit score, down payment, savings and job history, so a strong file can be approved above them and a weak one can be declined below them.

## How lenders count specific debts

Mortgage underwriters don't simply copy the balances from your credit report. A few rules often change the number they use:

- **Student loans in deferment or on income-driven plans.** If your credit report shows a $0 payment, lenders usually still count something. Conventional and FHA guidelines use either the documented income-driven payment or a percentage of the balance (FHA uses 0.5% of the outstanding balance when no payment is reported). On a $40,000 balance, that's $200 a month added to your debts.
- **Credit cards.** The minimum payment on your statement counts even if you pay the card in full every month. Paying down a balance before you apply can lower the minimum.
- **Loans close to paid off.** Conventional loans can usually leave out an installment loan with ten or fewer payments remaining, unless the payment is large enough to affect your ability to pay.
- **Co-signed loans.** A loan you co-signed counts as your debt unless you can show the other borrower has made the payments for the past 12 months.
- **Things that don't count:** utilities, phone bills, groceries, car and health insurance premiums, and income taxes.

Because of these rules, the DTI a lender calculates can be a few points higher than your own estimate. Leave yourself a margin below the guideline.

## How much debt fits your income?

To turn the ratios into dollars, multiply your gross monthly income by each limit:

| Gross annual income | Monthly income | Housing at 28% | All debts at 36% | All debts at 43% |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| $50,000 | $4,166.67 | $1,166.67 | $1,500.00 | $1,791.67 |
| $75,000 | $6,250.00 | $1,750.00 | $2,250.00 | $2,687.50 |
| $100,000 | $8,333.33 | $2,333.33 | $3,000.00 | $3,583.33 |
| $150,000 | $12,500.00 | $3,500.00 | $4,500.00 | $5,375.00 |

Every dollar of existing debt payments reduces what's left for housing under the back-end limit. On $75,000, a $500 car payment leaves at most $1,750 for housing at 36%, the same as the 28% housing limit; add a $250 student loan and the housing allowance falls to $1,500.

## What income lenders count

The income side of the ratio is just as important, and lenders are strict about what they'll use. Generally, they want income that's documented, stable and likely to continue:

- **Salary and hourly wages,** verified with recent pay stubs and W-2s.
- **Overtime, bonuses and commissions,** usually only if you have a two-year history of receiving them, averaged over that period.
- **Self-employment income,** typically the average net profit from your last two years of tax returns, after business expenses. Large write-offs lower your qualifying income.
- **Rental income,** often counted at about 75% of the rent to allow for vacancies and upkeep.
- **Alimony, child support and benefits** such as Social Security or disability, if they're documented and expected to continue for at least three more years.

A new job in the same field is usually fine, but a recent switch from salary to commission or self-employment can make income harder to count.

## How to lower your DTI before applying

If your ratio is above the guideline for the loan you want, a few months of preparation can make the difference:

1. **Pay off small debts completely.** Eliminating a $150 minimum lowers the ratio more than spreading the same money across several balances. In the example, clearing the credit card cut the couple's DTI from 35.63% to 33.75%.
2. **Don't open new credit.** A new car loan or store card before you apply adds a payment and a hard inquiry.
3. **Pay down credit card balances** to reduce the minimum payments that appear on your credit report.
4. **Add a co-borrower's income,** if a spouse or partner will be on the loan. Their debts count too, so check both.
5. **Make sure all your income is documented,** including side income you can show on tax returns.
6. **Consider a less expensive home or a larger down payment,** which lowers the housing payment itself.

## DTI beyond mortgages

Debt-to-income ratio isn't only for home loans:

- **Auto loans and personal loans.** Lenders check DTI too, often with limits somewhere in the 40% to 50% range, and they include the new loan's payment. A high ratio can mean a higher rate or a smaller loan.
- **Credit cards.** Card issuers must consider your ability to pay under the CARD Act, so income and existing debts affect your credit limit.
- **Renting an apartment.** Landlords use a simpler version: many want your gross income to be at least three times the monthly rent, which is the same as rent taking no more than about 33% of income. Some also look at your other debts.
- **Your own budget.** Even if no one is checking, a back-end ratio above about 36% usually means debt payments are crowding out savings. Watching the ratio fall as you pay debts down is a useful measure of progress.

## DTI is one piece of the approval

Lenders weigh DTI alongside your credit score, down payment and savings. A high credit score and several months of payments in reserve can offset a ratio slightly above the guideline, while a low score can make a lender stricter even within it. Strengthening any one of these makes the others matter less. If you're unsure where you stand, a lender's pre-approval checks all of them together, and it's free.

## Key terms

- **Gross monthly income:** income before taxes and deductions, divided by 12.
- **Front-end ratio:** the share of income going to housing costs.
- **Back-end ratio:** the share going to all required debt payments, including housing.
- **Compensating factors:** strengths such as cash reserves or a high credit score that let a lender accept a higher ratio.
- **Residual income:** what's left each month after debts, taxes and living costs, used by VA loans as a second test.

If your DTI is above the guideline for the loan you want, you have three levers: a smaller housing payment, fewer debt payments or more income.
