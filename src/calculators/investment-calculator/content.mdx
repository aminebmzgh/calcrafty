## How to use this investment calculator

Enter a starting amount, a monthly contribution, an expected return and a time frame. The projected balance, the share that comes from growth and the value in today's dollars all update as you type.

1. **Starting amount.** What's already invested, or 0.
2. **Monthly contribution.** What you'll add each month, for example through a 401(k) or an automatic transfer to a brokerage account.
3. **Expected annual return.** Use an average you're comfortable planning with, after fund fees.
4. **Years to grow.** How long until you need the money.
5. **More options.** Raise contributions each year to match pay raises, and set inflation. The inflation default is the latest 12-month change in the Consumer Price Index from the Bureau of Labor Statistics.

## Worked example: $15,000 plus $400 a month for 25 years

A 35-year-old has $15,000 invested, adds $400 a month, raises the contribution 3% each year and plans with a 6.5% average annual return and 3% inflation.

- **Projected value at 60:** **$472,116.99**
- **Total contributed:** $190,004.47
- **Investment growth:** $282,112.52, which is 59.8% of the final balance
- **Monthly contribution in year 25:** $813.12
- **In today's dollars:** **$225,485.70**

Three what-ifs show what drives the result:

| Change | Projected value |
|---|---|
| As planned | $472,116.99 |
| No yearly increase in contributions | $375,377.58 |
| Returns average 4.5% instead of 6.5% | $347,368.51 |
| Invest for 15 years instead of 25 | $184,965.19 |

Cutting the time in half cuts the result by more than half, because the last years of compounding add the most dollars.

## How the projection is calculated in plain English

Each month, the balance earns one month of return and then the contribution is added:

**balance = balance × (1 + r ÷ 12) + contribution**

At the start of each new year, the contribution rises by the percentage you chose. After the final month, the projected balance is converted to today's dollars by dividing by (1 + inflation)^years. With no contributions this reduces to the classic compound growth formula, A = P(1 + r ÷ 12)^(12t).

The projection uses a steady average return. Real markets don't move in a straight line, so treat the result as a planning estimate, and test lower returns too.

## Key terms

- **Rate of return:** the yearly percentage gain on an investment, including dividends reinvested.
- **Compounding:** growth earning further growth.
- **Real vs nominal value:** nominal is the future dollar amount; real is what it's worth in today's prices.
- **Expense ratio:** a fund's yearly fee as a percentage of your balance; it comes straight out of returns.
- **Dollar-cost averaging:** investing a fixed amount on a schedule regardless of market prices.

## How fees and returns change the result

Small differences in yearly return compound into large differences in the final balance. That's true whether the difference comes from markets or from costs you control, such as fund fees.

Using the same plan as the example ($15,000 to start, $400 a month rising 3% a year, 25 years), here's what happens as the net return falls:

| Net annual return | Value after 25 years |
|---|---|
| 6.5% | $472,116.99 |
| 6.0% | $436,318.05 |
| 5.5% | $403,804.84 |

A fund charging 0.5% a year more than a cheaper alternative effectively lowers your return by about that much. Here it costs roughly $35,800 over 25 years, about 8% of the final balance. A 1% difference costs more than $68,000.

Ways to keep more of your return:

- **Check expense ratios.** Every fund lists its expense ratio. Broad index funds often charge a small fraction of a percent; some actively managed funds and advisory wrappers charge 1% or more a year.
- **Use tax-advantaged accounts first.** A 401(k), IRA or HSA lets growth compound without yearly taxes. In a taxable account, dividends and realized gains are taxed along the way, which slows compounding.
- **Diversify.** Spreading money across many companies, sectors and countries reduces the chance that one bad investment derails your plan.
- **Match risk to your timeline.** Money needed within a few years usually belongs in safer assets; long-term money can ride out market swings.
- **Avoid selling in a panic.** Markets have historically recovered from downturns over long periods, but investors who sell after a drop lock in the loss and often miss the recovery.

**Rebalance occasionally.** Over time, the parts of a portfolio that grow fastest take up a bigger share and change its risk. Many investors rebalance once a year, or use target-date funds that do it automatically, to keep the mix they chose.

## What to do with your result

If the projection is meant for retirement, test it with the [retirement calculator](/finance/retirement-calculator/), which adds your age, income needs and withdrawals. To find the monthly amount needed for a specific target, use the [savings goal calculator](/finance/savings-goal-calculator/). For money you'll need within a few years, a savings account or CD is usually more suitable; model it with the [compound interest calculator](/finance/compound-interest-calculator/).

Raising contributions with each raise is one of the easiest wins. Check what a 1% bump costs per paycheck with the [paycheck calculator](/finance/paycheck-calculator/); because 401(k) contributions are pre-tax, it costs less take-home pay than you'd expect.
