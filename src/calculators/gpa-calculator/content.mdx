## How to use this GPA calculator

List your courses with their credits and letter grades, and your GPA updates as you go. The calculator shows both the unweighted GPA on the standard 4.0 scale and a weighted GPA that rewards Honors and AP/IB courses.

1. **Courses.** Enter a name (optional), the credits or credit hours, and the grade. Use **Add a course** for more rows, up to 15 per term.
2. **Level.** Choose **Honors** or **AP / IB / college** for weighted courses. Regular courses get no bonus.
3. **Previous GPA.** Under **More options**, enter your cumulative GPA and credits so far to see your new cumulative GPA.

In high school, a full-year class is often worth 1 credit; in college, most classes are 3 or 4 credit hours. What matters is using the same units for every course.

## Worked example: a five-course semester

A student takes five courses this semester:

| Course | Credits | Grade | Points | Quality points |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| English | 3 | A− | 3.7 | 11.1 |
| Calculus (AP) | 4 | B+ | 3.3 | 13.2 |
| Biology (Honors) | 4 | A | 4.0 | 16.0 |
| History | 3 | B | 3.0 | 9.0 |
| Spanish | 3 | A | 4.0 | 12.0 |
| **Total** | **17** | | | **61.3** |

- **Unweighted GPA:** 61.3 ÷ 17 = **3.606**, or 3.61
- **Weighted GPA:** Calculus earns +1.0 (4.3) and Biology +0.5 (4.5), adding 4 × 1.0 + 4 × 0.5 = 6 points: 67.3 ÷ 17 = **3.959**, or 3.96
- **Cumulative GPA:** with a 3.4 GPA over 45 earlier credits, (3.4 × 45 + 61.3) ÷ (45 + 17) = 214.3 ÷ 62 = **3.456**

Notice that the 4-credit B+ pulls the unweighted GPA down more than a 3-credit B+ would, because GPA is weighted by credits.

## The GPA formula in plain English

**GPA = Σ(grade points × credits) ÷ Σ credits**

Grade points on the standard 4.0 scale:

| Grade | Points | Grade | Points |
|---|---|---|---|
| A+ / A | 4.0 | C | 2.0 |
| A− | 3.7 | C− | 1.7 |
| B+ | 3.3 | D+ | 1.3 |
| B | 3.0 | D | 1.0 |
| B− | 2.7 | D− | 0.7 |
| C+ | 2.3 | F | 0.0 |

For the weighted GPA, the calculator adds 0.5 for Honors and 1.0 for AP, IB or college-level courses before multiplying by credits. An F stays at 0 regardless of level. The **Show the math** panel lists every course's points with your numbers.

## How to raise your GPA

- **Focus on high-credit courses.** A grade in a 4-credit course moves your GPA more than the same grade in a 1-credit course.
- **Early terms matter most for your cumulative GPA.** Each new term is a smaller share of your total credits, so the same improvement moves the average less over time.
- **Check your school's retake policy.** Some schools replace a failed or low grade when you retake the course; others average both attempts.
- **Plan with the calculator.** Enter expected grades for the coming term to see what you need to reach a target cumulative GPA.

## What grades do you need to reach a target GPA?

To work out the average you need in upcoming courses, use:

**GPA needed = (target × total credits after this term − current quality points) ÷ new credits**

For a student with a 3.0 GPA over 60 credits:

| New credits | Target 3.1 | Target 3.2 | Target 3.3 | Target 3.5 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 15 (one term) | 3.50 | 4.00 | 4.50 | 5.50 |
| 30 (two terms) | 3.30 | 3.60 | 3.90 | 4.50 |
| 45 (three terms) | 3.23 | 3.47 | 3.70 | 4.17 |

Anything above 4.0 is impossible on an unweighted scale, so reaching 3.3 needs at least three more terms of A-range work, and 3.5 isn't reachable within 45 credits. The more credits you've already earned, the slower your cumulative GPA moves, which is why the first year counts so much. To check a plan, enter your expected grades in the calculator along with your current GPA and credits.

## Converting percentage grades to letter grades

Many US schools use this common scale, although the cut-offs vary, so check your syllabus or school handbook:

| Percentage | Letter | Points |
|---|---|---|
| 93–100 | A | 4.0 |
| 90–92 | A− | 3.7 |
| 87–89 | B+ | 3.3 |
| 83–86 | B | 3.0 |
| 80–82 | B− | 2.7 |
| 77–79 | C+ | 2.3 |
| 73–76 | C | 2.0 |
| 70–72 | C− | 1.7 |
| 67–69 | D+ | 1.3 |
| 63–66 | D | 1.0 |
| 60–62 | D− | 0.7 |
| Below 60 | F | 0.0 |

Some schools don't use plus and minus grades at all, so a 91 and a 98 both count as an A worth 4.0.

## Pass/fail, withdrawals and incompletes

Not every grade affects your GPA. At most schools:

- **Pass/fail courses** earn credit toward graduation, but a pass doesn't count in the GPA. A failing grade sometimes does, so check the policy.
- **Withdrawals (W)** after the drop deadline appear on the transcript but carry no grade points.
- **Incompletes (I)** don't count until they're replaced by a final grade, and can turn into an F if the work isn't finished in time.
- **Transfer credits** often count toward your degree but not your GPA at the new school.

Leave these courses out of the calculator, or enter them only once they have a letter grade. Retaken courses depend on your school's policy: if the new grade replaces the old one, enter only the new grade; if both count, enter the course twice.

## GPA on other scales

The 4.0 scale is the most common in the US, but not the only one:

- **4.3 scale.** Some colleges give an A+ 4.3 points instead of 4.0, so a perfect GPA can exceed 4.0.
- **5.0 scale.** Many high schools report weighted GPAs on a 5.0 scale, where an A in an AP or IB course earns 5 points.
- **100-point or percentage scale.** Some high schools report an average percentage rather than a GPA.

Converting between scales isn't exact, because each school maps grades differently. If an application asks for your GPA "on a 4.0 scale," recalculate it from your letter grades with this calculator rather than scaling a weighted number down, which can understate or overstate your record.

## What counts as a good GPA?

It depends on the goal. A 3.0 is a solid B average and the minimum many graduate programs, scholarships and honor societies ask for. Many colleges require at least a 2.0 to stay in good academic standing, and some majors set higher thresholds for admission into the program. Selective colleges and graduate schools look at GPA alongside course difficulty, test scores where they're used, and the trend over time: steady improvement from a weak first year is often viewed favorably. Employers who ask for a GPA, usually for internships and first jobs, commonly use 3.0 as a cut-off.

## Semester credits vs quarter credits

Schools on a quarter system run three main terms a year instead of two, so each quarter credit represents less class time. A common conversion is **1 quarter credit = 2/3 of a semester credit**, so a 4-quarter-credit course equals about 2.67 semester credits. This matters when transferring between schools: the GPA itself stays the same, but the number of credits you bring with you shrinks when moving from quarters to semesters. Ask the new school's registrar how they'll convert your credits before you enroll.

## Weighted GPA policies differ by school

There's no single national rule for weighting. Common approaches include:

- **+0.5 for Honors and +1.0 for AP or IB**, the convention this calculator uses.
- **A 5.0 scale for AP only**, where an A in an AP course is 5.0 and Honors receive no bonus.
- **Weighting capped** at a certain number of advanced courses, or applied only in grades 10 to 12.
- **No weighting at all**, with course rigor shown separately on the transcript.

Colleges know this, which is why many recalculate an applicant's GPA from the transcript using their own method, often counting only core academic subjects (English, math, science, social studies and world languages) and their own weighting. Some ignore weighting entirely and judge rigor by the courses you took.

College GPAs are usually unweighted, but they're affected by credit hours: a 4-credit lab science counts for more than a 1-credit seminar. Many colleges also require a minimum GPA, often 2.0, to stay in good academic standing and keep federal financial aid under satisfactory academic progress rules. If you're near a cut-off, use the calculator to work out the grades you need this term.

To plan ahead, enter your current courses with the grades you realistically expect, then try a few variations. Seeing how one grade changes the result makes it easier to decide where extra study time will pay off most, and whether a target such as a 3.5 is within reach this term.

## Key terms

- **Grade point average (GPA):** the credit-weighted average of your grade points.
- **Quality points:** grade points multiplied by credits for one course.
- **Credit hours:** the weight of a course, usually based on weekly class time.
- **Weighted GPA:** a GPA that adds bonus points for advanced courses.
- **Cumulative GPA:** your GPA across all terms so far.
