## Seven strategies on the same loan

To compare them fairly, every strategy below is applied to the same loan: **$300,000 at 6.5% for 30 years**, with a monthly payment of $1,896.20 and $382,636.71 of interest if you just make the scheduled payments.

| Strategy | Extra money | Paid off in | Interest saved |
|---|---|---|---|
| $100 extra every month | $1,200 a year | 26 years | $60,995.81 |
| One-twelfth extra each month ($158.02) | $1,896 a year | 24 years 2 months | $87,259.03 |
| One extra payment a year ($1,896.20) | $1,896 a year | 24 years 4 months | $83,986.01 |
| $200 extra every month | $2,400 a year | 23 years 1 month | $103,450.19 |
| $5,000 extra every year | $5,000 a year | 19 years | $158,005.19 |
| $25,000 lump sum at the start | $25,000 once | 23 years 10 months | $116,534.82 |
| $717.13 extra a month (15-year target) | $8,606 a year | 15 years | $212,239.79 |

Every figure is calculated month by month, with interest rounded to the cent as lenders do. You can run your own loan through the [mortgage payoff calculator](/finance/mortgage-payoff-calculator/), which starts from your current balance, or see the full schedule in the [amortization calculator](/finance/amortization-calculator/).

## 1. Pay extra every month

The simplest strategy is to add a fixed amount to every payment and tell your servicer to apply it to principal. Each extra dollar reduces the balance immediately, so next month's interest is smaller and more of your regular payment goes to principal. The effect compounds over time: $100 a month saves $60,995.81, and $200 a month saves $103,450.19. Doubling the extra from $100 to $200 raises the savings by about 70%.

It works best when it's automatic. Set your payment to the higher amount, and you won't miss the money after a month or two.

## 2. Make biweekly payments, or the equivalent

Paying half your payment every two weeks adds up to 26 half-payments, or 13 full payments, a year. The 13th payment goes to principal. You get almost exactly the same result by adding one-twelfth of a payment each month: $158.02 on a $1,896.20 payment. That pays off the example loan in 24 years 2 months and saves $87,259.03.

Avoid companies that charge a setup or per-payment fee for biweekly plans; you can do the same thing for free. Also check how your servicer handles a half-payment: some hold it until the second half arrives, which removes most of the benefit.

## 3. Make one extra payment a year

If a monthly increase is hard, a single extra payment each year, for example from a tax refund or an annual bonus, does nearly as well: one extra $1,896.20 payment a year saves $83,986.01 and cuts more than five and a half years. It saves slightly less than the monthly version because the money arrives later in each year.

A larger annual amount, such as $5,000 a year, pays the loan off in 19 years and saves $158,005.19.

## 4. Put a lump sum toward principal

Windfalls such as an inheritance, a bonus or the proceeds of a sale can make a big dent. A $25,000 lump sum at the start of the loan saves $116,534.82 and cuts the loan by more than 6 years. Timing matters a lot: the same $10,000 saves $53,602.60 at the start of the loan but $24,871.60 if you wait ten years, because there's less interest left to save later on.

Before using a lump sum, make sure you'll still have an emergency fund afterward. Money paid into a house can only be reached again by selling, refinancing or borrowing against it.

## 5. Recast after a lump sum

A recast doesn't pay the loan off faster by itself. After a large principal payment, the servicer recalculates your monthly payment on the lower balance over the remaining term, usually for a fee of a few hundred dollars. You keep the interest saved by the lump sum and get a lower required payment. If you then keep paying the old amount, the difference goes to principal and you finish early, with the option to drop back to the lower payment if you need to. Not every loan can be recast; government-backed loans such as FHA and VA loans usually can't.

## 6. Refinance to a shorter term

A 15-year mortgage typically has a lower rate than a 30-year one, so you save on two fronts. On $300,000 at 6.5%, a 15-year term costs $2,613.32 a month and $170,398.28 in interest. If the 15-year rate is 5.875%, the payment is $2,511.36 and the interest $152,043.52, less than half the 30-year total.

The catch is that the higher payment is required every month, even in a bad year, and refinancing has closing costs, typically 2% to 5% of the loan. The [refinance calculator](/finance/refinance-calculator/) shows the break-even point. If rates are higher now than on your current loan, extra payments on the existing loan are almost always better.

## 7. Round up and use raises

Small habits add up. Rounding a $1,896.20 payment up to $2,000 adds $103.80 a month, worth more than $60,000 over the loan. When you get a raise, sending part of it to the mortgage keeps your spending the same while the loan shrinks faster. Some people also send the amount of a paid-off car loan or child care to the mortgage once those costs end.

## Setting a target: mortgage-free by retirement

Many people want the mortgage gone before they stop working, so retirement income doesn't have to cover a house payment. Work backward from the date: if you took out the example loan and plan to retire in 20 years, paying **$340.52 extra a month** finishes it in exactly 20 years. For 15 years, it's $717.13 a month. Enter your own balance, rate, payment and target in the [mortgage payoff calculator](/finance/mortgage-payoff-calculator/) to get your number, and revisit it each year as your income and plans change.

## What to check before paying extra

- **Emergency fund.** Keep three to six months of expenses in cash first; the [emergency fund calculator](/finance/emergency-fund-calculator/) helps set a number.
- **Higher-interest debt.** Paying off a credit card at 22% beats prepaying a mortgage at 6.5%. The [debt payoff calculator](/finance/debt-payoff-calculator/) shows the order.
- **Retirement match.** An employer 401(k) match is an immediate return that prepaying can't beat.
- **Your rate against safe savings.** If a high-yield savings account or Treasury bills pay more than your mortgage rate after tax, saving the cash can come out ahead. If your rate is higher, prepaying usually wins.
- **Prepayment penalties.** Most mortgages have none. Where federal rules allow one, it applies only in the first three years and is capped. Check your note.

## Making sure extra payments count

Tell your servicer that extra money is for principal. Most payment forms and apps have a separate box for "additional principal". Without it, some servicers treat the money as an early payment of next month's bill, which doesn't reduce interest. Check your next statement: the principal balance should fall by the extra amount. Keep records of extra payments, and check the payoff figure your servicer quotes when you're close to the end.

## Getting rid of PMI sooner

If you put down less than 20% on a conventional loan, you probably pay private mortgage insurance. Under federal law, you can ask the servicer to cancel it once the balance reaches 80% of the home's original value, and it ends automatically at 78%, as long as you're current on payments. Extra payments reach those points sooner, which removes a monthly cost on top of the interest saved. FHA mortgage insurance follows different rules and often lasts for the life of the loan unless you refinance.

## When paying off early isn't the best move

Paying off a mortgage early is a safe, guaranteed return, but it isn't always the best use of money:

- **Very low rates.** On a 3% mortgage, cash savings or investments may earn more.
- **Short time in the home.** If you'll sell in a few years, extra payments come back to you at the sale, but they don't earn more than your rate in the meantime.
- **Tight cash flow.** If extra payments would leave you relying on credit cards in an emergency, keep the cash.
- **Retirement saving behind schedule.** Tax-advantaged retirement accounts often matter more, especially with an employer match.
- **Itemized mortgage interest.** If you itemize deductions, part of your interest may be tax-deductible, which lowers the loan's real cost a little. Most households take the standard deduction, so for them the full rate is the real cost.

A balanced approach many people use is to split spare money: some to savings or retirement, some to the mortgage.

## Key terms

- **Principal:** what you still owe on the loan.
- **Extra principal payment:** money paid on top of the scheduled payment that goes directly to the balance.
- **Biweekly payments:** paying half the monthly payment every two weeks, 26 times a year.
- **Recast:** recalculating the payment after a large principal payment, keeping the end date.
- **PMI:** private mortgage insurance, charged on conventional loans with less than 20% equity.
- **Prepayment penalty:** a fee for paying off early, now rare on mortgages.
