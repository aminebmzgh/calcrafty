**Short answer:** the median U.S. household net worth is about **$39,000 under age 35, $135,000 at 35 to 44, $247,000 at 45 to 54, $364,000 at 55 to 64 and $410,000 at 65 to 74**, according to the Federal Reserve's 2022 Survey of Consumer Finances. Being above the median for your age is a good sign, but a "good" net worth is really one that is rising each year and on track to support the retirement you want. Income-based rules of thumb, covered below, are a better personal yardstick than a national median.

## Median net worth by age

The Federal Reserve's Survey of Consumer Finances (SCF), run every three years, is the most authoritative source on U.S. household wealth. Net worth is everything a household owns, including home equity and retirement accounts, minus all its debts. The 2022 results, rounded:

| Age of head of household | Median net worth |
|---|---|
| Under 35 | $39,000 |
| 35 to 44 | $135,000 |
| 45 to 54 | $247,000 |
| 55 to 64 | $364,000 |
| 65 to 74 | $410,000 |
| 75 and over | $335,000 |
| All households | about $193,000 |

The pattern is consistent from survey to survey: net worth rises through working life as people pay down mortgages and build retirement savings, peaks around ages 65 to 74, then falls as retirees spend down their savings. The next edition of the survey covers 2025.

## Median vs average: why the average misleads

The **median** is the middle household: half have more, half have less. The **average** (mean) adds up everyone's wealth and divides by the number of households. Because a small number of households hold very large fortunes, the mean is several times the median: about **$1.06 million** for all households in 2022, against a median of about **$193,000**.

That's why headlines about "average net worth" can be discouraging. Most households are far below the average; it's the median that tells you what's typical. Our [mean, median and mode calculator](/math/mean-median-mode-calculator/) shows the same effect on any set of numbers.

## Three better ways to judge your net worth

National medians mix people with very different incomes, family situations and costs of living. These benchmarks are more personal.

### 1. The income-based rule

In *The Millionaire Next Door*, Thomas Stanley and William Danko suggested a simple formula for expected net worth:

**Expected net worth = age × annual pre-tax household income ÷ 10**

| Age | Household income | Expected net worth |
|---|---|---|
| 30 | $55,000 | $165,000 |
| 40 | $80,000 | $320,000 |
| 50 | $100,000 | $500,000 |
| 60 | $120,000 | $720,000 |

They called people with twice the expected figure "prodigious accumulators of wealth". The formula is demanding for young people, who've had little time to save and often carry student loans, so it's most useful from about 40 onward.

### 2. Retirement savings multiples

Fidelity's widely quoted guideline looks only at retirement savings, as a multiple of salary:

| By age | Retirement savings |
|---|---|
| 30 | 1× salary |
| 40 | 3× salary |
| 50 | 6× salary |
| 60 | 8× salary |
| 67 | 10× salary |

These assume you start saving about 15% of pay in your mid-twenties and retire at 67. If you're behind, a higher contribution rate and catch-up contributions after 50 help; see [how much to contribute to your 401(k)](/guides/how-much-to-contribute-to-401k/).

### 3. Your own trend

The most meaningful benchmark is your past self. Unlike a national median, it accounts for your own income, family, location and goals, and it shows whether the choices you're making are working. Calculate your net worth once or twice a year, on the same dates and with the same method. Over several years, it should rise by at least what you save, plus investment growth. If it doesn't, find out why: rising debt, spending creep, or a large depreciating purchase such as a new car.

## Worked example: checking yourself against all three

Jordan is 40, has a household income of $80,000, a net worth of $250,000 (including $90,000 of home equity) and $180,000 in retirement accounts.

| Benchmark | Target | Jordan | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| Median net worth, ages 35 to 44 | $135,000 | $250,000 | Well above the median |
| Income rule (40 × $80,000 ÷ 10) | $320,000 | $250,000 | $70,000 below |
| Retirement savings at 40 (3× salary) | $240,000 | $180,000 (2.25×) | $60,000 behind |

The three answers differ, and that's the point. Compared with other households of the same age, Jordan is doing well. Against benchmarks tied to income, there's room to improve, particularly in retirement savings. Raising 401(k) contributions by a few percentage points over the next couple of years would close most of the gap by 50.

The lesson: use the median for context and the income-based targets for planning. And remember that the benchmarks don't know about your goals; if you plan to retire early, or expect a pension, your targets will differ.

## What isn't in net worth

Net worth is a balance sheet, not a full picture. It leaves out:

- **Future Social Security benefits,** which for many households are worth more than all their savings combined.
- **Defined-benefit pensions,** unless you can take them as a lump sum.
- **Your earning power,** often the largest asset of all for younger people.

That's why two households with the same net worth can be in very different positions. A 30-year-old doctor with student loans may have a negative net worth but excellent prospects; a 60-year-old with the same net worth and no pension has much less time to build.

## Net worth by life stage

### Under 35

Negative or low net worth is normal. Student loans, car loans and early-career incomes weigh on the balance sheet. Priorities: build an emergency fund, capture any 401(k) match, and pay off high-interest debt. Even small, consistent retirement contributions now have 30 to 40 years to compound.

### 35 to 44

Net worth often jumps as income rises and home equity builds, though childcare and housing costs compete for every dollar. This is often the decade when early savings habits start to show, because contributions made in your twenties have had ten or more years to compound. The median more than triples from the under-35 group. Priorities: raise retirement contributions toward 15% of pay, avoid lifestyle creep as income grows, and keep debts in check.

### 45 to 54

Usually peak earning years. Priorities: maximize retirement savings, use catch-up contributions from 50, plan for college costs without sacrificing retirement, and start estimating retirement income with the [retirement calculator](/finance/retirement-calculator/).

### 55 to 64

The final stretch before retirement. Many households pay off the mortgage, which boosts net worth. Priorities: decide when to claim Social Security, plan the mix of traditional and Roth withdrawals, and reduce investment risk gradually.

### 65 and over

Net worth typically peaks and then declines as savings fund retirement. That's the plan working, not a failure. Required minimum distributions from traditional accounts start at 73 or 75 and gradually draw balances down. The focus shifts from growth to making the money last.

## What drives net worth most

- **Savings rate.** How much of your income you keep is the biggest lever, especially early on.
- **Time.** Compound growth needs decades; someone who starts at 25 can end up with more than twice as much as someone who starts at 35 with the same contribution rate.
- **Debt.** High-interest debt works like compounding in reverse. The [debt payoff calculator](/finance/debt-payoff-calculator/) shows the fastest way out.
- **Home ownership.** Paying down a mortgage builds equity, which is a large part of most households' net worth, although it isn't easily spent.
- **Investment costs and choices.** Low-cost, diversified funds held for the long term beat frequent trading for most people.
- **Life events.** Divorce, job loss, illness and inheritances can move net worth sharply in either direction.

## Why net worth varies so much

Wealth differs widely by education, income, race and region, and the SCF shows large and persistent gaps between groups. Homeowners have far higher median net worth than renters, partly because of home equity and partly because homeowners tend to be older and have higher incomes. Couples' net worth is reported per household, so a single person and a two-earner couple aren't directly comparable. Keep these differences in mind before judging yourself against a national figure.

## How to increase your net worth

1. **Track it.** Use the [net worth calculator](/finance/net-worth-calculator/) to set your starting point.
2. **Automate saving** into retirement and savings accounts each payday.
3. **Take the full employer match;** the [401(k) calculator](/finance/401k-calculator/) shows what it adds up to.
4. **Pay off high-interest debt** before investing beyond the match.
5. **Invest for the long term** in low-cost funds; the [investment calculator](/finance/investment-calculator/) projects the growth.
6. **Avoid big depreciating purchases on credit,** especially new cars.
7. **Save half of every raise,** so your lifestyle grows more slowly than your income.

## Key terms

- **Net worth:** total assets minus total debts.
- **Median:** the middle value; half of households have more and half have less.
- **Mean:** the arithmetic average, pulled up by very wealthy households.
- **Home equity:** your home's value minus the mortgage balance.
- **Survey of Consumer Finances:** the Federal Reserve's triennial survey of U.S. household finances.
