**Short answer:** to scale a recipe, work out a **scale factor**, the servings you want divided by the servings the recipe makes, and multiply every ingredient by it. Then turn awkward results into amounts you can measure, use a pan whose **area** matches the new amount, and judge the baking time by doneness rather than by the clock. Going from 4 servings to 6 is a factor of 6 ÷ 4 = 1.5, so 2 cups of flour becomes 3 cups and 1/3 cup of sugar becomes 1/2 cup. The [recipe scaler](/food/recipe-scaler/) does this for a whole ingredient list at once.

## Step 1: find the scale factor

**Scale factor = what you want ÷ what the recipe makes**

The "what" can be anything you can count or measure:

- **Servings:** a recipe for 4 scaled to 10 has a factor of 10 ÷ 4 = 2.5.
- **Pieces:** a recipe for 24 cookies scaled to 36 has a factor of 1.5.
- **Pan area:** an 8-inch square pan (64 square inches) scaled to a 9 × 13-inch pan (117 square inches) has a factor of 117 ÷ 64 ≈ 1.83.
- **An ingredient you're short of:** if the recipe needs 4 eggs and you only have 3, the factor is 3 ÷ 4 = 0.75.

Common factors are easy to remember: **2** doubles, **0.5** halves, **1.5** adds half again, **0.75** takes away a quarter, and **3** triples. Write the factor at the top of the recipe before you start, so you apply the same number to every line.

## Step 2: multiply every ingredient

Multiply each quantity by the factor. For a factor of 0.75, based on the 3 eggs you have:

| Ingredient | Original | × 0.75 | To measure |
|---|---|---|---|
| Flour | 2 cups | 1.5 | 1 1/2 cups |
| Sugar | 1 cup | 0.75 | 3/4 cup |
| Salt | 1/2 tsp | 0.375 | 3/8 tsp |
| Oil | 1/3 cup | 0.25 | 1/4 cup |
| Eggs | 4 | 3 | 3 eggs |
| Milk | 1 1/4 cups | 0.9375 | 15 tablespoons |

Every ingredient gets the same factor. It's tempting to scale only the main ingredients and "eyeball" the rest, but a cake with 1.5 times the flour and the original amount of baking powder won't rise properly.

## Step 3: make the amounts measurable

Multiplying fractions often gives amounts that aren't on your measuring cups. Convert them using the standard U.S. equivalents:

- 1 tablespoon = 3 teaspoons
- 1/4 cup = 4 tablespoons
- 1/3 cup = 5 tablespoons + 1 teaspoon
- 1/2 cup = 8 tablespoons
- 1 cup = 16 tablespoons = 48 teaspoons

Some results that come up often when halving:

| Original | Half | How to measure |
|---|---|---|
| 1/4 cup | 1/8 cup | 2 tablespoons |
| 1/3 cup | 1/6 cup | 2 tablespoons + 2 teaspoons |
| 3/4 cup | 3/8 cup | 6 tablespoons |
| 1 tablespoon | 1/2 tablespoon | 1 1/2 teaspoons |
| 1/4 teaspoon | 1/8 teaspoon | a small pinch, or a 1/8 tsp measure |

In the table above, 0.9375 cup of milk is exactly 15 tablespoons, which is just 1 tablespoon short of a cup. For arithmetic with fractions, the [fraction calculator](/math/fraction-calculator/) adds, multiplies and simplifies them.

### Eggs

Eggs don't divide neatly. For half an egg, beat a whole egg and use about half of it, roughly 1½ tablespoons for a large egg. When a scaled recipe calls for 4½ eggs, you can usually use 4 large eggs plus a splash of milk or water in a forgiving recipe, or 5 eggs in something like an omelet or frittata. For precise baking, weigh them: a large egg is roughly 50 grams out of the shell.

## Step 4: choose the right pan

When you scale a baked recipe, the pan has to change with it. The goal is to keep the batter at the **same depth**, so it bakes the same way. That means matching the pan's **area**, not its width.

| Pan | Area |
|---|---|
| 8-inch square | 64 sq in |
| 9-inch square | 81 sq in |
| 9 × 13-inch rectangle | 117 sq in |
| 8-inch round | about 50 sq in |
| 9-inch round | about 64 sq in |

So a recipe for an 8-inch square doubles nicely into a 9 × 13-inch pan (117 sq in is close to 128), and a 9-inch round holds about the same as an 8-inch square. A 9-inch square holds about 27% more than an 8-inch square, which matters for brownies and bars. For round pans, the area is 3.14 × radius².

If you don't have a pan of the right size, bake in two smaller pans, or fill a larger pan less deeply and check it early.

## Step 5: adjust the cooking

Times and temperatures don't scale the way ingredients do:

- **Same depth, more pans:** the time stays about the same. Rotate pans halfway if your oven has hot spots, and leave room for air to circulate.
- **Deeper pan:** it takes longer to bake through. Some bakers lower the oven by about 25 °F and bake longer to stop the edges overbaking before the center sets.
- **Smaller amounts:** halved recipes in smaller pans often bake faster; start checking several minutes early.
- **Roasts and casseroles:** a bigger roast takes longer per piece, but not in proportion to its weight. Use a thermometer and cook to the right internal temperature.
- **Soups and sauces:** a larger batch reduces more slowly in the same pot, and a wider pot evaporates faster. Adjust the liquid by eye and taste.

Always use the recipe's doneness cues, such as a toothpick coming out clean, a set center or a target internal temperature, rather than relying on time alone.

## Ingredients that need care

Most ingredients scale exactly, but a few deserve attention:

- **Leavening (baking powder, baking soda, yeast):** fine to scale exactly for doubling or halving. For very large batches, slightly less than the full amount is common.
- **Salt and strong spices:** scale them, but taste before adding the last of it in savory dishes; a little less is easier to fix than too much.
- **Thickeners (flour, cornstarch, gelatin):** scale exactly for consistent texture.
- **Liquids in baking:** scale exactly; in stovetop cooking, adjust for evaporation.
- **Alcohol and vinegar in marinades:** scale exactly, but marinating time doesn't change with quantity.

## Weighing makes scaling easier

Measuring by weight removes most of the awkwardness. Multiply grams by the factor and weigh the result; 1.5 × 250 g of flour is 375 g, with no fractions of a cup to convert. Weighing is also more accurate: a cup of flour can weigh noticeably different amounts depending on how it's scooped, which is a common reason a scaled baking recipe turns out differently from the original.

If your recipe uses cups, it's worth weighing your own cup of flour once and writing it down, so future scaling can be done by weight. The [percentage calculator](/math/percentage-calculator/) helps with baker's percentages, where each ingredient is a percentage of the flour's weight.

## Converting between U.S. and metric recipes

Scaling and converting are separate steps, and it's easiest to do them in that order: scale the recipe in its original units, then convert if you need to. A U.S. cup is about 236.6 mL, a tablespoon about 14.8 mL and a teaspoon about 4.9 mL. Recipes from Australia, New Zealand and some other countries use a 250 mL cup and a 20 mL tablespoon, and many European recipes give dry ingredients by weight. Oven temperatures convert with °C = (°F − 32) × 5/9, so 350 °F is about 177 °C, usually written as 175 or 180 °C. Our [metric conversion chart](/guides/metric-conversion-chart/) lists the common kitchen conversions.

## Write down what worked

Once you've scaled a recipe and cooked it, note the new amounts, the pan you used, the oven temperature and the actual time it took. The second time you make it, those notes turn an experiment into a reliable recipe, and they make it easy to scale again from a version you already know works.

## Scaling for a crowd

For large gatherings:

- **Decide the number of servings first,** with a margin of 10% or so.
- **Make several batches** of the original size rather than one enormous one, especially for baking. It keeps pans, oven space and mixer capacity manageable.
- **Check equipment early:** your largest pot, bowl or baking sheet sets the practical batch size.
- **Allow extra time** for prep and cooking, since chopping and mixing big quantities takes longer.

## Common mistakes

- **Scaling only some ingredients** instead of all of them.
- **Doubling the pan's width instead of its area.** A square pan twice as wide on each side holds four times as much, not twice.
- **Doubling the baking time** along with the ingredients.
- **Rounding early and inconsistently,** so the ratios drift. Convert to measurable amounts once, at the end.
- **Mixing up teaspoons and tablespoons** when converting small amounts.

## Key terms

- **Scale factor:** the number every ingredient is multiplied by.
- **Yield:** how many servings or pieces a recipe makes.
- **Leavening:** ingredients that make baked goods rise.
- **Baker's percentage:** an ingredient's weight as a percentage of the flour's weight.
- **Doneness test:** a check, such as a toothpick or thermometer, that shows food is fully cooked.
- **Limiting ingredient:** the ingredient you have least of, relative to the recipe, which sets the largest batch you can make.
- **Batch:** one complete preparation of a recipe at a given size.
