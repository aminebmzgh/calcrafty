**Short answer:** to find a sale price, multiply the original price by **(1 − the discount as a decimal)**. For 25% off $80, that's $80 × 0.75 = **$60**. To find the percent off between two prices, divide the amount saved by the original price. To find the original price from a sale price, divide by the share you paid. The [discount calculator](/math/discount-calculator/) does all of this, including stacked coupons and sales tax.

## Step 1: Turn the percentage into a decimal

Every discount calculation starts by changing the percentage into a decimal: divide by 100, or move the decimal point two places to the left.

- 25% → 0.25
- 15% → 0.15
- 7.5% → 0.075
- 60% → 0.60

Then work out the share you **pay**, which is 1 minus that decimal. At 25% off, you pay 0.75 of the price; at 60% off, you pay 0.40.

## Step 2: Find the discount and the sale price

There are two equivalent ways to get the sale price.

**Method 1, subtract the discount:**

1. Discount amount = original price × discount decimal.
2. Sale price = original price − discount amount.

For **15% off $36**: $36 × 0.15 = **$5.40** off, and $36 − $5.40 = **$30.60**.

**Method 2, multiply by what you pay:**

Sale price = original price × (1 − discount decimal).

For 15% off $36: $36 × 0.85 = **$30.60**. This is one step instead of two, and it's the method that makes stacked discounts easy.

## Finding the percent off from two prices

When a tag shows the old price and the new price but not the percentage, work it out like this:

**Percent off = (original price − sale price) ÷ original price × 100**

A sofa marked down from **$250 to $185**: $250 − $185 = $65 off, and $65 ÷ $250 = 0.26, so it's **26% off**.

Always divide by the **original** price. Dividing by the sale price gives the wrong answer ($65 ÷ $185 = 35%), because that measures how much more the original price was than the sale price, which is a different question.

## Finding the original price

This is the calculation people most often get wrong. If an item is **$63.75 after 25% off**, it's tempting to add 25% back: $63.75 × 1.25 = $79.69. That's wrong, because the 25% was taken from the original price, which is larger than $63.75.

The correct method is to divide by the share you paid:

**Original price = sale price ÷ (1 − discount decimal)**

$63.75 ÷ 0.75 = **$85.00**. Check: 25% of $85 is $21.25, and $85 − $21.25 = $63.75.

The same idea explains why a price that falls 20% needs to rise **25%** to get back to where it was: $100 × 0.80 = $80, and $80 × 1.25 = $100.

## Finding the discount you need for a target price

Sometimes you know the price you want to pay and want to know what discount gets you there, for example when deciding whether to wait for a sale. Use the percent-off formula with your target as the sale price.

A pair of boots costs **$72**, and you'd buy them at **$50**. The discount needed is ($72 − $50) ÷ $72 = **30.6%**. A 30%-off sale would bring them to $50.40, just above your target; a 35%-off sale would bring them to $46.80.

## Calculating discounts in your head

You rarely need a calculator for a rough figure. Build any percentage from easy pieces:

- **10%:** move the decimal point one place left. 10% of $60 = $6.
- **5%:** half of 10%. 5% of $60 = $3.
- **1%:** move the decimal two places. 1% of $60 = $0.60.
- **25%:** a quarter. 25% of $60 = $15.
- **50%:** half.

Then combine them. **35% off $60** is 30% ($18) plus 5% ($3) = $21 off, so you pay **$39**. **15% off $36** is 10% ($3.60) plus 5% ($1.80) = $5.40 off.

Fraction discounts work the same way. "**One third off**" $45 means paying two thirds: $45 × 2 ÷ 3 = **$30**.

## Stacked and successive discounts

When a store offers "an extra 20% off sale prices", the second discount applies to the already reduced price. Multiply the shares you pay:

**Final price = original × (1 − first discount) × (1 − second discount)**

For **30% off, then an extra 20% off**: 0.70 × 0.80 = 0.56. You pay 56% of the original price, so the total discount is **44%**, not 50%.

On a **$89.50** item: $89.50 × 0.70 = $62.65, then $62.65 × 0.80 = **$50.12**.

The order of two percentage discounts doesn't matter, because multiplication works in any order: 0.80 × 0.70 is also 0.56. The order does matter when one discount is a fixed dollar amount. Taking a percentage off first and then the dollar coupon leaves a lower price than the reverse, because the percentage applies to the larger amount.

## Multi-buy deals as percentages

To compare a multi-buy offer with a straight discount, divide what you pay by the full price of everything you get.

- **Buy 2, get 1 free** on $8 items: you pay $16 for $24 of goods, which is **33.3% off** each.
- **Buy one, get one 40% off** on two $50 items: you pay $50 + $30 = $80 for $100, which is **20% off** each.
- **Spend $100, save $15:** a 15% discount if you spend exactly $100; less if you spend more.

These deals only save money on things you'd have bought anyway.

## Comparing prices per unit

A discount isn't the only way to judge value. For groceries and household goods, the **unit price** is what tells you which deal is cheaper:

**Unit price = price ÷ number of units (ounces, sheets, cans, loads)**

A **24-pack** of sparkling water for **$13.99** is $13.99 ÷ 24 = **58.3¢** a can. A **36-pack** for **$19.49** is $19.49 ÷ 36 = **54.1¢** a can, so the bigger pack is cheaper per can even though no discount is advertised. NIST's uniform unit pricing guidance is what many states base their shelf-label rules on, which is why many store shelf tags already show a unit price.

## Discounts in a spreadsheet

In Excel or Google Sheets, with the original price in A2 and the discount percentage in B2:

- **Sale price:** =A2*(1-B2/100), or =A2*(1-B2) if B2 is formatted as a percentage.
- **Amount saved:** =A2*B2/100.
- **Percent off between two prices** (original in A2, sale in C2): =(A2-C2)/A2.
- **Original price from a sale price:** =C2/(1-B2/100).

## Percentage discounts vs dollar discounts

A dollar discount is worth more on cheap items and a percentage discount is worth more on expensive ones. To find where they break even, divide the dollar amount by the percentage as a decimal. A **$20-off** coupon and **15% off** are worth the same on a $20 ÷ 0.15 = **$133.33** purchase. Below that, take the $20; above it, take the 15%. If a store lets you choose, run this check before you pay, especially on big purchases such as furniture or electronics.

## Rounding

Stores round each price to the cent, usually to the nearest cent. A **$49.99** item at **20% off** is $49.99 × 0.80 = $39.992, which rings up as **$39.99**. When several discounts apply, each step is usually rounded, so the final price can differ by a cent from a single combined calculation.

## Discounts, tax and tips together

When a bill includes a discount, sales tax and a tip, the order matters:

1. **Apply the discount** to the item prices.
2. **Add sales tax,** which in most states is charged on the discounted price.
3. **Tip** on the amount before the discount if it's a restaurant meal, as most etiquette guides suggest, since the service was the same.

A **$60** meal with a **25%-off** voucher and **8%** tax: the food costs $45, tax is $3.60, and a 20% tip on the original $60 is $12, so you pay **$60.60** in total. The [tip calculator](/other/tip-calculator/) handles the tip side.

## Practice problems

Try these, then check the answers below.

1. A $140 coat is 35% off. What's the sale price?
2. A lamp drops from $80 to $56. What percent off is that?
3. After 40% off, a speaker costs $57. What was the original price?
4. A $200 bike is 20% off, with an extra 15% off at the register. What do you pay, and what's the total percent off?
5. Which is the better deal on a $90 purchase: 15% off or $15 off?

**Answers:**

1. $140 × 0.65 = **$91**.
2. $24 ÷ $80 = **30% off**.
3. $57 ÷ 0.60 = **$95**.
4. $200 × 0.80 × 0.85 = **$136**, which is **32% off** in total.
5. 15% of $90 is $13.50, so **$15 off** is better. The two are equal at $100; above that, the percentage wins.

## Common mistakes

1. **Adding stacked discounts.** 40% and 15% off is 49% off, not 55%.
2. **Dividing by the wrong price.** Percent off always uses the original price as the base.
3. **Adding the percentage back to find the original price.** Divide by the share paid instead.
4. **Forgetting tax and shipping.** Compare the final cost, not the advertised discount.
5. **Trusting an inflated "was" price.** A big percentage off a price nobody paid isn't a real saving.
