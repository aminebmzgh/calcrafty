**Short answer:** choose **traditional** if you expect your tax rate in retirement to be **lower** than it is now, and **Roth** if you expect it to be **higher**. If you expect it to be about the same, the two give the same after-tax result, and splitting contributions between them gives you flexibility. Earlier in a career, with lower income, Roth is often the better bet; in peak earning years, traditional's upfront deduction is usually worth more.

## How each one is taxed

| Feature | Traditional 401(k) | Roth 401(k) |
|---|---|---|
| Contributions | Before income tax | After income tax |
| Tax break | Now: lowers this year's taxable income | Later: qualified withdrawals are tax-free |
| Investment growth | Tax-deferred | Tax-free |
| Withdrawals in retirement | Taxed as ordinary income | Tax-free if qualified (59½ and five years) |
| Required minimum distributions | From age 73 (75 if born in 1960 or later) | None during your lifetime |
| 2026 contribution limit | $24,500 combined with Roth, plus catch-up after 50 | Same shared limit |
| Income limit | None | None |
| Social Security and Medicare tax | Still applies to contributions | Applies to contributions |

Both options sit inside the same 401(k) plan, use the same investment choices and share one contribution limit.

## The key insight: equal tax rates give equal results

It's natural to assume tax-free growth must be better. But with the same tax rate before and after, the two come out exactly equal, because multiplying by the growth and then taking tax is the same as taking tax and then multiplying.

Suppose you're in the 22% bracket and can afford to give up $3,900 of take-home pay. Your money grows 7% a year for 30 years, multiplying it by about 7.61.

- **Traditional:** you can contribute $5,000, because a $5,000 pre-tax contribution costs you only $3,900 after the 22% tax saving. It grows to **$38,061**. Withdrawn at a 22% rate, you keep **$29,688**.
- **Roth:** you contribute the $3,900 after tax. It grows to **$29,688**, all tax-free.

Same result. What tips the balance is a **difference** between your tax rate now and in retirement.

## When each one wins

The same $5,000 traditional contribution, against the $3,900 Roth contribution, with different tax rates in retirement:

| Tax rate in retirement | Traditional, after tax | Roth, tax-free | Better choice |
|---|---|---|---|
| 12% (lower than now) | $33,494 | $29,688 | Traditional, by $3,806 |
| 22% (same as now) | $29,688 | $29,688 | Equal |
| 24% (higher than now) | $28,927 | $29,688 | Roth, by $761 |

So the question is really: **will your tax rate be higher or lower when you withdraw the money?**

## Reasons to favor traditional

- **You're in a high bracket now** (22%, 24% or above) and expect a lower income in retirement, which is true for many people.
- **You'll retire in a lower-tax state,** or one with no income tax.
- **You need the cash flow now.** The tax saving makes each dollar of contribution cheaper today, which can help you contribute more.
- **Lowering your income now** can help you qualify for other tax credits, income-driven student loan payments, or a deductible traditional IRA.
- **You plan Roth conversions later,** in low-income years between retirement and Social Security, converting some traditional money at low rates.

## Reasons to favor Roth

- **You're early in your career** in the 10% or 12% bracket, with higher earnings ahead.
- **You expect higher taxes in retirement,** because of a large pension, big savings, or a belief that tax rates will rise.
- **You want flexibility.** Tax-free withdrawals don't push up your taxable income in retirement, which can reduce the tax on Social Security benefits and Medicare premium surcharges.
- **No required distributions.** Roth 401(k)s aren't subject to RMDs during your lifetime, so the money can keep growing.
- **You want to leave money to heirs,** who generally receive Roth withdrawals tax-free.
- **You max out the 401(k).** The limit is the same for both, but a Roth dollar holds more after-tax value. $24,500 in a Roth growing at 7% for 30 years becomes about $186,500, all yours; the same $24,500 in a traditional account becomes the same amount before tax, about $145,470 after a 22% tax.

## Why many people split contributions

Nobody knows future tax rates, or their own income in 30 years. Splitting contributions, such as half traditional and half Roth, gives you **tax diversification**: in retirement, you can choose each year whether to draw taxable or tax-free money, managing your bracket. It's a reasonable default when you're unsure.

A common approach by stage:

| Stage | Typical tilt |
|---|---|
| Early career, 10% or 12% bracket | Mostly or all Roth |
| Mid-career, 22% bracket | A mix |
| Peak earnings, 24% bracket or above | Mostly traditional |
| Low-income year (sabbatical, career change) | Roth, or a Roth conversion |

## What it means for your paycheck

Traditional contributions lower your taxable pay, so your take-home pay falls by less than the contribution. In the 22% bracket, a $200 traditional contribution per paycheck reduces take-home pay by about $156 in federal income tax terms, plus any state tax savings. A $200 Roth contribution reduces take-home pay by the full $200. Social Security and Medicare are charged on both.

## Employer matches and the 2026 catch-up rule

- **Matches:** employer matches have traditionally gone into the pre-tax side, whatever you choose. Under the SECURE 2.0 Act, plans can offer Roth matching contributions; if you choose that, the match is included in your taxable income for the year.
- **Catch-up contributions:** if you're 50 or older, you can add $8,000 in 2026 ($11,250 at ages 60 to 63). From 2026, if you earned more than $150,000 in FICA wages from the employer in the previous year, catch-up contributions must be Roth.

## Roth 401(k) vs Roth IRA

A Roth IRA works the same way for taxes but has a much lower limit ($7,500 in 2026), income limits that phase out eligibility for higher earners, and more flexible access to contributions. A Roth 401(k) has no income limit and a far higher contribution limit. Many people contribute to the 401(k) up to the match, then fund an IRA, then return to the 401(k). When you leave a job, a Roth 401(k) can be rolled into a Roth IRA.

## Don't forget state taxes

Your state income tax counts too. Contributing to a traditional 401(k) while living in a high-tax state, such as California or New York, and then retiring to a state with no income tax, such as Florida, Texas or Nevada, means you take the deduction at a high combined rate and pay little state tax on withdrawals. That move tilts strongly toward traditional. The reverse, working in a no-tax state and retiring to a high-tax one, tilts toward Roth. Most states follow the federal treatment of 401(k) contributions, but a few, such as Pennsylvania, don't let you deduct them for state tax.

## Common myths

- **"Tax-free always beats tax-deferred."** Not with equal tax rates, as the example above shows. What matters is the difference in rates.
- **"Roth is only for young people."** Anyone who expects a higher rate later can benefit, including high earners with large traditional balances that will produce big required distributions.
- **"I'll be in a much lower bracket in retirement."** Maybe, but many retirees have taxable income from pensions, Social Security and RMDs that keeps them in a similar bracket. Run the numbers rather than assuming.
- **"My match will be Roth if I choose Roth."** Only if your plan offers Roth matching and you elect it; otherwise the match goes into the traditional side.
- **"Roth money is locked until 59½."** Partly true for a Roth 401(k): an early, non-qualified withdrawal is treated as part contributions and part earnings, and the earnings part is taxed and usually penalized. After leaving a job, though, rolling the money into a Roth IRA lets you withdraw your original contributions at any time without tax or penalty.

## How to decide in three steps

1. **Find your current marginal tax rate** (your federal bracket plus your state rate).
2. **Estimate your retirement rate.** Use the [retirement calculator](/finance/retirement-calculator/) to estimate retirement income, including Social Security and 401(k) withdrawals, and see which bracket that falls in.
3. **Compare.** Clearly lower in retirement: lean traditional. Clearly higher: lean Roth. Unsure or close: split.

Whatever you choose, the bigger decisions are contributing enough to get the full match and saving consistently; see [how much to contribute to your 401(k)](/guides/how-much-to-contribute-to-401k/). The [401(k) calculator](/finance/401k-calculator/) projects your balance either way.

## Key terms

- **Marginal tax rate:** the rate on your next dollar of income.
- **Tax-deferred:** taxed later, when withdrawn.
- **Qualified distribution:** a Roth withdrawal after age 59½ and the five-year period, free of tax and penalty.
- **Required minimum distribution (RMD):** a minimum yearly withdrawal from traditional accounts from age 73 or 75.
- **Roth conversion:** moving money from a traditional account to a Roth account and paying tax on it now.
