**Short answer:** amortization is how a fixed monthly payment pays off a loan over time. Each payment first covers the interest charged on the remaining balance, and the rest repays principal. Because the balance is highest at the start, early payments are mostly interest: on a $300,000, 30-year mortgage at 6.5%, the first payment of $1,896.20 includes **$1,625 of interest and only $271.20 of principal**. As the balance falls, the split shifts, and by the last year almost every dollar goes to principal.

## The payment stays the same; the split changes

A standard fixed-rate mortgage is **fully amortizing**: the lender sets one monthly payment that, if paid every month, brings the balance to exactly zero at the end of the term. The payment is calculated with this formula:

**Payment = P × r ÷ (1 − (1 + r)^−n)**

where *P* is the amount borrowed, *r* is the monthly interest rate (the annual rate ÷ 12) and *n* is the number of payments. For $300,000 at 6.5% over 30 years, *r* = 0.065 ÷ 12 ≈ 0.005417 and *n* = 360, giving a payment of **$1,896.20** for principal and interest. Taxes, insurance and any PMI are added on top, but they don't affect amortization.

Each month, the lender does the same three steps:

1. **Interest** = remaining balance × monthly rate.
2. **Principal** = payment − interest.
3. **New balance** = old balance − principal.

## The first two payments

| Month | Payment | Interest | Principal | Balance after |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | $1,896.20 | $1,625.00 | $271.20 | $299,728.80 |
| 2 | $1,896.20 | $1,623.53 | $272.67 | $299,456.13 |

In month 1, interest is $300,000 × 0.065 ÷ 12 = $1,625.00. The remaining $271.20 reduces the balance. In month 2, interest is charged on the slightly smaller balance, so it's $1.47 lower, and principal is $1.47 higher. That small shift repeats every month, and it compounds: the principal share grows faster and faster over the life of the loan.

## Over the life of the loan

Here's the same loan year by year:

| Year | Principal paid | Interest paid | Share to principal | Balance at year end |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | $3,353 | $19,401 | 15% | $296,647 |
| 5 | $4,346 | $18,409 | 19% | $280,833 |
| 10 | $6,009 | $16,745 | 26% | $254,329 |
| 15 | $8,310 | $14,445 | 37% | $217,679 |
| 20 | $11,491 | $11,263 | 50% | $166,998 |
| 25 | $15,890 | $6,865 | 70% | $96,916 |
| 30 | $21,978 | $782 | 97% | $0 |

Some striking points from the schedule:

- **After 10 years** of payments, totaling about $227,500, you've paid $181,873 of interest and only $45,671 of principal. The balance is still $254,329, about 85% of the original loan.
- **The crossover** month, when principal finally exceeds interest, is **month 233**, more than 19 years in.
- **Half the balance** isn't repaid until **month 257**, more than 21 years in.
- **Total interest** over 30 years is **$382,637**, more than the amount borrowed.

The final payment is adjusted slightly ($1,900.91 here) to clear rounding differences.

## Why it works this way

It isn't a trick by the lender: you're simply paying interest on what you still owe. In the first year you owe nearly $300,000, so the interest is large. In the last year you owe less than $22,000, so the interest is small. The total payment stays level because the lender spreads repayment so that the falling interest is exactly offset by rising principal.

The result is that **equity builds slowly at first**, which matters if you plan to sell or refinance within a few years. After five years on this loan, you'd have repaid only about $19,000 of principal; most of any equity would have to come from rising home prices and your down payment.

## How the rate and term change the picture

The interest rate decides how front-loaded the schedule is. At higher rates, a bigger share of early payments is interest and the crossover comes later; at lower rates it comes sooner.

The term matters even more. The same $300,000 at 6.5%:

| Term | Monthly payment | Total interest |
|---|---|---|
| 30 years | $1,896.20 | $382,637 |
| 15 years | $2,613.32 | $170,398 |

The 15-year loan costs $717 more a month but saves over $212,000 of interest, because the balance falls faster and there are half as many years of interest. Fifteen-year rates are also usually lower than 30-year rates; at 5.75%, the 15-year payment would be $2,491.23 and total interest $148,422.

## What extra payments do

Any amount you pay above the scheduled payment goes **entirely to principal**. That lowers the balance immediately, so every later month is charged less interest, and the loan is paid off early.

On the $300,000, 6.5% 30-year loan:

| Extra each month | Interest saved | Time saved |
|---|---|---|
| $100 | $60,996 | 4 years (48 months) |
| $200 | $103,450 | 6 years 11 months (83 months) |

Extra payments are most powerful **early in the loan**, when each dollar of principal avoids interest for the most remaining years. Before paying extra, check that your lender applies it to principal (most have an option to specify this), that there's no prepayment penalty, and that you have an emergency fund and no higher-interest debt. Paying off a 22% credit card is a better return than prepaying a 6.5% mortgage.

Other ways to prepay include one extra payment a year, often by paying half the monthly amount every two weeks (26 half-payments make 13 full payments a year), or putting windfalls such as a bonus or tax refund toward principal.

## How amortization builds equity

Your home equity is the home's value minus what you owe. It grows in two ways: through the principal you repay, which is what amortization controls, and through any rise in the home's value.

Suppose the $300,000 loan was used to buy a $375,000 home with a $75,000 (20%) down payment. If the home's value stayed flat, equity after five years would be the $75,000 down payment plus $19,167 of principal repaid, about **$94,167**. After ten years it would be $75,000 + $45,671 = **$120,671**. Price changes usually swamp these figures in either direction, which is why a home bought just before a price drop can leave owners with little equity for years.

Amortization also decides when private mortgage insurance ends. On a conventional loan with less than 20% down, lenders must cancel PMI automatically when the balance is scheduled to reach 78% of the original home value, and you can ask for it to be removed at 80%. Extra principal payments bring that date forward.

## Adjustable-rate mortgages

With an adjustable-rate mortgage (ARM), the rate is fixed for an initial period, commonly five, seven or ten years, then resets periodically. At each reset, the lender recalculates the payment so that the remaining balance amortizes over the remaining term at the new rate. If rates have risen, the payment jumps and a bigger share goes to interest; if they've fallen, the payment drops. The mechanics are the same as a fixed-rate loan, just recalculated at each change.

## Recasting and refinancing

Two other ways to change your amortization:

- **Recasting:** after a large lump-sum payment, some lenders will recalculate your payment over the remaining term for a small fee. The rate and payoff date stay the same, but the monthly payment drops. It's an option if you want a lower payment rather than an earlier payoff.
- **Refinancing:** replacing the loan with a new one restarts amortization. If you refinance into a new 30-year loan, you go back to mostly-interest payments, which can raise the total interest even at a lower rate. The [refinance calculator](/finance/refinance-calculator/) compares both loans, and our guide [is refinancing worth it?](/guides/is-refinancing-worth-it/) explains the break-even math.

## Reading an amortization schedule

An amortization schedule lists every payment with the same columns as the tables above: payment number, payment amount, interest, principal and remaining balance. Your lender must give you one on request, and your monthly statement shows the split for each payment. Use it to:

- **See your equity** at any date: your home's value minus the remaining balance.
- **Plan extra payments,** by seeing how the balance changes.
- **Estimate mortgage interest for tax purposes** if you itemize deductions; your lender's Form 1098 gives the actual amount.

The [loan calculator](/finance/loan-calculator/) builds a full amortization schedule for any fixed-rate loan, and the [mortgage calculator](/finance/mortgage-calculator/) adds taxes, insurance and PMI to show the full monthly payment.

## Amortization on other loans

Car loans, personal loans and student loans amortize the same way: a fixed payment, with interest charged on the remaining balance. Because they're shorter, the interest share falls faster. Credit cards are different: they're revolving debt with a minimum payment that changes with the balance, so they don't amortize on a fixed schedule. Interest-only loans and some HELOCs during their draw period don't amortize at all until the repayment period starts.

## Key terms

- **Amortization:** paying off a loan through regular payments that cover interest and principal.
- **Principal:** the amount borrowed that's still owed, on which each month's interest is charged.
- **Equity:** the home's value minus the remaining mortgage balance.
- **Amortization schedule:** a table of every payment and how it's split.
- **Fully amortizing loan:** a loan whose payments bring the balance to zero by the end of the term.
- **Recast:** recalculating the payment after a lump-sum principal payment.
- **Negative amortization:** when unpaid interest is added to the balance, so the loan grows.
