**Short answer:** a 1-point lower rate is usually worth it **if you'll keep the loan longer than the break-even point**, which is your closing costs divided by your monthly savings. With closing costs of 3% of the balance, a drop from 7.5% to 6.5% takes about **45 months** to pay back. Just as important: if the new loan restarts a full 30-year term, you can pay *more* interest overall even at the lower rate. Pick a term that matches the years you have left.

## The 1% rule, and why it's incomplete

The old rule of thumb says refinance when you can cut your rate by at least one percentage point. It's a useful starting point because a full point usually produces savings large enough to cover closing costs within a few years. But it leaves out the three things that decide whether a refinance actually pays:

1. **How long you'll keep the new loan.** Closing costs are paid on day one; savings arrive monthly.
2. **What the refinance costs.** Fees, points and third-party charges vary widely between lenders.
3. **The new loan's term.** A new 30-year loan stretches your remaining balance over more years.

## Monthly savings by loan size

Here's what a lower rate saves each month on a 30-year loan, starting from 7.5%:

| Balance | 0.5 points lower (7.0%) | 1 point lower (6.5%) | 1.5 points lower (6.0%) |
|---|---|---|---|
| $200,000 | $67.83 | $134.29 | $199.33 |
| $300,000 | $101.73 | $201.44 | $298.99 |
| $400,000 | $135.65 | $268.59 | $398.66 |
| $500,000 | $169.56 | $335.73 | $498.32 |

If closing costs are 3% of the balance, the break-even is the same for every loan size, because both the costs and the savings grow with the balance:

| Rate drop | Break-even at 3% closing costs |
|---|---|
| 0.5 points | about 88 months (7.4 years) |
| 1 point | about 45 months (3.7 years) |
| 1.5 points | about 30 months (2.5 years) |

In practice, part of the closing costs is a flat amount (appraisal, title, recording), so **larger loans break even sooner** and small loans need a bigger rate drop to be worth it.

## The term-reset trap: a worked example

Five years ago, Dana took a $350,000 mortgage at 7.5% for 30 years. The payment is $2,447.25, the balance today is $331,161.09, and 25 years of payments remain, with **$403,014.93** of interest still to pay. A lender now offers 6.5% with $7,000 in closing costs.

| Option | Monthly payment | Saves per month | Break-even | Interest left to pay, plus costs |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Keep current loan | $2,447.25 | – | – | $403,014.93 |
| Refinance, new 30-year | $2,093.16 | $354.09 | 20 months | $429,380.11 |
| Refinance, new 25-year | $2,236.02 | $211.23 | 33 months | $346,647.45 |

The 30-year refinance has the bigger monthly saving and the faster break-even, so it looks like the better deal. But it adds five years of payments, and Dana would pay **$26,365 more** in total than by not refinancing at all. Refinancing into a 25-year loan keeps the same payoff date and **saves $56,367**.

If you want the lower payment for cash flow, you can still take the 30-year loan and pay extra each month to stay on your original schedule; most mortgages allow prepayment without a penalty.

## When refinancing makes sense, and when it doesn't

Refinancing is usually worth it when:

- You'll stay in the home (and keep the loan) well past the break-even point.
- The new term doesn't extend your payoff date, or you'll make extra payments to stay on schedule.
- You're switching from an adjustable rate to a fixed rate you can live with, or dropping mortgage insurance.

It's usually not worth it when:

- You plan to sell or move within the break-even period.
- The savings come mainly from restarting a 30-year term.
- You'd roll high closing costs into the balance and pay interest on them for decades.
- Your credit or income has changed enough that you won't get the advertised rate. Lenders look at your [debt-to-income ratio](/finance/debt-to-income-ratio-calculator/) again, just as they did the first time.

## Should you take a no-closing-cost refinance?

A "no-closing-cost" refinance doesn't make the costs disappear. The lender covers them in exchange for a higher rate, or adds them to the loan balance. Whether it's a good deal depends on how long you'll keep the loan.

Compare two offers on a $300,000, 30-year refinance:

- **Option A:** 6.5% with $9,000 in closing costs. Payment $1,896.20.
- **Option B:** 6.75% with no closing costs. Payment $1,945.79.

Option A saves $49.59 a month, so it takes about 182 months, roughly 15 years, to earn back the $9,000. If you sell or refinance again within five years, Option B leaves you about **$5,230 better off**: you've paid $6,024.60 less out of pocket, which more than offsets owing $794.23 more on the balance. If you're confident you'll keep the loan for 15 years or more, Option A wins.

No-closing-cost offers are especially useful when rates might fall further, because they leave you free to refinance again without having sunk thousands into the first one.

## Types of refinance

- **Rate-and-term refinance:** replaces the loan with a new rate, a new term or both, without taking out cash. This is what most of this guide describes.
- **Cash-out refinance:** borrows more than you owe and pays you the difference. Lenders typically limit the new loan to about 80% of the home's value for a conventional cash-out. It can fund renovations or replace high-interest debt, but it turns that money into a 30-year mortgage debt secured by your home, and cash-out loans usually carry slightly higher rates. A home equity loan or line of credit can be a cheaper way to borrow a smaller amount without touching a low first-mortgage rate.
- **Streamline refinances:** FHA Streamline and the VA's Interest Rate Reduction Refinance Loan (IRRRL) let existing FHA and VA borrowers refinance with less paperwork, often without a new appraisal, as long as the new loan provides a clear benefit such as a lower payment.

## Other reasons to refinance

A lower rate isn't the only reason. Refinancing can also make sense to:

- **Switch from an adjustable rate to a fixed rate** before the adjustable period resets, for payment certainty.
- **Remove mortgage insurance.** If your home's value has risen, a new conventional loan below 80% of the current value can drop PMI, or replace FHA mortgage insurance that would otherwise last for the life of the loan.
- **Shorten the term,** for example from 30 years to 15, to pay the home off sooner and pay much less interest overall, if the higher payment fits your budget.
- **Remove a co-borrower** after a divorce or separation, which usually requires qualifying on your own income.

## What affects the rate you're offered

Lenders price a refinance much like a new mortgage. A higher credit score, more equity (a lower loan-to-value ratio), a lower debt-to-income ratio and a primary residence rather than an investment property all lead to better rates. Cash-out refinances and loans above about 80% of the home's value usually cost a little more. Check where you stand with the [debt-to-income ratio calculator](/finance/debt-to-income-ratio-calculator/) before you apply.

## How long a refinance takes

From application to closing, a refinance commonly takes 30 to 45 days, longer if an appraisal or underwriting questions slow things down. Lock your rate once you've chosen a lender, and make sure the lock lasts until the expected closing date. On most refinances of your primary home with a new lender, federal law gives you three business days after signing to cancel without penalty, known as the right of rescission.

## Costs to check on the Loan Estimate

Every lender must give you a standardized Loan Estimate within three business days of your application, which makes offers easy to compare side by side. Look at:

- **Section A, origination charges:** lender fees and points. Points lower the rate but take longer to pay back.
- **Sections B and C, services:** appraisal, title insurance and settlement fees. You can shop for the ones in Section C.
- **The APR:** it includes the lender's fees, so it's a fairer comparison than the rate. Our [APR calculator](/finance/apr-calculator/) shows how fees change it.
- **Cash to close:** whether costs are paid upfront or added to the loan.

## A refinance checklist

1. Find your current balance, rate and the number of payments left on your latest statement.
2. Get Loan Estimates from at least three lenders on the same day, since rates change daily.
3. Divide total closing costs by the monthly savings to find your break-even month.
4. Compare total interest for the rest of your current loan with the new loan, plus costs.
5. Choose a term that doesn't push your payoff date further out, unless you need the lower payment.
6. Ask whether points make sense for how long you'll keep the loan.
