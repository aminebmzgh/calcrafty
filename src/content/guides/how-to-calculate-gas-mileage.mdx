**Short answer:** gas mileage is **miles driven ÷ gallons used**. The most reliable way to measure it is the **fill-up method**: fill the tank, drive until it needs fuel again, refill and divide the miles since the last fill by the gallons it took. For example, 349 miles on 11.42 gallons is **30.6 mpg**. Once you know your real mpg, the [fuel cost calculator](/other/fuel-cost-calculator/) turns any trip into dollars.

## The fill-up method, step by step

1. **Fill the tank completely** at the pump, letting it click off once. Don't top it up past the click.
2. **Write down the odometer reading,** or reset a trip meter to zero.
3. **Drive normally** until you're ready to refuel, ideally at least half a tank.
4. **Fill up again** the same way, at the same pump if you can, and note the **gallons** shown on the pump.
5. **Note the new odometer reading** and subtract the first one to get the miles driven.
6. **Divide miles by gallons.**

**Example:** the odometer reads **34,512** at the first fill and **34,861** at the second, so you drove **349 miles**. The second fill took **11.42 gallons**. Your mileage is 349 ÷ 11.42 = **30.6 mpg**.

The method works because the second fill replaces exactly the fuel you burned since the first, so you never need to know how big the tank is or how full it was. Filling the same way each time, to the first click, keeps the measurement consistent.

## Averaging over several tanks

One tank can be skewed by a long highway trip or a week of short errands. For a figure you can rely on, measure over three or more tanks and average them the right way: **total miles ÷ total gallons**.

| Tank | Miles | Gallons | mpg for that tank |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 349 | 11.42 | 30.6 |
| 2 | 288 | 12.10 | 23.8 |
| **Total** | **637** | **23.52** | **27.1** |

The correct average is 637 ÷ 23.52 = **27.1 mpg**. Averaging the two tank figures, (30.6 + 23.8) ÷ 2, gives 27.2, slightly too high, because it gives the smaller tank as much weight as the larger one. With more tanks and bigger differences, that error grows.

Keeping a small log in your phone or glovebox, with the date, odometer, gallons and price at each fill, gives you mileage and fuel spending at the same time.

## Converting between mpg and other units

| To convert | Formula | Example |
|---|---|---|
| mpg to L/100 km | 235.215 ÷ mpg | 30 mpg = 7.84 L/100 km |
| L/100 km to mpg | 235.215 ÷ L/100 km | 6 L/100 km = 39.2 mpg |
| mpg to km per litre | mpg × 0.425144 | 30 mpg = 12.75 km/L |
| mpg to gallons per 100 miles | 100 ÷ mpg | 30 mpg = 3.33 gal per 100 mi |

Countries that use litres per 100 km measure **consumption** (lower is better), while mpg measures **economy** (higher is better). The U.S. figures here are U.S. gallons; a UK (imperial) gallon is about 20% larger, so UK mpg figures look higher for the same car.

## Why gallons per 100 miles tells you more

Mpg is a misleading scale for comparing cars, because savings don't rise in a straight line. Look at the fuel each car burns over 100 miles:

- Going from **10 to 20 mpg** cuts fuel from 10 to 5 gallons per 100 miles: **5 gallons saved**.
- Going from **25 to 50 mpg** cuts fuel from 4 to 2 gallons per 100 miles: only **2 gallons saved**.

Replacing a very thirsty vehicle saves far more fuel than making an already efficient one even better. That's why the EPA fuel economy label shows gallons per 100 miles alongside mpg.

## Comparing your mpg with the EPA rating

Every new car sold in the U.S. has EPA fuel economy ratings for **city**, **highway** and **combined** driving, shown on the window sticker and on fueleconomy.gov. The combined figure weights city driving at 55% and highway at 45%, using a harmonic average:

**Combined mpg = 1 ÷ (0.55 ÷ city mpg + 0.45 ÷ highway mpg)**

A car rated **28 city** and **36 highway** has a combined rating of 1 ÷ (0.55 ÷ 28 + 0.45 ÷ 36) = **31.1 mpg**, slightly below a simple average of 32, because the slower city miles burn more fuel.

If your measured mpg is close to the rating, your car and driving are typical. If it's well below, look at the factors in the next section.

## What lowers gas mileage

Fueleconomy.gov, run by the Department of Energy and the EPA, lists the main causes of poor real-world mileage:

- **Speed.** Mileage usually drops quickly above about 50 mph, because air resistance rises with the square of speed.
- **Aggressive driving.** Rapid acceleration and hard braking waste fuel, especially in stop-and-go traffic.
- **Cold weather and short trips.** A cold engine runs less efficiently until it warms up, and winter fuel blends contain slightly less energy.
- **Extra weight and drag.** Roof boxes, bike racks and heavy cargo all cost fuel; remove racks when you're not using them.
- **Underinflated tires.** Keep them at the pressure on the driver's door sticker.
- **Idling.** An idling engine gets zero miles per gallon.
- **Air conditioning,** particularly in very hot weather and at low speeds.

Regular maintenance helps too: a well-tuned engine, the right grade of motor oil and properly aligned wheels all keep mileage close to the rating.

## Turning mpg into money

Your mpg sets your fuel **cost per mile**: **price per gallon ÷ mpg**. At $4.15 a gallon, a car that gets **28 mpg** costs about **14.8¢** a mile in fuel, and one that gets **40 mpg** about **10.4¢**.

Small improvements add up over a year. Driving **12,000 miles** at 28 mpg uses about 428.6 gallons. Raising your average by 10%, to 30.8 mpg, through gentler driving and correct tire pressure cuts that to 389.6 gallons, saving about **39 gallons**, or roughly **$162** a year at $4.15.

When you're shopping for a car, include fuel in the true cost of ownership. The [car affordability calculator](/finance/car-affordability-calculator/) and the [auto loan calculator](/finance/auto-loan-calculator/) cover the purchase side; a few cents a mile in fuel can matter as much as a point on the loan rate.

## Mileage and emissions

Every gallon of gasoline burned produces about **8,887 grams** of CO₂, according to the EPA, so better mileage means lower emissions in direct proportion. A car that improves from 25 to 30 mpg over 12,000 miles burns 80 fewer gallons and emits about 711 kg less CO₂ a year. The [car CO2 calculator](/ecology/car-co2-calculator/) works out your annual emissions from your mpg and mileage.

## Hybrids, plug-ins and electric cars

Hybrids are rated in mpg like any gasoline car and often achieve 45 mpg or more, because they recover energy when braking and can shut the engine off at low speeds. Plug-in hybrids and electric cars also carry an **MPGe** rating, miles per gallon equivalent, where 33.7 kWh of electricity counts as one gallon of gasoline. For an electric car, the more useful figure for costs is **kWh per 100 miles**; multiply it by your electricity price to compare with a gas car's cost per mile.

## Measuring city and highway mileage separately

Your overall average hides a lot. If you want to know what a particular kind of driving costs, measure it on its own:

- **Highway:** fill up just before a long highway drive and again at the end, without refueling in between. Divide the trip miles by the gallons.
- **Commute:** fill up on a Monday, drive only your normal commute for the week, and refill on Friday.
- **Towing or a loaded car:** measure the first full tank with the trailer or roof box attached.

A car that averages 27 mpg overall might return 34 on a steady highway run and 22 in winter city driving. Knowing both helps you budget a road trip more accurately than the overall figure.

## Using a fuel log to budget

A simple fuel log turns into a budget almost by itself. Record the date, odometer, gallons and total price at every fill. After a month, you'll know your real mileage, your average price per gallon and how much you spend on fuel. Divide the month's spending by the miles driven for your true cost per mile, including the stations you actually use. If the number creeps up, check tire pressure first; it's the easiest fix.

## Key terms

- **Miles per gallon (mpg):** distance traveled per U.S. gallon of fuel; higher is better.
- **Litres per 100 km:** fuel used per 100 kilometres; lower is better.
- **Gallons per 100 miles:** fuel used per 100 miles; the clearest way to compare savings.
- **EPA combined rating:** official fuel economy, weighting city driving 55% and highway 45%.
- **MPGe:** miles per gallon equivalent, used for electric and plug-in vehicles.
- **Cost per mile:** price per gallon divided by mpg.

## Common mistakes

1. **Topping up past the click** on one fill and not the other, which skews both readings.
2. **Averaging per-tank mpg figures** instead of dividing total miles by total gallons.
3. **Measuring after only a few miles,** where rounding in the gallons makes a big difference.
4. **Comparing UK and U.S. mpg** without converting gallon sizes.
5. **Judging a car by mpg alone** when gallons per 100 miles shows the real fuel used.
