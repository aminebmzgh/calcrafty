**Short answer:** list every debt, keep paying all the minimums, and put every extra dollar toward **one debt at a time**. Paying the **highest interest rate first** (the avalanche method) costs the least; paying the **smallest balance first** (the snowball) gives faster wins. Either way, when a debt is paid off, roll its payment into the next one. The [debt payoff calculator](/finance/debt-payoff-calculator/) compares both methods with your numbers.

## Step 1: List everything you owe

Write down each debt with its balance, interest rate (APR) and minimum payment. Include credit cards, personal loans, car loans, student loans, medical bills and buy-now-pay-later plans. Your free credit reports at AnnualCreditReport.com help you catch anything you've forgotten.

## Step 2: Choose a method

Both methods pay all minimums and send extra money to one target debt.

- **Avalanche:** target the highest APR first. Mathematically optimal.
- **Snowball:** target the smallest balance first. Faster early wins.

Here's a real comparison. Someone owes $900 on a store card at 26.99% (minimum $35), $7,400 on a Visa at 24.49% (minimum $185) and $6,000 on a personal loan at 11.5% (minimum $198), and can add $300 a month.

| Method | Debt-free in | Total interest | Order |
|---|---|---|---|
| Avalanche | 2 years | $2,792.76 | Store card, Visa, loan |
| Snowball | 2 years 1 month | $3,414.02 | Store card, loan, Visa |
| Minimums only | 7 years | $9,699.03 | |

The avalanche saves $621.26 here because it attacks the 24.49% Visa before the 11.5% loan. The snowball wipes out the loan in month 14, six months before the avalanche clears the Visa, and that early win keeps some people going. Choose the method you'll actually stick with.

## Snowball or avalanche? A quick way to decide

Run both in the calculator, then look at two numbers: the difference in total interest, and how long until the first debt disappears.

- **If the avalanche saves only a little,** say under a few hundred dollars, and the snowball gives you a win in the first few months, the snowball's motivation is probably worth more than the savings.
- **If the avalanche saves a lot,** typically because a large balance carries a much higher rate than the others, use the avalanche. Paying thousands more in interest is a high price for motivation.
- **If your smallest debt also has the highest rate,** the two methods are identical; just start.

You can also combine them: clear one tiny balance first for a quick win, then switch to highest-rate-first for the rest.

## Step 3: Find extra money

The extra payment drives everything. Even $100 a month shortens payoff by months or years. Places to look:

- **Your take-home pay.** Check what actually lands in your account with the [paycheck calculator](/finance/paycheck-calculator/), then build the budget around that.
- **Subscriptions and recurring bills.** Cancel what you don't use; call insurers and phone carriers for better rates.
- **Windfalls.** Tax refunds, bonuses and gifts can go straight to the target debt.
- **Temporary income.** Overtime or side work for a defined period, all of it going to debt.

## How much faster an extra payment gets you out

The size of the extra payment matters more than the method. For the same three debts, using the avalanche order:

| Extra each month | Debt-free in | Total interest |
|---|---|---|
| $0 (rolling over minimums only) | 53 months | $7,507.99 |
| $100 | 37 months | $4,773.15 |
| $200 | 29 months | $3,496.73 |
| $300 | 24 months | $2,792.76 |
| $500 | 18 months | $2,020.30 |

The first $100 is worth the most: it cuts more than a year and over $2,700 of interest. Each additional $100 still helps, by less each time. If you can't find much extra money yet, don't wait: start with what you have and raise it as you go.

## What a 24-month plan looks like

Using the avalanche method with $300 extra, the plan unfolds like this:

- **Months 1–3:** the extra $300 plus the $35 minimum go to the store card, which is gone in **month 3**.
- **Months 4–20:** its $35 minimum rolls over, so $520 a month goes to the Visa, which is paid off in **month 20**.
- **Months 21–24:** everything, $718 a month, goes to the personal loan, finished in **month 24**.

Notice that the total monthly payment never changes. What changes is where it goes, and as each debt disappears, more of every payment reduces the next balance instead of paying interest.

## Step 4: Lower the interest you pay

Cheaper interest means more of each payment reduces the balance.

- **Ask for a lower rate.** Card issuers sometimes reduce APRs for customers with good payment history.
- **Balance transfer.** A 0% promotional card, usually with a 3% to 5% fee, can save a lot if you clear the balance before the promotion ends. The [credit card payoff calculator](/finance/credit-card-payoff-calculator/) shows the monthly payment needed.
- **Consolidation loan.** A fixed-rate personal loan at a clearly lower APR gives one payment and an end date. Check the total cost with the [loan calculator](/finance/loan-calculator/).

## Step 5: Stop new debt from creeping in

A payoff plan only works if balances don't grow back. Keep a small emergency fund so a car repair doesn't go on a card, switch to debit or cash for everyday spending, and remove saved cards from online stores. Automate every minimum payment so you never miss one; late fees and penalty APRs undo progress quickly.

## Step 6: Track progress and celebrate milestones

Re-run the calculator every few months with your new balances. Watching the debt-free date move closer is motivating, and each paid-off account frees up its minimum for the next. Mark milestones with something small that doesn't involve new debt.

## If you can't afford the minimum payments

If your income doesn't cover even the minimums, a payoff method won't solve the problem on its own. Act early, before accounts go to collections:

- **Call your lenders.** Many card issuers and lenders have hardship programs that temporarily lower the rate or the payment. Federal student loans offer income-driven repayment plans that can reduce payments based on income.
- **Talk to a nonprofit credit counselor.** Agencies affiliated with the National Foundation for Credit Counseling offer budget reviews, often free, and can set up a debt management plan that may lower interest rates on cards.
- **Be wary of debt settlement offers.** Companies that promise to settle debts for less usually ask you to stop paying, which damages your credit and adds fees, and the Federal Trade Commission warns about scams. A legitimate company can't charge fees before it settles a debt.
- **Know that bankruptcy exists.** It has serious long-term consequences but can be the right choice for overwhelming debt. A bankruptcy attorney or legal aid office can explain the options.

## Common mistakes that slow people down

- **Closing paid-off cards right away.** It can raise your credit utilization and lower your score. Keep no-fee cards open and unused instead.
- **Ignoring the emergency fund.** Without even a small cushion, the next unexpected bill goes back on a card.
- **Paying extra on low-rate debt first.** Extra payments toward a 4% car loan while a card charges 24% cost you money, unless you've deliberately chosen the snowball for motivation.
- **Missing the employer 401(k) match.** Free money from your employer usually beats paying down debt faster.
- **Not tracking new spending.** Paying $500 a month toward debt while adding $400 in new charges barely moves the total.

## How paying off debt helps beyond the interest

Clearing debt does more than save interest. Lower card balances usually lift your credit score within a couple of billing cycles, because credit utilization is a major scoring factor. Fewer monthly payments also lower your debt-to-income ratio, which lenders check before approving a mortgage or car loan; the [debt-to-income ratio calculator](/finance/debt-to-income-ratio-calculator/) shows how each paid-off debt changes it. And every payment you eliminate frees up cash flow for saving and investing.

## Your plan in one week

1. **Day 1:** pull your credit reports and list every debt with its balance, APR and minimum.
2. **Day 2:** enter them in the [debt payoff calculator](/finance/debt-payoff-calculator/) and pick a method.
3. **Day 3:** find your extra amount, even if it's only $50 to start.
4. **Day 4:** set every minimum to autopay.
5. **Day 5:** schedule the extra payment to your target debt for the day after payday.
6. **Day 6:** call your highest-rate card issuer and ask for a lower APR.
7. **Day 7:** put a reminder in your calendar to re-run the numbers in three months.

That's the whole system. After the first week, the plan mostly runs itself: autopay covers the minimums, the scheduled transfer handles the extra, and your only job is to avoid new debt and send any windfall to the target. Most people find the hardest part is starting, not keeping it going.

## After you're debt-free

Keep the habit going. Send the same monthly amount to savings: first an emergency fund of three to six months of expenses, then longer-term goals. The [savings goal calculator](/finance/savings-goal-calculator/) shows how fast that money adds up once it's no longer going to lenders.
