**Short answer:** list every debt, keep paying all the minimums, and put every extra dollar toward **one debt at a time**. Paying the **highest interest rate first** (the avalanche method) costs the least; paying the **smallest balance first** (the snowball) gives faster wins. Either way, when a debt is paid off, roll its payment into the next one. The [debt payoff calculator](/finance/debt-payoff-calculator/) compares both methods with your numbers.

## Step 1: List everything you owe

Write down each debt with its balance, interest rate (APR) and minimum payment. Include credit cards, personal loans, car loans, student loans, medical bills and buy-now-pay-later plans. Your free credit reports at AnnualCreditReport.com help you catch anything you've forgotten.

## Step 2: Choose a method

Both methods pay all minimums and send extra money to one target debt.

- **Avalanche:** target the highest APR first. Mathematically optimal.
- **Snowball:** target the smallest balance first. Faster early wins.

Here's a real comparison. Someone owes $900 on a store card at 26.99% (minimum $35), $7,400 on a Visa at 24.49% (minimum $185) and $6,000 on a personal loan at 11.5% (minimum $198), and can add $300 a month.

| Method | Debt-free in | Total interest | Order |
|---|---|---|---|
| Avalanche | 2 years | $2,792.76 | Store card, Visa, loan |
| Snowball | 2 years 1 month | $3,414.02 | Store card, loan, Visa |
| Minimums only | 7 years | $9,699.03 | |

The avalanche saves $621.26 here because it attacks the 24.49% Visa before the 11.5% loan. The snowball wipes out the loan in month 14, six months before the avalanche clears the Visa, and that early win keeps some people going. Choose the method you'll actually stick with.

## Step 3: Find extra money

The extra payment drives everything. Even $100 a month shortens payoff by months or years. Places to look:

- **Your take-home pay.** Check what actually lands in your account with the [paycheck calculator](/finance/paycheck-calculator/), then build the budget around that.
- **Subscriptions and recurring bills.** Cancel what you don't use; call insurers and phone carriers for better rates.
- **Windfalls.** Tax refunds, bonuses and gifts can go straight to the target debt.
- **Temporary income.** Overtime or side work for a defined period, all of it going to debt.

## Step 4: Lower the interest you pay

Cheaper interest means more of each payment reduces the balance.

- **Ask for a lower rate.** Card issuers sometimes reduce APRs for customers with good payment history.
- **Balance transfer.** A 0% promotional card, usually with a 3% to 5% fee, can save a lot if you clear the balance before the promotion ends. The [credit card payoff calculator](/finance/credit-card-payoff-calculator/) shows the monthly payment needed.
- **Consolidation loan.** A fixed-rate personal loan at a clearly lower APR gives one payment and an end date. Check the total cost with the [loan calculator](/finance/loan-calculator/).

## Step 5: Stop new debt from creeping in

A payoff plan only works if balances don't grow back. Keep a small emergency fund so a car repair doesn't go on a card, switch to debit or cash for everyday spending, and remove saved cards from online stores. Automate every minimum payment so you never miss one; late fees and penalty APRs undo progress quickly.

## Step 6: Track progress and celebrate milestones

Re-run the calculator every few months with your new balances. Watching the debt-free date move closer is motivating, and each paid-off account frees up its minimum for the next. Mark milestones with something small that doesn't involve new debt.

## After you're debt-free

Keep the habit going. Send the same monthly amount to savings: first an emergency fund of three to six months of expenses, then longer-term goals. The [savings goal calculator](/finance/savings-goal-calculator/) shows how fast that money adds up once it's no longer going to lenders.
