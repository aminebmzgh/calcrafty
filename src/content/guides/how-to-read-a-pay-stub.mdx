**Short answer:** a pay stub shows what you earned (**gross pay**), everything taken out (**taxes and deductions**) and what's left (**net pay**), for this pay period and for the year to date. Read it top to bottom: earnings, then pre-tax deductions, then taxes, then after-tax deductions, then net pay. Check that the hours and rate are right, that the deductions match what you signed up for, and that the year-to-date totals make sense.

## The main sections of a pay stub

Layouts vary by employer and payroll provider, but almost every pay stub has the same building blocks:

1. **Employee and employer details:** your name, employee ID, employer name and address, and sometimes the last digits of your Social Security number.
2. **Pay period and pay date:** the dates the work was done and the date you were paid.
3. **Earnings:** each type of pay, with hours, rate and amount, for this period and year to date.
4. **Taxes:** federal income tax withholding, Social Security, Medicare, and any state and local taxes.
5. **Deductions:** pre-tax items such as a 401(k) and health insurance, and after-tax items such as Roth 401(k) contributions or wage garnishments.
6. **Net pay:** the amount deposited, often with the bank account it went to.
7. **Leave balances:** paid time off, vacation and sick leave, on many stubs.

## A worked example

Here's a simplified pay stub for a single employee earning a $65,000 salary, paid every two weeks (26 times a year), with a 6% traditional 401(k) contribution and $120 of health insurance premiums per paycheck, in a state with no income tax. This is pay period 10 of the year.

| Line | This period | Year to date |
|---|---|---|
| **Gross pay** (salary) | $2,500.00 | $25,000.00 |
| 401(k), 6% (pre-tax) | −$150.00 | −$1,500.00 |
| Health insurance (pre-tax) | −$120.00 | −$1,200.00 |
| Federal income tax | −$183.75 | −$1,837.50 |
| Social Security, 6.2% | −$147.56 | −$1,475.60 |
| Medicare, 1.45% | −$34.51 | −$345.10 |
| **Net pay** | **$1,864.18** | **$18,641.80** |

The rest of this guide explains each line and how it's calculated.

## Gross pay

**Gross pay** is everything you earned before anything is taken out. For a salaried employee paid biweekly, it's the annual salary ÷ 26: $65,000 ÷ 26 = $2,500. Hourly employees see one line per type of pay:

- **Regular:** hours × hourly rate.
- **Overtime:** overtime hours × 1.5 × the regular rate. The [overtime calculator](/finance/overtime-calculator/) checks this line.
- **Other earnings:** bonuses, commissions, shift differentials, holiday pay, paid time off, tips and reimbursements.

Check the hours against your own records; the [time card calculator](/everyday/time-card-calculator/) totals a week from your clock-in and clock-out times.

## Pre-tax deductions

These come out of your pay **before** income tax is calculated, so they lower your tax bill:

- **Traditional 401(k) or 403(b):** here, 6% of $2,500 = $150. It reduces income tax but not Social Security and Medicare.
- **Health, dental and vision premiums** under a Section 125 (cafeteria) plan: $120 here. These reduce income tax **and** FICA taxes.
- **HSA and FSA contributions** through payroll, which also escape both.
- **Commuter benefits** for transit and parking, up to monthly limits.

Because of these deductions, your taxable wages are lower than your gross pay, and some stubs show two different figures: **federal taxable wages** and **Social Security and Medicare wages**.

## Taxes

### Social Security and Medicare (FICA)

These are flat percentages of your FICA wages, which here are gross pay minus the Section 125 health premiums: $2,500 − $120 = $2,380.

- **Social Security:** 6.2% × $2,380 = **$147.56**. In 2026 it applies only to the first $184,500 of wages in the year; above that, this line drops to zero.
- **Medicare:** 1.45% × $2,380 = **$34.51**. There's no cap, and an extra 0.9% applies to wages over $200,000.

Your employer pays the same amounts again on your behalf; you won't see that on your stub.

### Federal income tax withholding

This is the hardest line to check by hand, because it depends on your Form W-4, your pay frequency and the progressive tax brackets. In outline, the payroll system:

1. Annualizes your taxable pay: ($2,500 − $150 − $120) × 26 = $57,980.
2. Subtracts the standard deduction for your filing status: $57,980 − $16,100 = $41,880 for a single filer in 2026.
3. Applies the tax brackets to that amount (10% on the first portion, 12% on the rest here) to get an annual tax of $4,777.60.
4. Divides by the number of paychecks: $4,777.60 ÷ 26 = **$183.75**.

That's 7.35% of gross pay, even though this employee is in the 12% bracket, because of the pre-tax deductions, the standard deduction and the lower 10% bracket. Extra withholding you request on your W-4, multiple jobs, or other income will change it.

### State and local taxes

Most states, and some cities and counties, also withhold income tax, and a few states have payroll taxes for disability or paid family leave, such as California's SDI. They appear as separate lines. Our example uses a state with no income tax, so there are none.

## After-tax deductions

These come out **after** taxes are calculated, so they don't reduce your tax:

- **Roth 401(k) contributions,** which are taxed now and withdrawn tax-free in retirement. See [Roth vs traditional 401(k)](/guides/roth-vs-traditional-401k/).
- **Some insurance,** such as supplemental life or disability coverage.
- **Union dues, charitable gifts** and **wage garnishments** such as child support.

## Net pay

**Net pay = gross pay − pre-tax deductions − taxes − after-tax deductions.**

Here: $2,500.00 − $150.00 − $120.00 − $183.75 − $147.56 − $34.51 = **$1,864.18**, about 75% of gross pay. The [paycheck calculator](/finance/paycheck-calculator/) does the same calculation for any salary, state and set of deductions.

Notice that of the $635.82 withheld, $270 goes to benefits that are still yours: the 401(k) and health coverage. The 401(k) money is a saving, not a cost; see what it could grow to with the [401(k) calculator](/finance/401k-calculator/).

## Year-to-date totals

The **YTD** column adds up every pay period since January 1. In our example, after 10 paychecks, it's simply 10 times each line. It changes when something changes during the year:

- **A raise or bonus** increases gross pay and the related taxes.
- **Social Security stops** once YTD Social Security wages pass $184,500.
- **Benefits changes** at open enrollment change the deductions.

Your last pay stub of the year should match your **Form W-2**: box 1 shows federal taxable wages (gross minus pre-tax deductions such as the 401(k)), and boxes 3 and 5 show Social Security and Medicare wages, which are higher because the 401(k) doesn't reduce them.

## Employer-paid benefits on your stub

Many pay stubs include a section for **employer contributions** or **employer-paid benefits**: your employer's 401(k) match, its share of health premiums, HSA contributions and group life insurance. These amounts don't come out of your pay and aren't included in your gross or net pay, but they're part of your total compensation. A 4% match on a $65,000 salary, for example, is $2,600 a year, or $100 a paycheck. One exception to watch: employer-paid group life insurance above $50,000 of coverage is a taxable fringe benefit, so a small "imputed income" amount may appear in your taxable wages.

## When you'll need your pay stubs

- **Mortgage and loan applications:** lenders usually ask for your most recent pay stubs, often covering 30 days, alongside W-2s. They check gross pay, year-to-date income and any deductions for debts such as garnishments.
- **Renting:** landlords often want proof that income is at least about three times the rent.
- **Checking your W-2** at the end of the year.
- **Applying for benefits** or resolving a wage dispute.

## A checklist for every pay stub

- **Hours and rate:** do they match your time records and your offer letter?
- **Overtime:** is it paid at 1.5 times, and does the rate include any bonuses?
- **Deductions:** do the 401(k) percentage and benefits match your elections?
- **Filing status and withholding:** do they reflect your current W-4? If you got a big refund or owed a lot last year, consider updating it.
- **Net pay:** does the deposit in your bank account match the stub?
- **Leave balances:** are you accruing paid time off as expected?

If something is wrong, contact payroll or HR promptly and in writing, and keep copies. Federal law generally allows two years to claim unpaid wages, or three for willful violations.

## Key terms

- **Gross pay:** earnings before any deductions.
- **Net pay:** the amount actually paid to you.
- **Withholding:** tax your employer takes out and sends to the government on your behalf.
- **FICA:** Social Security and Medicare taxes.
- **Pre-tax deduction:** an amount taken out before income tax is calculated.
- **W-4:** the form that tells your employer how much federal tax to withhold.
- **W-2:** the year-end form your employer sends showing your total wages and taxes withheld.
