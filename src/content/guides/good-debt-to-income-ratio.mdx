**Short answer:** a debt-to-income ratio of **35% or less** is generally considered good. For a mortgage, the classic guideline is **28% of gross income for housing and 36% for all debts**, but limits vary by loan: FHA allows **43%**, VA uses **41%**, and conventional loans can reach **45% to 50%** with strong credit and savings. The [debt-to-income ratio calculator](/finance/debt-to-income-ratio-calculator/) works out both of your ratios and checks them against each loan type.

## How DTI is calculated

Your debt-to-income ratio compares your required monthly debt payments with your gross monthly income:

**DTI = total monthly debt payments ÷ gross monthly income × 100**

Lenders look at two versions:

- **Front-end ratio:** housing costs only: mortgage principal and interest, property tax, homeowners insurance, mortgage insurance and HOA dues.
- **Back-end ratio:** housing plus every other required debt payment. When people say "DTI," they usually mean this one.

## What counts as a good DTI

| Back-end DTI | What it usually means |
|---|---|
| 35% or less | Good. Debt is manageable and lenders see room for new credit. |
| 36% to 43% | Acceptable for many loans, but it narrows your options. |
| 44% to 49% | High. Approval may need strong credit, savings or a larger down payment. |
| 50% or more | Very high. Most lenders will decline new credit, and your budget is likely stretched. |

These bands aren't official rules; each lender sets its own. But they reflect how most lenders and financial counselors read the number.

## DTI limits by mortgage type

| Loan type | Housing (front-end) | All debts (back-end) | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Conventional | 28% | 36% | Fannie Mae allows up to 45% manually and 50% through Desktop Underwriter |
| FHA | 31% | 43% | Higher with compensating factors such as cash reserves |
| VA | none | 41% | Also requires enough residual income after expenses |
| USDA | 29% | 41% | For eligible rural and suburban areas |

Since 2021, the CFPB's General Qualified Mortgage rule no longer sets a hard 43% DTI cap; it uses limits on the loan's price instead. Lenders still must consider DTI, and 43% remains a common internal benchmark, but there's no single legal maximum for all mortgages.

## How much debt fits your income

To turn the percentages into dollars, multiply your gross monthly income by each limit:

| Gross annual income | Monthly income | Housing at 28% | All debts at 36% | All debts at 43% |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| $60,000 | $5,000.00 | $1,400.00 | $1,800.00 | $2,150.00 |
| $90,000 | $7,500.00 | $2,100.00 | $2,700.00 | $3,225.00 |
| $120,000 | $10,000.00 | $2,800.00 | $3,600.00 | $4,300.00 |

Whatever your other debts take up comes out of the all-debts column first. On $90,000, a $600 car payment and $300 of student loans leave $1,800 for housing under the 36% rule, less than the $2,100 the housing rule alone would allow.

## Why lenders care so much about DTI

DTI is one of the clearest predictors of whether a borrower can keep up with a new payment. When most of your income is already committed, a job loss, medical bill or rate increase can quickly make payments unmanageable. That's why DTI sits alongside your credit score and down payment as one of the three main factors in nearly every lending decision, and why lenders recalculate it themselves rather than relying on your estimate.

## Worked example: Priya's mortgage application

Priya earns $72,000 a year, which is $6,000 a month before tax. The home she wants would cost $1,650 a month including taxes and insurance. She also pays $410 on a car loan, $260 on student loans and $95 in credit card minimums.

- **Total monthly debts:** $1,650 + $410 + $260 + $95 = **$2,415**
- **Front-end ratio:** $1,650 ÷ $6,000 = **27.5%**
- **Back-end ratio:** $2,415 ÷ $6,000 = **40.25%**

Her housing ratio fits every program, but her total DTI of 40.25% is above the conventional 36% guideline. It does meet FHA's 43%, VA's 41% and USDA's 41%. To qualify for a conventional loan at 36%, she'd need to cut $255 a month of debt payments or choose a home costing no more than $1,395 a month.

Here's how different moves would change her back-end ratio:

| Change | New DTI |
|---|---|
| Pay off the credit card balances | 38.67% |
| Pay off the car loan | 33.42% |
| Choose a $1,400-a-month home instead | 36.08% |
| Add a co-borrower earning $30,000 | 28.41% |

Paying off the car loan is the single biggest lever, because it removes a $410 payment entirely. If she can't do that before applying, an FHA loan would accept her ratio as it stands.

## What counts as debt, and what doesn't

**Included:**
- The new or current mortgage payment, with taxes, insurance, mortgage insurance and HOA dues
- Car loans and leases
- Student loans, including some payment even if they're deferred
- Minimum payments on credit cards and store cards
- Personal loans, buy-now-pay-later plans with set payments, and any co-signed loans
- Child support and alimony you pay

**Not included:**
- Utilities, phone and internet bills
- Groceries, transportation and childcare costs
- Car, health and life insurance premiums
- Income taxes and payroll deductions
- Subscriptions and other living expenses

That's why DTI isn't a full budget. A household with a 30% DTI can still struggle if childcare or commuting costs are high.

## How lenders treat specific debts

Mortgage underwriters follow detailed rules that can move your ratio by several points:

- **Deferred or income-driven student loans.** If your credit report shows a $0 payment, lenders often still count a payment: either the documented income-driven amount or a percentage of the balance. FHA, for example, uses 0.5% of the outstanding balance when no payment is reported.
- **Credit cards paid in full.** The minimum payment on your statement still counts, even if you never carry a balance.
- **Installment loans close to payoff.** Conventional guidelines usually let lenders ignore a loan with ten or fewer payments left, unless the payment is large.
- **Co-signed debts.** They count as yours unless you can show the other borrower has made the payments for the past year.
- **Authorized-user cards.** Often excluded if you're not responsible for the debt, but lender policies differ.

## Gross income vs take-home pay

DTI uses gross income, but you pay bills from take-home pay. For Priya, $2,415 is 40% of her gross income but a much larger share of what reaches her bank account once federal and state taxes, Social Security, Medicare and any retirement contributions come out. Check your real take-home pay in the [paycheck calculator](/finance/paycheck-calculator/) and make sure the payments fit that number, not just the lender's ratio.

## How to lower your DTI

1. **Pay off small debts completely.** A paid-off debt drops out of the ratio entirely; partial payments on several debts may not change their minimums.
2. **Target the largest payment you can eliminate.** In Priya's case, the car loan.
3. **Avoid new debt before applying.** A new car or store card adds a payment and a hard inquiry at the worst time.
4. **Lower credit card balances** to reduce the minimum payments lenders see.
5. **Document all your income,** including steady overtime, bonuses or side income, which usually needs a two-year history.
6. **Consider a co-borrower,** whose income and debts are both counted.
7. **Look at a less expensive home or a larger down payment,** which shrinks the housing payment itself.

The [debt payoff calculator](/finance/debt-payoff-calculator/) shows the fastest order to clear several debts, and the [mortgage affordability calculator](/finance/mortgage-affordability-calculator/) turns your ratios into a home price.

## DTI vs credit utilization

These are different measures that people often confuse. **DTI** compares your monthly debt payments with your income and is used by lenders to judge whether you can afford a new payment. **Credit utilization** compares your credit card balances with your credit limits and is part of your credit score. Paying down credit cards helps both: it lowers your minimum payments and your utilization at the same time.

The two can also move in opposite directions. Closing a paid-off card doesn't change your DTI, since there's no payment left, but it removes that card's limit and can raise your utilization. Taking out a consolidation loan to pay off cards may lower your utilization sharply while leaving your DTI roughly where it was, because a new monthly payment replaces the old minimums.

## DTI for other kinds of borrowing

Debt-to-income ratio matters beyond mortgages. Auto lenders and personal loan lenders often look for a DTI below somewhere in the 40% to 50% range, including the new payment. Card issuers consider your ability to pay before setting a credit limit. And many landlords use a simpler version, wanting your gross income to be at least three times the rent.

## A quick checklist before you apply

- Calculate both ratios with the new housing payment included.
- Compare them with the limits for the loan types you're considering.
- Check your credit reports for debts you've forgotten or errors that add payments.
- Clear any small balances you can.
- Gather two years of income documentation.
- Leave room below the limit; lenders often calculate a slightly higher DTI than you do, especially with student loans or co-signed debts.

Once your ratio is where you want it, model the full payment of a specific home in the [mortgage calculator](/finance/mortgage-calculator/).
