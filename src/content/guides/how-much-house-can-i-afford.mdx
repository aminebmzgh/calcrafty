**Short answer:** most lenders will let you spend up to about **28% of your gross monthly income** on a mortgage payment (including property tax and insurance) and up to **36% on all debts combined**. In practice, that puts the affordable home price at roughly **3 to 3.5 times your annual income** at today's rates, before accounting for other debts, local taxes and your down payment. The [mortgage affordability calculator](/finance/mortgage-affordability-calculator/) works out your exact number.

## How much house can I afford by income?

These figures assume a 30-year fixed loan at 7%, a down payment of 40% of annual income, 1% property tax, $1,800 a year of homeowners insurance, PMI when the down payment is under 20%, and $400 a month of other debt payments.

| Household income | Down payment | Maximum home price | Monthly payment |
|---|---|---|---|
| $60,000 | $24,000 | about $178,000 | $1,400 |
| $90,000 | $36,000 | about $276,000 | $2,100 |
| $120,000 | $48,000 | about $375,000 | $2,800 |
| $150,000 | $60,000 | about $473,000 | $3,500 |

In each case the 28% housing ratio is the limit. The monthly payment is exactly 28% of gross monthly income, and it includes principal, interest, property tax, insurance and PMI.

## The rules lenders use

Lenders look at two debt-to-income (DTI) ratios:

- **Front-end ratio:** your total housing payment divided by gross monthly income. The traditional maximum is 28%.
- **Back-end ratio:** housing plus all other monthly debt payments (car, student loans, card minimums) divided by gross monthly income. The traditional maximum is 36%; many programs allow 43% or more.

Your housing budget is the lower of the two limits. With $90,000 of income, 28% is $2,100 a month. If you also had a $900 car payment, the 36% rule would allow only $2,700 − $900 = $1,800 for housing, and the affordable price would fall from about $276,000 to about $239,000.

## What changes your budget most

**Interest rate.** A lower rate means more of each payment goes to the home instead of interest. For the $90,000 household:

| Rate | Maximum home price |
|---|---|
| 5.5% | about $310,000 |
| 6.5% | about $287,000 |
| 7.5% | about $267,000 |

Improving your credit score and comparing several lenders on the same day can be worth tens of thousands of dollars of buying power.

**Other debts.** Every dollar of monthly debt reduces the housing budget dollar for dollar once the 36% rule binds. Paying off a car loan before applying can raise your limit noticeably.

**Down payment.** A bigger down payment shrinks the loan and, at 20%, removes PMI. Plan it with the [savings goal calculator](/finance/savings-goal-calculator/).

**Location.** Property tax rates vary several-fold between states and counties, and insurance costs vary even more. A 2% property tax rate instead of 1% can cut the affordable price by around 10%.

## What income do you need for a given price?

Working the other way: here's the monthly payment for common home prices with 10% down at 7%, 1% property tax, $1,800 a year of insurance and 0.6% PMI, and the gross income at which that payment equals 28% of income:

| Home price | Principal and interest | Total monthly payment | Income needed at 28% |
|---|---|---|---|
| $250,000 | $1,496.93 | $1,967.76 | about $84,300 |
| $350,000 | $2,095.70 | $2,694.87 | about $115,500 |
| $450,000 | $2,694.48 | $3,421.98 | about $146,700 |
| $550,000 | $3,293.25 | $4,149.08 | about $177,800 |

Taxes, insurance and PMI add $470 to $860 a month on top of principal and interest at these prices, which is why the purchase price alone understates the cost of a home. Any other monthly debts raise the income you'd need under the 36% rule.

## Low down payment options for first-time buyers

You don't necessarily need 20% down. Common programs:

- **Conventional loans with 3% down,** such as Fannie Mae's HomeReady and Freddie Mac's Home Possible, for eligible buyers, with PMI until you reach enough equity.
- **FHA loans** with 3.5% down for credit scores of 580 or more. They carry mortgage insurance premiums, usually for the life of the loan when you put less than 10% down.
- **VA loans** for eligible service members, veterans and some surviving spouses, with no down payment and no monthly mortgage insurance.
- **USDA loans** for eligible rural and suburban areas, with no down payment, subject to income limits.
- **State and local down payment assistance,** usually through your state's housing finance agency, offering grants or low-cost second loans for first-time buyers.

A smaller down payment gets you into a home sooner, but it means a larger loan, mortgage insurance and less equity if prices dip. Run both versions in the [mortgage calculator](/finance/mortgage-calculator/) to see the difference in monthly cost.

## The ongoing costs of owning

The mortgage payment is the cost lenders measure, but owning a home comes with bills that renters don't pay:

- **Maintenance and repairs.** A common rule of thumb is 1% to 2% of the home's value a year, more for older homes. On a $350,000 home, 1% is $3,500 a year, about $292 a month.
- **Homeowners association (HOA) dues.** Condos, townhouses and many newer neighborhoods charge monthly dues that lenders count in your housing payment. Ask what they cover and whether any special assessments are planned.
- **Utilities.** A larger home, or a house instead of an apartment, usually means higher heating, cooling and water bills, plus services such as trash collection that a landlord may have covered.
- **Rising taxes and insurance.** Property taxes are reassessed periodically, and insurance premiums have risen sharply in some regions. Because both are usually paid through escrow, your monthly payment can increase even on a fixed-rate loan.

Adding these to the payment before you set a price gives a far more honest picture of what the home will cost to live in.

## Should you wait for lower prices or rates?

Many buyers try to time the market, waiting for prices or mortgage rates to fall. Both are hard to predict, and they often move in opposite directions: when rates drop, more buyers can afford to bid, which can push prices up.

A more reliable approach is to buy when your own situation is ready: steady income, a down payment and cash cushion saved, manageable debts, and a plan to stay in the area for at least five years or so. If rates fall significantly after you buy, refinancing may lower your payment later, as long as the savings outweigh the closing costs.

## Stress-test the budget

Before committing, check how the payment holds up if things change:

- **On one income.** If you're buying with a partner, could you keep paying for a few months if one income stopped?
- **With a big repair.** Could you cover a $5,000 roof or furnace repair without a credit card?
- **When taxes and insurance rise.** Both tend to go up over time, and escrow payments rise with them.

A home you can afford in a bad month is one you'll enjoy owning in a good one.

It also helps to look at the payment as a share of take-home pay rather than gross income. A payment that's 28% of gross income is often 35% to 40% of what actually reaches your bank account after taxes, retirement contributions and health insurance. If that share feels uncomfortable, it probably is; lower the price until it doesn't, or wait until your savings or income catch up with the homes you want.

## From budget to keys: the order of steps

1. Check your credit reports and fix any errors.
2. Work out a comfortable payment from your take-home pay.
3. Save the down payment, closing costs and a cushion.
4. Get pre-approved by two or three lenders.
5. Shop for homes within the comfortable price, not the pre-approval maximum, and get an inspection before you commit.
6. Compare Loan Estimates, lock a rate, do a final walk-through and close.

## A better question: what payment fits your life?

The lender's maximum is based on gross income. Your real budget is based on take-home pay and everything else you spend money on. A practical approach:

1. Find your monthly take-home pay with the [paycheck calculator](/finance/paycheck-calculator/).
2. Subtract fixed costs (transport, childcare, insurance, debt payments) and savings goals (retirement, emergency fund).
3. Decide what's left for housing, including about 1% of the home's value each year for maintenance.
4. Use that payment in the [mortgage calculator](/finance/mortgage-calculator/) to see the price it supports.

If that number is lower than the lender's maximum, shop at your number. Being "house poor," with most of your income going to the mortgage, makes every other goal harder.

## Don't forget the cash you need

Beyond the down payment, budget for:

- **Closing costs:** typically 2% to 5% of the price for lender fees, title, appraisal and prepaid taxes and insurance.
- **Moving and setup:** movers, furniture, utility deposits.
- **An emergency fund:** a new home brings new repairs. Keeping several months of expenses in savings protects you from putting them on a credit card.

## Should you buy at all?

If you might move within a few years, the costs of buying and selling can outweigh the equity you build. The [rent vs. buy calculator](/finance/rent-vs-buy-calculator/) compares your net worth under each choice and shows the year buying breaks even.
