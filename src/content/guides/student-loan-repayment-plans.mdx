## The plans at a glance

The 2025 budget law (P.L. 119-21) rebuilt federal student loan repayment. Which plans you can use now depends mainly on when your loans were first disbursed:

| Plan | Payment based on | Term | Loans before July 1, 2026 | Loans on or after July 1, 2026 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Standard | Balance (level) | 10 years | Yes | No |
| Graduated | Balance (rising) | 10 years | Yes | No |
| Extended | Balance (level or rising) | 25 years, over $30,000 owed | Yes | No |
| Tiered Standard | Balance (level) | 10–25 years by balance | No | Yes |
| Income-Based Repayment (IBR) | Income | 20–25 years | Yes | No |
| Repayment Assistance Plan (RAP) | Income | Up to 30 years | Yes, by choice | Yes |

The older income-driven plans, SAVE, PAYE and ICR, are being closed, with remaining borrowers moved to IBR or RAP by July 2028. Taking out a new loan or consolidating after July 1, 2026 can move you onto the new set of plans, so check before you borrow again or consolidate.

To see what the fixed plans cost for your balance, use the [student loan calculator](/finance/student-loan-calculator/).

## Fixed plans: pay off the balance on a schedule

Fixed plans set your payment from the balance, rate and term. Your income doesn't matter. They're predictable and, the shorter the term, the cheaper. For $35,000 at 6.52%:

| Plan | Monthly payment | Total interest |
|---|---|---|
| Standard, 10 years | $397.77 | $12,733.13 |
| Graduated, 10 years (estimate) | $228.75 rising to $686.20 | $16,207.59 |
| Tiered Standard, 15 years | $305.27 | $19,949.23 |
| Extended, 25 years | $236.76 | $36,027.99 |

The **Tiered Standard** plan, for new loans, sets the term by your balance when you enter repayment: 10 years under $25,000, 15 years under $50,000, 20 years under $100,000 and 25 years for $100,000 or more. Larger balances get a longer term and a lower payment, but more interest. Because there's no prepayment penalty, you can always pay more than the minimum and finish sooner.

The **Graduated** plan starts with lower payments that rise every two years, suited to people who expect their income to grow steadily. The **Extended** plan stretches payments over 25 years if you owe more than $30,000; it lowers the payment the most among fixed plans and costs the most interest.

## Income-based plans: pay a share of what you earn

Income-based plans set the payment from your income and family size, and forgive any balance left at the end of the term. They protect you when your income is low relative to your debt.

### Repayment Assistance Plan (RAP)

RAP is the income-based plan for new loans, and borrowers with older loans can choose it too. The main features:

- **Payment:** a percentage of your adjusted gross income (AGI), starting at 1% for incomes just over $10,000 and rising one point for each additional $10,000 of income, up to 10% above $100,000. The annual amount is divided by 12.
- **Dependents:** the payment is reduced by $50 a month for each dependent.
- **Minimum:** $10 a month; there are no $0 payments.
- **Interest:** if your payment doesn't cover the month's interest, the unpaid interest is waived, so the balance doesn't grow when you pay on time.
- **Principal:** if your payment reduces principal by less than $50, the government adds a matching amount, up to $50, so the balance always falls.
- **Forgiveness:** any remaining balance is forgiven after 360 qualifying payments, or 30 years.

**Example (estimate):** a single borrower with an AGI of $55,000 and no dependents falls in the 5% tier: $55,000 × 5% = $2,750 a year, or about $229.17 a month. With one dependent, it's about $179.17. On a $35,000 balance at 6.52%, that's less than the $397.77 Standard payment, but because the payment barely exceeds the monthly interest of $190.17, the balance falls slowly. As income rises, so does the payment.

### Income-Based Repayment (IBR)

IBR remains available for loans made before July 1, 2026. Payments are 10% or 15% of discretionary income (income above 150% of the federal poverty guideline for your family size), depending on when you first borrowed, and never more than the 10-year Standard payment. Remaining balances are forgiven after 20 or 25 years.

## How to choose

Start with two questions: how big is your debt compared with your income, and are you aiming for forgiveness?

- **Debt below your annual income, and no plans for forgiveness.** A fixed plan, ideally paid off in about 10 years, usually costs the least. If the required payment is tight, take a longer term and pay extra when you can.
- **Debt well above your income.** An income-based plan keeps payments affordable and protects you if your income falls. You may pay more interest over time, but forgiveness can cover what's left.
- **Working in public service.** If you work for a government or nonprofit employer, Public Service Loan Forgiveness can forgive the balance after 120 qualifying payments, about 10 years. An income-based plan keeps those payments low, which leaves more to be forgiven.
- **Income expected to rise fast.** A doctor or lawyer early in their career might start on an income-based plan and switch to paying the loan off quickly once their income grows.

Check the payment against your budget. The [paycheck calculator](/finance/paycheck-calculator/) shows your take-home pay, and lenders will look at your [debt-to-income ratio](/finance/debt-to-income-ratio-calculator/) when you apply for a mortgage or car loan.

## A side-by-side example

Take a new graduate with $35,000 of undergraduate loans at 6.52% and a starting AGI of $55,000:

| Plan | First payment | Payoff | What happens |
|---|---|---|---|
| Tiered Standard | $305.27 | 15 years | Same payment every month; $19,949.23 interest |
| Tiered Standard, paid in 10 years | $397.77 | 10 years | About $93 above the minimum; $12,733.13 interest |
| RAP | about $229 | Depends on income | Payment rises with income; slower payoff |

Neither choice is wrong. The fixed plan costs more each month but is finished in a known time. RAP gives breathing room now and adjusts if income drops, at the cost of a longer, less predictable payoff.

## Before and during repayment

**The grace period.** Most Direct Loans give you six months after you leave school or drop below half-time before the first payment is due. Interest keeps building on unsubsidized and PLUS loans during school and grace. Paying that interest as it accrues, even in small amounts, keeps it from being added to your balance when repayment begins.

**Recertifying income every year.** On income-based plans, your payment is reset each year from your latest income and family size. Keep your consent for the Department of Education to get your tax information current, or recertify on time; otherwise your payment can jump to a much higher amount until you do.

**Keeping records.** Save your repayment plan confirmations, your payment history and, if you're aiming for PSLF, your employment certifications. Servicers change, and records make it easier to fix mistakes in your payment count.

## Paying more than the minimum

Every federal plan lets you pay extra with no penalty. Extra money goes first to any interest that has built up since your last payment, then to principal. Adding $100 a month to a $35,000 Standard plan loan at 6.52% finishes 2 years 7 months early and saves $3,516.84.

Tell your servicer to apply extra payments to principal, not to advance your next due date. If you have several loans, put the extra on the highest-rate loan first. When you also have credit card or private loan debt, the [debt payoff calculator](/finance/debt-payoff-calculator/) helps decide which to pay first.

## Consolidation

A Direct Consolidation Loan combines several federal loans into one, at the weighted average of their rates rounded up to the next one-eighth of a percent. It simplifies payments and can make some older loans, such as certain Parent PLUS or FFEL loans, eligible for more plans. But consolidating after July 1, 2026 can move your loans onto the new set of plans and may affect progress toward forgiveness, so check the effect on your plan options before applying.

Private refinancing is different: a private lender pays off your federal loans with a new private loan. You may get a lower rate, but you permanently lose federal protections such as income-based plans, deferment, forbearance and forgiveness programs.

## If you're struggling to pay

Contact your servicer before you miss a payment. Options include switching to an income-based plan, deferment and forbearance. Missed payments make a loan delinquent; after 270 days it goes into default, which can lead to wage garnishment, seizure of tax refunds and serious credit damage. Getting back to good standing is possible through rehabilitation or consolidation, but it's far easier to avoid default in the first place.

## Key terms

- **Adjusted gross income (AGI):** your income after certain deductions, from your federal tax return; income-based plans use it.
- **Discretionary income:** for IBR, income above 150% of the poverty guideline for your family size.
- **Forgiveness:** cancellation of the remaining balance after the required number of qualifying payments.
- **Servicer:** the company that handles your federal loan payments.
- **Consolidation:** combining several federal loans into one Direct Consolidation Loan.
- **Tiered Standard plan:** the fixed plan for loans first disbursed on or after July 1, 2026.
