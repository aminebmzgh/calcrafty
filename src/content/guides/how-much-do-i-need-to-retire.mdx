**Short answer:** figure out the yearly income you'll want, subtract what Social Security and any pension will cover, and multiply the gap by about **25**. That rule of thumb, the flip side of the "4% rule," gives a rough nest egg in today's dollars. For a precise answer that accounts for your age, savings, returns and inflation, use the [retirement calculator](/finance/retirement-calculator/).

## Step 1: Estimate the income you'll need

A common starting point is **70% to 80% of your income before retirement**. Some costs disappear when you stop working: commuting, payroll taxes and retirement contributions. Others grow, such as healthcare and travel. Build your own estimate from current spending if you can: housing (is your mortgage paid off?), food, transport, insurance, healthcare and the things you want to do.

## Step 2: Subtract guaranteed income

Social Security is the foundation for most Americans. Your benefit depends on your 35 highest-earning years and the age you claim: claiming at 62 permanently reduces it, while waiting past full retirement age (67 for anyone born in 1960 or later) increases it up to age 70. Get your estimate at ssa.gov/myaccount and include any pension or annuity income.

The yearly gap your savings must cover is: **income you want − Social Security − pensions**.

## Step 3: Turn the gap into a savings target

Your savings need to pay that gap every year for as long as retirement lasts, rising with inflation, while the rest stays invested. Assuming retirement at 67, money lasting to 92, a 4.5% return during retirement and 2.5% inflation, here's the nest egg needed at 67, in today's dollars, for different income targets with $2,000 a month of Social Security:

| Income wanted | Gap after Social Security | Nest egg needed |
|---|---|---|
| $50,000 | $26,000 | about $520,000 |
| $75,000 | $51,000 | about $1,021,000 |
| $100,000 | $76,000 | about $1,521,000 |

Notice the multiplier: each target is about **20 times** the yearly gap under these assumptions. The popular **25× rule** (the 4% rule) is more conservative because it aims for 30 years or longer and allows for poor market sequences.

## The 4% rule in context

The 4% rule comes from studies of historical US stock and bond returns: a 4% first-year withdrawal, raised each year with inflation, survived most 30-year periods. It's a useful benchmark, but:

- It assumes a balanced portfolio and no big fees.
- Retiring early means a longer retirement and a lower safe rate.
- Poor returns in the first few years of retirement, known as sequence-of-returns risk, hurt the most.
- Being flexible, such as spending less after a bad year, makes savings last longer.

## Step 4: Check whether you're on track

Your future savings depend on what you have now, what you add, and time. Money invested in your 30s has decades to compound; the same amount in your 50s has far less. The [investment calculator](/finance/investment-calculator/) shows how contributions grow, and the [compound interest calculator](/finance/compound-interest-calculator/) shows why starting early matters so much.

If the retirement calculator shows a shortfall, it also gives the monthly saving that would close it.

## Levers if you're behind

- **Save more, starting with the employer match.** It's an immediate return on your money. The 2026 401(k) limit is $24,500, plus catch-up contributions from age 50.
- **Work longer.** Each extra year adds savings and growth and shortens the time savings must last.
- **Delay Social Security.** Benefits rise for each year you wait past full retirement age, up to 70.
- **Reduce expected spending.** Paying off a mortgage before retiring lowers the income you need.
- **Keep fees low.** A 1% difference in fees compounds into a large difference over decades.

Raising contributions is easier than it looks, because traditional 401(k) contributions are pre-tax; the [paycheck calculator](/finance/paycheck-calculator/) shows how little a 1% increase changes your take-home pay.
