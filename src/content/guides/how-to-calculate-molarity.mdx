**Short answer:** molarity is **moles of solute divided by liters of solution**: **M = mol ÷ L**. If you know the mass of the solute, first convert it to moles by dividing by the molar mass. For example, 11.7 g of sodium chloride (58.44 g/mol) is 0.200 mol; dissolved and made up to 2.00 L, the solution is 0.200 ÷ 2.00 = **0.100 M**. The [molarity calculator](/chemistry/molarity-calculator/) does this for any compound and also finds the mass to weigh or the volume to make up.

## The molarity formula

**Molarity (M) = moles of solute ÷ liters of solution**

Three things matter in that definition:

- **Moles of solute:** the amount of the dissolved substance, not its mass.
- **Liters:** the volume in liters, not milliliters.
- **Of solution:** the final volume of the whole mixture, not the volume of water you started with.

The unit is moles per liter (mol/L), written **M** and read as "molar". A 0.5 M solution is "half molar".

## Step 1: find the molar mass

The molar mass is the mass of one mole of a substance, in grams per mole. Add up the atomic weights of every atom in the formula, using a periodic table or standard atomic weights:

- **Sodium chloride, NaCl:** 22.990 + 35.45 = **58.44 g/mol**.
- **Glucose, C₆H₁₂O₆:** 6 × 12.011 + 12 × 1.008 + 6 × 15.999 = **180.16 g/mol**.
- **Calcium chloride, CaCl₂:** 40.078 + 2 × 35.45 = **110.98 g/mol**.

Watch for **hydrates**, which have water molecules built into the crystal. Calcium chloride dihydrate, CaCl₂·2H₂O, has a molar mass of about 147.01 g/mol, not 110.98. Use the formula on the bottle.

## Step 2: convert grams to moles

**Moles = mass (g) ÷ molar mass (g/mol)**

11.7 g of NaCl is 11.7 ÷ 58.44 = **0.200 mol**.

If the problem already gives you moles, skip this step.

## Step 3: convert the volume to liters

**Liters = milliliters ÷ 1,000**

250 mL = 0.250 L; 2,000 mL = 2.00 L.

## Step 4: divide

**M = moles ÷ liters**

0.200 mol ÷ 2.00 L = **0.100 M**.

That's all there is to it. Most molarity problems are these four steps in some order.

## Rearranging the formula

The same relationship can be solved for any of its parts:

- **Moles = M × liters.** 40.0 mL of 0.150 M solution contains 0.150 × 0.0400 = **0.00600 mol**.
- **Mass = M × liters × molar mass.** To make 750 mL of 0.250 M calcium chloride: 0.250 × 0.750 = 0.1875 mol, × 110.98 = **20.81 g**.
- **Liters = moles ÷ M.** 2.00 g of NaOH (40.00 g/mol) is 0.0500 mol; to make 0.500 M, the volume is 0.0500 ÷ 0.500 = 0.100 L, or **100 mL**.

## Preparing a solution in the lab

To make a solution of known molarity:

1. **Calculate the mass** of solute you need: M × L × molar mass.
2. **Weigh it** accurately on a balance.
3. **Dissolve it** in a beaker with less water than the final volume.
4. **Transfer** it to a volumetric flask of the right size, rinsing the beaker into the flask.
5. **Fill to the mark** with water, so the bottom of the meniscus touches the line.
6. **Mix** by capping and inverting the flask several times.

The reason for dissolving first and then making up to volume is that the solute itself takes up space. Adding 58.44 g of NaCl to exactly 1 L of water gives slightly more than 1 L of solution, so the concentration would be a little under 1 M.

## Dilutions: M₁V₁ = M₂V₂

When you add water to a solution, the moles of solute don't change; only the volume does. So:

**M₁ × V₁ = M₂ × V₂**

where M₁ and V₁ are the stock solution's molarity and the volume you take, and M₂ and V₂ are the diluted solution's molarity and final volume. Both volumes can be in milliliters, as long as they're in the same unit.

**Example:** you need 500 mL of 0.100 M HCl from a 1.00 M stock.

V₁ = M₂ × V₂ ÷ M₁ = 0.100 × 500 ÷ 1.00 = **50.0 mL** of stock, made up to 500 mL.

**Example:** 25.0 mL of 6.00 M HCl is diluted to 300 mL. The new concentration is 6.00 × 25.0 ÷ 300 = **0.500 M**.

When diluting acids, always add acid to water, never water to concentrated acid, to avoid dangerous spattering.

## Molarity from percent and density

Concentrated reagents are often labeled with a percentage by mass and a density rather than a molarity. To convert:

**M = density (g/mL) × 1,000 × (percent ÷ 100) ÷ molar mass**

Concentrated hydrochloric acid at 37% by mass and 1.19 g/mL is 1.19 × 1,000 × 0.37 ÷ 36.46 ≈ **12.1 M**.

For solutions labeled in percent weight per volume (grams per 100 mL), multiply by 10 to get grams per liter, then divide by the molar mass. Normal saline, 0.9% NaCl, is 9 g/L ÷ 58.44 = **0.154 M**.

## Molarity in reactions

Molarity turns volumes of solution into moles, which is what balanced equations use. In a titration, 25.0 mL of 0.100 M NaOH contains 0.100 × 0.0250 = 0.00250 mol. If it exactly neutralizes 20.0 mL of hydrochloric acid, which reacts one to one, the acid contained 0.00250 mol, and its molarity is 0.00250 ÷ 0.0200 = **0.125 M**.

If the reaction ratio isn't one to one, multiply by the ratio from the balanced equation. Sulfuric acid, H₂SO₄, reacts with two moles of NaOH for every mole of acid, so it takes twice as much base.

## A checklist for any molarity problem

1. **Write down what you know and what you need,** with units: grams, moles, milliliters, liters or molarity.
2. **Find the molar mass** if any quantity is in grams.
3. **Convert grams to moles** and milliliters to liters before doing anything else.
4. **Choose the right form of the formula:** M = mol ÷ L, mol = M × L, or L = mol ÷ M.
5. **For a dilution, use M₁V₁ = M₂V₂** and keep both volumes in the same unit.
6. **Check that the answer is sensible.** A teaspoon of salt in a glass of water shouldn't come out at 50 M, and a dilution should always give a lower concentration than the stock.
7. **Round to the right number of significant figures** and include the unit, M or mol/L.

## Practice problems

Try these, then check the answers below.

1. What is the molarity of 11.7 g of NaCl dissolved to make 2.00 L of solution?
2. How many grams of CaCl₂ are needed for 750 mL of a 0.250 M solution?
3. 25.0 mL of 6.00 M HCl is diluted to 300 mL. What is the new molarity?
4. How many moles of solute are in 40.0 mL of a 0.150 M solution?
5. What volume of 0.500 M solution can be made from 2.00 g of NaOH?

**Answers:** (1) 0.200 mol ÷ 2.00 L = **0.100 M**. (2) 0.1875 mol × 110.98 g/mol = **20.8 g**. (3) 6.00 × 25.0 ÷ 300 = **0.500 M**. (4) 0.150 × 0.0400 = **0.00600 mol**. (5) 0.0500 mol ÷ 0.500 M = **0.100 L (100 mL)**.

## Common mistakes

- **Using milliliters as liters.** Always divide mL by 1,000.
- **Using the mass instead of moles.** Molarity needs moles; divide grams by the molar mass first.
- **Using the volume of water, not solution.** The definition is per liter of solution.
- **Wrong molar mass,** especially for hydrates or similar-sounding compounds.
- **Rounding too early.** Keep extra digits until the final answer, then round to the right number of significant figures.

## Concentration of ions

Ionic compounds split into ions when they dissolve, and each ion has its own concentration. In 0.100 M NaCl, both Na⁺ and Cl⁻ are 0.100 M. In 0.100 M CaCl₂, Ca²⁺ is 0.100 M but Cl⁻ is **0.200 M**, because each formula unit releases two chloride ions. Problems about ions, osmotic pressure or precipitation often need these ion concentrations rather than the concentration of the compound.

## Molarity and pH

For a strong acid such as hydrochloric acid, which ionizes completely, the hydrogen-ion concentration equals the acid's molarity, and the pH is the negative logarithm of it:

**pH = −log₁₀[H⁺]**

A 0.0100 M HCl solution has [H⁺] = 0.0100 M, so its pH is −log₁₀(0.0100) = **2.00**. Diluting it tenfold to 0.00100 M raises the pH to 3.00. Weak acids such as acetic acid only partly ionize, so their pH needs the acid's dissociation constant as well.

## Significant figures

Report molarity with the precision your measurements support. If you weighed 11.7 g (three significant figures) and used a 2.00 L flask (three significant figures), the answer 0.100 M has three significant figures too. Intermediate results can keep more digits; round only the final answer. Lab balances and volumetric glassware usually justify three or four significant figures.

## Molarity vs molality

**Molality** (m) is moles of solute per **kilogram of solvent**, not per liter of solution. Because it's based on mass, it doesn't change with temperature, which makes it the preferred unit for boiling-point and freezing-point calculations. For dilute water solutions near room temperature, molarity and molality are close in value.

## Key terms

- **Solute:** the substance dissolved.
- **Solvent:** the substance it's dissolved in, usually water.
- **Mole:** 6.022 × 10²³ particles of a substance.
- **Molar mass:** the mass of one mole, in g/mol.
- **Stock solution:** a concentrated solution diluted to make working solutions.
- **Volumetric flask:** glassware calibrated to contain an exact volume at its mark.
- **Titration:** adding a solution of known molarity to find the concentration of another.
