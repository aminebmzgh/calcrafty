**Short answer:** paying only the minimum on a credit card usually takes **10 to 30 years**, depending on your balance, rate and your issuer's formula, and can cost **more in interest than you originally borrowed**. A $5,000 balance at 22% APR takes about **19 years** and **$8,100 of interest** at the minimum. Switching to a fixed $200 a month clears it in under three years. The [credit card payoff calculator](/finance/credit-card-payoff-calculator/) shows your exact numbers.

## How long minimum payments take, by balance

These examples assume a 22% APR and a common minimum payment formula: that month's interest plus 1% of the balance, with a $25 floor, and no new charges.

| Balance | Time to pay off | Total interest |
|---|---|---|
| $1,000 | 70 months (5 years 10 months) | $766.51 |
| $2,500 | 161 months (13 years 5 months) | $3,516.48 |
| $5,000 | 230 months (19 years 2 months) | $8,099.69 |
| $10,000 | 299 months (24 years 11 months) | $17,266.56 |
| $20,000 | 368 months (30 years 8 months) | $35,599.68 |

At $10,000 and above, the interest is larger than the original balance. In effect, the minimum payment turns a credit card into a 25- or 30-year loan at a rate far above any mortgage.

## How the APR changes the timeline

On a $5,000 balance with the same minimum payment formula:

| APR | Time to pay off | Total interest |
|---|---|---|
| 18% | 222 months | $6,538.62 |
| 22% | 230 months | $8,099.69 |
| 26% | 238 months | $9,677.58 |
| 30% | 245 months | $11,268.34 |

A higher rate adds less time than you might expect, because this formula always includes the month's interest, but it adds a lot of cost: going from 18% to 30% raises the interest by about $4,700.

## Why minimum payments take so long

On the $5,000 balance at 22%, the first month's interest is $91.67. The minimum is that interest plus 1% of the balance, $50, for a payment of **$141.67**. Only $50 of it reduces what you owe.

Next month, the balance is a little lower, so both the interest and the 1% are a little lower, and so is the payment. The payment keeps shrinking along with the balance, and progress slows to a crawl:

| After | Balance remaining |
|---|---|
| 1 year | $4,431.92 |
| 5 years | $2,735.76 |
| 10 years | $1,496.89 |

After a full decade of payments, almost $1,500 of the original $5,000 is still owed. The minimum is designed to keep the account current, not to get you out of debt.

## The formula matters: how issuers calculate the minimum

Card issuers use different formulas, and the difference is enormous. For the same $5,000 at 22%:

| Minimum payment rule | Time to pay off | Total interest |
|---|---|---|
| Interest + 1% of balance, $25 floor | 230 months | $8,099.69 |
| Interest + 1% of balance, $35 floor | 197 months | $7,673.05 |
| Interest + 2% of balance, $25 floor | 137 months | $4,228.61 |
| Interest + 3% of balance, $35 floor | 90 months | $2,807.71 |

A higher floor helps at the end of the payoff, when the balance is small. A higher percentage helps throughout. Your cardholder agreement states the exact formula; enter it under More options in the calculator.

## The power of a fixed payment

The single most effective change is to stop following the shrinking minimum and pay a fixed amount every month. On $5,000 at 22%:

| Fixed monthly payment | Time to pay off | Total interest |
|---|---|---|
| $141.67 (the first minimum, kept fixed) | 58 months | $3,121.33 |
| $150 | 52 months | $2,798.09 |
| $200 | 34 months | $1,749.90 |
| $250 | 26 months | $1,285.71 |
| $300 | 21 months | $1,021.61 |
| $500 | 12 months | $574.44 |

Simply keeping the payment at the first month's minimum, $141.67, instead of letting it fall, cuts the payoff from more than 19 years to under 5, and saves almost $5,000 in interest. You don't need to find a lot of extra money to escape the minimum-payment trap; you need to stop the payment from shrinking.

## Would a balance transfer help?

A 0% balance transfer card can cut the interest dramatically, if you pay the balance off before the promotion ends. Moving the $5,000 to a card with an 18-month 0% offer and a 3% transfer fee ($150) means paying $5,150 in total. Clearing it within the promotion takes **$286.11 a month**.

The same $286.11 a month on the original card at 22% would take 22 months and cost $1,083.18 in interest, so after the $150 fee the transfer saves about $930. The catches:

- **Any balance left when the promotion ends** goes to the card's regular rate, often above 20%.
- **New purchases** may not get the 0% rate, and payments may be applied to the 0% balance first.
- **Missing a payment** can end the promotion early.
- **You need good credit** to qualify, and the new card adds a hard inquiry.

A transfer works best as part of a plan with a fixed monthly payment that clears the balance in time, not as a way to lower the minimum.

## Paying off several cards at once

With more than one card, paying the minimum on all of them spreads your money thin, and each card follows its own slow schedule. A better approach: pay the minimum on every card, then send all your extra money to one card at a time. Paying the highest interest rate first (the avalanche method) costs the least; paying the smallest balance first (the snowball) clears a card sooner, which some people find motivating. When a card is paid off, add its payment to the next one. The [debt payoff calculator](/finance/debt-payoff-calculator/) compares both orders with your actual balances.

## How minimum payments affect your credit score

Paying at least the minimum on time protects your payment history, the most important part of your credit score. But your **credit utilization**, your balance as a share of your limit, matters too, and minimum payments leave it high for years.

Say the $5,000 balance sits on a card with a $6,000 limit: utilization is about 83%. After a year of minimum payments, the balance is $4,431.92, still 74% of the limit. Paying a fixed $300 a month instead brings it down to $2,231.86, about 37%, in the same year. Lower utilization usually lifts scores within a billing cycle or two of the new balance being reported, which can mean better rates on your next car loan or mortgage.

## What your statement tells you

Since the CARD Act of 2009, credit card statements must include a **minimum payment warning**: how long it will take to pay off the balance if you pay only the minimum, the total cost, and the monthly payment needed to pay it off in three years along with the total cost at that payment. If you only look at one part of your statement, look at that box.

## How to get out faster

1. **Pay a fixed amount, not the minimum.** Choose the highest amount you can sustain, and automate it.
2. **Stop adding new charges.** Paying $300 a month while charging $250 barely moves the balance. Switch to debit or cash while you pay down the card.
3. **Lower the rate.** A 0% balance transfer card, a lower-rate personal loan or a call to your issuer can cut the interest. Compare offers by APR and fees with the [APR calculator](/finance/apr-calculator/).
4. **Target one card at a time.** With several cards, pay minimums on all and send extra to one, highest rate first. The [debt payoff calculator](/finance/debt-payoff-calculator/) plans the order.
5. **Use windfalls.** Tax refunds and bonuses applied to the balance shorten the payoff immediately.
6. **Find the money in your budget.** Start from your real take-home pay in the [paycheck calculator](/finance/paycheck-calculator/).

## When the minimum is all you can pay

Sometimes paying the minimum is genuinely all that fits, and paying it on time is far better than missing a payment. A missed payment brings a late fee, can trigger a penalty APR around 30%, and a payment 30 days late can be reported to the credit bureaus. If you're stuck at the minimum for more than a few months:

- **Call your issuer** and ask about hardship programs that lower the rate or payment temporarily.
- **Contact a nonprofit credit counselor,** such as an agency affiliated with the National Foundation for Credit Counseling, about a debt management plan.
- **Avoid debt settlement companies** that ask you to stop paying; they damage your credit and often charge high fees.

## Key terms

- **Minimum payment:** the smallest payment that keeps the account in good standing.
- **APR:** the yearly interest rate on the balance you carry.
- **Credit utilization:** your card balances divided by your credit limits; a key factor in credit scores.
- **Minimum payment warning:** the statement box showing how long minimum payments will take and what they'll cost.
- **Penalty APR:** a higher rate that can apply after a payment is 60 days late.
