**Short answer:** most adults need somewhere between **1,600 and 3,000 calories a day**. Your number depends on your **size, age, sex and activity level**. To estimate it, calculate your resting metabolic rate and multiply by an activity factor; the [calorie calculator](/health/calorie-calculator/) does both in seconds. To lose about a pound a week, eat roughly **500 calories a day less** than that.

## Typical daily calorie needs

These estimates use the Mifflin-St Jeor equation, which a 2005 review found to be the most accurate of the common formulas for most adults:

| Person | Sedentary | Moderately active |
|---|---|---|
| Woman, 30, 5'4", 140 lb | about 1,600 | about 2,080 |
| Woman, 55, 5'4", 160 lb | about 1,570 | about 2,020 |
| Man, 30, 5'10", 180 lb | about 2,140 | about 2,760 |
| Man, 55, 5'10", 200 lb | about 2,100 | about 2,710 |

Activity makes the biggest difference. For the same 30-year-old man, moving from a desk-bound week to exercising 3 to 5 days a week raises his needs by over 600 calories a day.

## How your number is calculated

**1. Basal metabolic rate (BMR).** The energy your body uses at rest to breathe, circulate blood and keep warm. Mifflin-St Jeor estimates it as 10 × weight (kg) + 6.25 × height (cm) − 5 × age, plus 5 for men or minus 161 for women.

**2. Activity factor.** BMR is multiplied by about 1.2 if you're sedentary, 1.375 for light exercise, 1.55 for moderate, 1.725 for hard daily exercise and 1.9 for very active jobs or twice-daily training. The result is your total daily energy expenditure (TDEE), your maintenance calories.

**3. Goal adjustment.** A pound of body weight is commonly estimated at about 3,500 calories, so a daily change of 500 calories corresponds to roughly a pound a week.

## Setting a safe calorie target

To lose weight, aim for a deficit of **250 to 1,000 calories a day**, which works out to about 0.5 to 2 pounds a week. Faster isn't better: large deficits make it harder to get enough protein and nutrients, increase muscle loss and are hard to sustain. Intakes below about 1,200 calories a day for women or 1,500 for men are generally not recommended without medical supervision.

To gain weight or build muscle, a surplus of 250 to 500 calories a day, combined with strength training, limits fat gain.

## How many calories to lose weight: worked examples

Subtracting 500 calories from maintenance is the classic starting point, but for smaller, less active people it can push intake too low. Using the same four example adults:

| Person | Maintenance (sedentary) | Minus 500 | Maintenance (moderately active) | Minus 500 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Woman, 30, 5'4", 140 lb | 1,608 | 1,108 | 2,077 | 1,577 |
| Woman, 55, 5'4", 160 lb | 1,567 | 1,067 | 2,024 | 1,524 |
| Man, 30, 5'10", 180 lb | 2,139 | 1,639 | 2,763 | 2,263 |
| Man, 55, 5'10", 200 lb | 2,098 | 1,598 | 2,710 | 2,210 |

For both sedentary women, a 500-calorie deficit falls below the 1,200-calorie level that's generally considered the minimum without medical supervision. Two better options:

- **Use a smaller deficit.** Cutting 250 to 300 calories a day still produces steady loss of about half a pound a week, and it's easier to keep up.
- **Add activity instead.** Moving from sedentary to moderately active raises the 30-year-old woman's maintenance to 2,077 calories, so a 500-calorie deficit leaves 1,577, with room for enough protein and nutrients.

The men in the table have more room, but the same principle applies: the more active you are, the easier it is to lose weight without eating very little.

## Calories during pregnancy and breastfeeding

Needs change during pregnancy and breastfeeding, and weight loss isn't usually recommended during pregnancy. According to the Dietary Guidelines for Americans, most people need no extra calories in the first trimester, about 340 extra calories a day in the second and about 450 in the third. Breastfeeding adds roughly 330 calories a day in the first six months and about 400 in the second six months, compared with pre-pregnancy needs. Your prenatal care provider can tailor these to your situation, especially with twins or if you started above or below a healthy weight.

## Why the scale doesn't always match the math

Even with a perfect calorie target, daily weigh-ins jump around. A few common reasons:

- **Water from salt and carbohydrates.** A salty dinner or a high-carb day can add a pound or two of water overnight; it's not fat.
- **Food still in your system.** A large meal weighs something until it's digested.
- **Hormonal cycles.** Many women see water-weight changes of several pounds across the menstrual cycle.
- **New exercise.** Starting a training program can cause temporary water retention in muscles.

Because of all this, compare weekly averages, or the same day of the cycle month to month, rather than any single morning. A steady downward trend over three or four weeks means the plan is working, even if individual days look discouraging.

## Common calorie-counting mistakes

- **Underestimating portions.** Studies consistently find that people underestimate how much they eat, especially with foods that are hard to measure, like oils, nuts, cheese and pasta. A kitchen scale for a couple of weeks resets your eye.
- **Forgetting drinks.** Coffee drinks, juice, soda and alcohol can add hundreds of calories a day.
- **Eating back exercise calories in full.** Fitness trackers often overestimate calories burned. If you eat them back, start with half.
- **Weekend drift.** Sticking to the target five days a week and overeating on two can erase the whole week's deficit.
- **Choosing an unrealistically high activity level.** If you have a desk job and exercise three times a week, "light" or "moderate" is usually more accurate than "active."

## Spreading calories across the day

A daily number is easier to hit when you split it into meals. One simple pattern is three meals and one or two snacks:

| Daily target | Breakfast | Lunch | Dinner | Snacks |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1,600 | 350 | 450 | 550 | 250 |
| 2,000 | 450 | 550 | 650 | 350 |
| 2,400 | 500 | 650 | 800 | 450 |

These are examples, not rules: some people prefer a bigger breakfast, others skip snacks entirely. What helps most is building each meal around a source of protein and some vegetables or fruit, then filling the rest with whole grains and healthy fats. That combination tends to keep you full until the next meal, which makes the daily target feel less like a fight.

## Does meal timing matter?

For weight change, total calories over days and weeks matter far more than when you eat them. Eating late in the evening doesn't by itself cause weight gain, although late-night snacking often adds calories people don't notice. Intermittent fasting works for some people mainly because a shorter eating window makes it easier to eat less, not because of any special effect of timing. Choose whichever eating pattern helps you stay consistent week after week.

## Calorie needs in later life

Energy needs usually fall with age, as muscle mass declines and many people become less active, while the need for protein, calcium and vitamin D stays the same or rises. That makes the quality of each calorie more important. For older adults, losing weight unintentionally is often a bigger health concern than gaining a few pounds, so any weight-loss plan after about 65 is best discussed with a doctor. Strength training helps at every age: it preserves muscle, supports balance and keeps everyday tasks like climbing stairs and carrying groceries easier.

## Why your needs may differ from the estimate

- **Body composition.** Muscle burns more energy than fat. If you know your body fat percentage, the Katch-McArdle formula in the calculator uses lean mass instead of total weight; estimate it with the [body fat calculator](/health/body-fat-calculator/).
- **Everyday movement.** Walking, standing and fidgeting vary a lot between people and can matter as much as workouts.
- **Weight change itself.** As you lose weight, your body burns slightly less, so progress naturally slows. Recalculate after every 10 pounds or so.
- **Medical conditions and medications.** Thyroid conditions and some medications change energy needs. Talk to your doctor.
- **Teenagers.** The adult equations don't account for growth, which raises energy needs during the teen years. Teens shouldn't diet without guidance from a pediatrician or registered dietitian.
- **Climate and illness.** Recovering from illness or injury can temporarily raise needs, and so can working outdoors in extreme cold or heat for long periods.

## Where the calories come from matters too

Calories decide whether weight goes up or down, but the mix affects fullness, energy and muscle. Higher protein helps you feel full and keep muscle during weight loss; fiber from vegetables, fruit and whole grains helps fullness and digestion. The [macro calculator](/health/macro-calculator/) turns your calorie target into grams of protein, carbohydrate and fat.

## Track, then adjust

Use the calculator's number for two to three weeks, weighing yourself under the same conditions a few times a week. If the weekly average isn't moving as planned, adjust by 100 to 200 calories a day. Check your progress against a healthy range with the [BMI calculator](/health/bmi-calculator/), and remember that how you feel, sleep and perform matters just as much as the number on the scale.
