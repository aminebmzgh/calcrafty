**Short answer:** most adults need somewhere between **1,600 and 3,000 calories a day**. Your number depends on your **size, age, sex and activity level**. To estimate it, calculate your resting metabolic rate and multiply by an activity factor; the [calorie calculator](/health/calorie-calculator/) does both in seconds. To lose about a pound a week, eat roughly **500 calories a day less** than that.

## Typical daily calorie needs

These estimates use the Mifflin-St Jeor equation, which a 2005 review found to be the most accurate of the common formulas for most adults:

| Person | Sedentary | Moderately active |
|---|---|---|
| Woman, 30, 5'4", 140 lb | about 1,600 | about 2,080 |
| Woman, 55, 5'4", 160 lb | about 1,570 | about 2,020 |
| Man, 30, 5'10", 180 lb | about 2,140 | about 2,760 |
| Man, 55, 5'10", 200 lb | about 2,100 | about 2,710 |

Activity makes the biggest difference. For the same 30-year-old man, moving from a desk-bound week to exercising 3 to 5 days a week raises his needs by over 600 calories a day.

## How your number is calculated

**1. Basal metabolic rate (BMR).** The energy your body uses at rest to breathe, circulate blood and keep warm. Mifflin-St Jeor estimates it as 10 × weight (kg) + 6.25 × height (cm) − 5 × age, plus 5 for men or minus 161 for women.

**2. Activity factor.** BMR is multiplied by about 1.2 if you're sedentary, 1.375 for light exercise, 1.55 for moderate, 1.725 for hard daily exercise and 1.9 for very active jobs or twice-daily training. The result is your total daily energy expenditure (TDEE), your maintenance calories.

**3. Goal adjustment.** A pound of body weight is commonly estimated at about 3,500 calories, so a daily change of 500 calories corresponds to roughly a pound a week.

## Setting a safe calorie target

To lose weight, aim for a deficit of **250 to 1,000 calories a day**, which works out to about 0.5 to 2 pounds a week. Faster isn't better: large deficits make it harder to get enough protein and nutrients, increase muscle loss and are hard to sustain. Intakes below about 1,200 calories a day for women or 1,500 for men are generally not recommended without medical supervision.

To gain weight or build muscle, a surplus of 250 to 500 calories a day, combined with strength training, limits fat gain.

## Why your needs may differ from the estimate

- **Body composition.** Muscle burns more energy than fat. If you know your body fat percentage, the Katch-McArdle formula in the calculator uses lean mass instead of total weight; estimate it with the [body fat calculator](/health/body-fat-calculator/).
- **Everyday movement.** Walking, standing and fidgeting vary a lot between people and can matter as much as workouts.
- **Weight change itself.** As you lose weight, your body burns slightly less, so progress naturally slows. Recalculate after every 10 pounds or so.
- **Medical conditions and medications.** Thyroid conditions and some medications change energy needs. Talk to your doctor.

## Where the calories come from matters too

Calories decide whether weight goes up or down, but the mix affects fullness, energy and muscle. Higher protein helps you feel full and keep muscle during weight loss; fiber from vegetables, fruit and whole grains helps fullness and digestion. The [macro calculator](/health/macro-calculator/) turns your calorie target into grams of protein, carbohydrate and fat.

## Track, then adjust

Use the calculator's number for two to three weeks, weighing yourself under the same conditions a few times a week. If the weekly average isn't moving as planned, adjust by 100 to 200 calories a day. Check your progress against a healthy range with the [BMI calculator](/health/bmi-calculator/), and remember that how you feel, sleep and perform matters as much as the scale.
