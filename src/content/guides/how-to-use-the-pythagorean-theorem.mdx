**Short answer:** to use the Pythagorean theorem, (1) confirm the triangle has a right angle, (2) label the side opposite the right angle **c** and the other two sides **a** and **b**, (3) substitute the two sides you know into **a² + b² = c²**, and (4) solve for the missing side with a square root. To find the hypotenuse, add the squares; to find a leg, subtract. The [Pythagorean theorem calculator](/math/pythagorean-theorem-calculator/) does the arithmetic and shows the exact square-root form.

## Step 1: Check that there's a right angle

The theorem only applies to **right triangles**. In word problems, the right angle is usually implied rather than drawn:

- A wall meeting level ground.
- Directions at right angles, such as "east, then north".
- The length and width of a rectangle, which meet at a corner.
- Words like "perpendicular", "vertical" and "square corner".

If none of these is present, the triangle may not be right-angled, and you'd need the law of cosines instead.

## Step 2: Label the sides

The **hypotenuse c** is always the side opposite the right angle, and always the longest side. The other two sides, the **legs a and b**, form the right angle; it doesn't matter which you call a and which b.

A quick sketch helps more than anything else. Draw the triangle, mark the right angle with a small square, label c first, then write in the lengths you know and a letter for the one you don't.

## Step 3: Set up the equation

Write **a² + b² = c²** and substitute the two known sides.

- If you know both legs, the unknown is c: **c² = a² + b²**.
- If you know the hypotenuse and one leg, the unknown is a leg: **a² = c² − b²**.

The most common error is adding when you should subtract. If you know c, you're always subtracting.

## Step 4: Solve and check

Take the square root of both sides, then check the answer makes sense: the hypotenuse must be longer than either leg, and the legs must be shorter than the hypotenuse. If a word problem asks for a unit, include it, and round only at the end.

A fast sanity check: the hypotenuse is always less than the two legs added together, and more than the longer leg. For legs of 7 and 10, c must be between 10 and 17. The real answer, about 12.2, sits comfortably inside that range. If your answer falls outside it, look for a squaring or subtraction slip.

## Worked word problems

### 1. The diagonal of a room

A rectangular room is **12 feet** by **16 feet**. How far is it from one corner to the opposite corner?

The length and width are the legs: c² = 12² + 16² = 144 + 256 = 400, so c = √400 = **20 feet**. That's a 3-4-5 triangle scaled up by four.

### 2. How high does the kite fly?

A kite is on **150 feet** of string, and it's directly above a point **90 feet** away along the ground. How high is it, assuming the string is straight?

The string is the hypotenuse. Height² = 150² − 90² = 22,500 − 8,100 = 14,400, so the height is √14,400 = **120 feet**.

### 3. Walking across a park

You walk **1.2 miles** east and then **0.5 miles** north. How far are you from where you started, in a straight line?

East and north are at right angles: distance² = 1.2² + 0.5² = 1.44 + 0.25 = 1.69, so the distance is √1.69 = **1.3 miles**.

### 4. Home plate to second base

The bases on a baseball diamond are **90 feet** apart, and the diamond is a square. How far does the catcher throw from home plate to second base?

The throw is the diagonal of a 90-foot square: c² = 90² + 90² = 16,200, so c = 90√2 ≈ **127.28 feet**, or about 127 feet 3⅜ inches, which is the figure in the official rules of baseball.

### 5. A wheelchair ramp

The ADA Standards for Accessible Design limit the slope of a ramp to **1:12**, one inch of rise for every 12 inches of run. A ramp needs to climb **30 inches**. How long is the ramp surface?

The run is 30 × 12 = 360 inches. Ramp length² = 360² + 30² = 129,600 + 900 = 130,500, so the length is √130,500 ≈ **361.25 inches**, about 30 feet 1¼ inches. Because the slope is so gentle, the ramp is only slightly longer than its run.

### 6. Will the TV fit?

A TV box is **24 inches** wide and **10 inches** deep, and a doorway is 26 inches wide. Can you carry it through diagonally, tilted on its edge? The diagonal of the box's footprint is √(576 + 100) = √676 = **26 inches**, exactly the width of the door. It's too close to call in practice; measure the real door and box first.

## The Pythagorean theorem in three dimensions

For a box, a room or any rectangular solid, the longest straight line inside it runs from one corner to the opposite corner, through the middle. Its length is:

**d = √(length² + width² + height²)**

This is the 2D theorem used twice: first the diagonal of the floor, then that diagonal with the height.

A shipping box is **12 × 9 × 8 inches**. The longest item that fits, lying corner to corner, is √(144 + 81 + 64) = √289 = **17 inches**.

A room **24 × 6 × 8 feet** has a space diagonal of √(576 + 36 + 64) = √676 = **26 feet**, which tells you the longest pole or pipe you could store in it.

## The distance formula

On a coordinate grid, the distance between two points (x₁, y₁) and (x₂, y₂) is:

**d = √((x₂ − x₁)² + (y₂ − y₁)²)**

The horizontal difference and the vertical difference are the legs of a right triangle. From (1, 2) to (5, 9), the legs are 4 and 7, so d = √(16 + 49) = √65 ≈ **8.06**. Map apps, game engines and GPS software use this idea, adjusted for the curve of the Earth over long distances.

## Recognizing Pythagorean triples

Spotting a triple saves a lot of arithmetic. The common ones are **3-4-5, 5-12-13, 8-15-17 and 7-24-25**, plus their multiples, such as 6-8-10, 9-12-15, 10-24-26 and 12-16-20.

When a problem gives you two sides from a triple, you know the third without calculating. Legs of 10 and 24? That's 5-12-13 doubled, so the hypotenuse is 26.

### Generating your own triples

Euclid's formula produces as many triples as you like. Choose whole numbers **m** and **n** with m greater than n, then:

- a = m² − n²
- b = 2mn
- c = m² + n²

| m | n | a | b | c |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2 | 1 | 3 | 4 | 5 |
| 3 | 2 | 5 | 12 | 13 |
| 4 | 1 | 15 | 8 | 17 |
| 4 | 3 | 7 | 24 | 25 |
| 5 | 2 | 21 | 20 | 29 |

Teachers use this to write problems with clean answers, and it's a quick way to check your own work.

## Answers that aren't whole numbers

Most real triangles don't come out evenly. For legs of 4 and 7, c = √65, which can't be simplified because 65 has no square factor other than 1. For legs of 6 and 6, c = √72 = **6√2**, because 72 = 36 × 2.

To simplify a square root, look for the largest perfect square (4, 9, 16, 25, 36, 49, 64, 81, 100 and so on) that divides the number, and take its root outside. If the question asks for a decimal, use the [scientific calculator](/math/scientific-calculator/) or round the exact value at the end.

## Using it on real projects

The theorem is one of the most practical pieces of school math:

- **Layout:** builders check square corners with the 3-4-5 rule, measuring 3 and 4 units along the two sides and confirming the diagonal is 5.
- **Roofs:** a rafter is the hypotenuse of the roof's rise and run. The [roofing calculator](/construction/roofing-calculator/) turns pitch into the real roof area.
- **Floors and walls:** splitting an angled room into rectangles and right triangles is how you find its area with the [square footage calculator](/construction/square-footage-calculator/).
- **Screens:** TV and monitor sizes are diagonal measurements.

## Practice problems

Try these, then check below.

1. A right triangle has legs of 9 and 40. Find the hypotenuse.
2. A 13-foot ladder reaches 12 feet up a wall. How far is its foot from the wall?
3. A square has sides of 7 cm. How long is its diagonal, in exact form?
4. A drone flies 300 meters north and then 400 meters west. How far is it from its launch point?
5. Are 10, 15 and 18 the sides of a right triangle?

**Answers:**

1. √(81 + 1,600) = √1,681 = **41**.
2. √(169 − 144) = √25 = **5 feet**.
3. √(49 + 49) = √98 = **7√2 cm**, about 9.90 cm.
4. √(90,000 + 160,000) = √250,000 = **500 meters**.
5. 10² + 15² = 325, but 18² = 324, so **no**, though it's very close to one. The largest angle is just under 90°.

## Common mistakes

1. **Labeling the wrong side as c.** The hypotenuse is opposite the right angle, not just "the side on the slant".
2. **Forgetting to square.** a + b = c is never right.
3. **Forgetting the square root.** If c² = 400, the answer is 20, not 400.
4. **Using mixed units.** Convert everything to inches, feet or meters first.
5. **Applying it without a right angle.** Check the angle before you start.
