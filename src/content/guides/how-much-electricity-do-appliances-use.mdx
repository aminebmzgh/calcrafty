**Short answer:** an appliance's electricity use is its **wattage × hours of use ÷ 1,000**, which gives kilowatt-hours (kWh), the unit on your bill. Heat-making appliances use the most: space heaters, water heaters, dryers and ovens draw 1,000 to 5,500 watts. Electronics and LED lights use far less, often under 150 watts. Multiply the kWh by your price per kWh for the cost, or use the [electricity cost calculator](/physics/electricity-cost-calculator/), which starts at the current U.S. average price.

## How to turn watts into kWh and dollars

There are three steps:

1. **Daily kWh = watts × hours used per day ÷ 1,000.**
2. **Yearly kWh = daily kWh × days used per year.**
3. **Yearly cost = yearly kWh × your price per kWh.**

The Virginia Cooperative Extension's own example: a **200-watt window fan** used **4 hours a day** for **120 days a year** uses 200 × 4 × 120 ÷ 1,000 = **96 kWh**. At their example rate of 8.5¢, that was $8.16 a year; at **19.6¢**, roughly the U.S. city average reported by the Bureau of Labor Statistics for September 2026, it's **$18.82**.

Short bursts of high power add up to surprisingly little: a 1,500-watt hair dryer used for 10 minutes a day costs less a year than a 75-watt ceiling fan running every night. It's the hours that matter as much as the watts. When you're hunting for savings, start with the appliances that run for many hours, not the ones with the biggest number on the label.

## Appliances that cycle on and off

Refrigerators, freezers, water heaters, dehumidifiers and anything with a thermostat don't run at full power all the time. They switch on when needed and off again. Using the nameplate wattage for 24 hours a day badly overstates their use.

The extension service's rule of thumb for a refrigerator is to divide the time it's plugged in by **three**: 24 hours becomes about 8 hours of running at full power. For a 725-watt refrigerator, that's 725 × 8 ÷ 1,000 = 5.8 kWh a day, or about 2,117 kWh a year. A new refrigerator usually uses a fraction of that; its yellow **EnergyGuide** label shows the estimated yearly kWh, which is the best number to use.

## Measuring your own appliances

Estimates are useful, but measuring is better.

- **EnergyGuide labels.** Refrigerators, freezers, dishwashers, washers, water heaters and many other appliances sold in the U.S. carry a yellow label with estimated yearly energy use and cost.
- **Plug-in usage meters.** Plug the meter into the wall and the appliance into the meter, and it shows watts and kWh used over time. Leave it for a week to capture normal use. Some utilities and libraries lend them.
- **Smart plugs and smart meters.** Many smart plugs report energy use, and some utilities show hourly use from your smart meter online.
- **Amps and volts.** If the label shows amps, multiply by volts: a space heater drawing 12.5 amps on a 120-volt outlet uses 1,500 watts. The [Ohm's law calculator](/physics/ohms-law-calculator/) shows how watts, volts and amps relate.

## Standby power: the hidden load

Many devices keep drawing power when they're "off": TVs, game consoles, cable boxes, printers, speakers, microwaves with clocks and anything with a remote. These standby or "phantom" loads are small one by one, but they run 24 hours a day.

A device drawing **5 watts** all year uses 5 × 24 × 365 ÷ 1,000 = **43.8 kWh**, about **$8.58** at 19.6¢. Ten such devices add up to about **$86 a year**. Plugging a TV, console and sound system into a switched power strip and turning it off at night cuts these loads at once.

## How much does a whole home use?

The Energy Information Administration reports that the average U.S. residential customer used **10,791 kWh** in 2022, about **899 kWh a month**. Use varies widely with climate, home size and heating fuel: Louisiana averaged about 14,774 kWh a year per customer and Hawaii about 6,178 kWh.

To see where yours goes, compare your bill with the estimates above. If an electric water heater and air conditioning account for most of it, those are where savings come from. If your use is much higher than similar homes, check for an old second refrigerator, a pool pump, electric heating or a failing appliance.

## Reading an EnergyGuide label

The yellow EnergyGuide label, required by the Federal Trade Commission on many major appliances, is the quickest way to compare models before you buy. It shows:

- **Estimated yearly energy cost,** at a national average electricity price used when the label was printed. That price may be out of date, so recalculate with your own rate.
- **Estimated yearly electricity use** in kWh. Multiply this by your price per kWh for your real cost.
- **A cost range** for similar models, showing where this one sits.
- **Whether it's ENERGY STAR certified,** if the label includes the logo.

For example, if one refrigerator is labeled at 450 kWh a year and another at 650 kWh, the difference is 200 kWh, about **$39 a year** at 19.6¢. Over a 12-year life, that's around $470, which can justify a higher purchase price for the more efficient model.

## Seasonal patterns

Monthly use rarely stays flat. Air conditioning drives up summer bills in hot climates, while electric heating, longer evenings with lights on and holiday decorations raise winter bills in cold ones. A dehumidifier may run only in humid months, and a pool pump only in summer. When you estimate a seasonal appliance, enter only the days it actually runs, rather than a full year. Comparing the same month across two years on your bill is a better way to see whether a change, such as a new appliance or a thermostat schedule, has worked.

## Where the biggest savings are

1. **Heating, cooling and hot water.** Thermostat settings, insulation, sealing air leaks and an efficient heat pump or heat-pump water heater make the largest difference.
2. **Laundry.** Wash in cold water when possible and dry full loads; heat-pump dryers use much less energy than standard electric dryers.
3. **Lighting.** LED bulbs use a fraction of the power of incandescent bulbs for the same light.
4. **Old appliances.** A decades-old refrigerator or freezer can use several times what a new one does.
5. **Standby loads.** Switched power strips for entertainment and office setups.

## Electricity and emissions

Using less electricity also lowers the emissions from generating it. How much depends on your local power mix, which ranges from mostly hydro, nuclear or renewables in some regions to mostly coal or gas in others. For the emissions from driving, the [car CO2 calculator](/ecology/car-co2-calculator/) works from your fuel economy and mileage.

## Key terms

- **Watt (W):** the rate at which an appliance uses energy.
- **Kilowatt-hour (kWh):** 1,000 watts used for one hour; the unit on your electric bill.
- **Nameplate wattage:** the maximum power an appliance is rated to draw.
- **Duty cycle:** the share of time a cycling appliance actually runs.
- **Standby power:** power drawn while a device is off or idle.
- **EnergyGuide label:** the yellow label showing an appliance's estimated yearly energy use and cost.
