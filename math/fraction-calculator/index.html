How to use this fraction calculator

Enter two fractions or mixed numbers, choose an operation, and the simplified answer appears instantly as a fraction, a mixed number and a decimal. The Show the math panel lists every step, so you can check homework or follow the method.

  1. First fraction. Enter the whole number part (use 0 for a simple fraction), the numerator (top) and the denominator (bottom).
  2. Operation. Add, subtract, multiply or divide.
  3. Second fraction. Enter it the same way.

The calculator uses exact whole-number arithmetic, so even very large numerators and denominators give exact answers, never rounded decimals.

Worked examples

Adding mixed numbers: 1 1/2 + 2 2/3

  1. Convert to improper fractions: 1 1/2 = 3/2 and 2 2/3 = 8/3.
  2. Least common denominator of 2 and 3: 6.
  3. Rewrite: 3/2 = 9/6 and 8/3 = 16/6.
  4. Add the numerators: 9/6 + 16/6 = 25/6.
  5. As a mixed number: 25 ÷ 6 = 4 remainder 1, so 4 1/6.

Subtracting: 3/4 − 1/6

The LCD of 4 and 6 is 12, so 3/4 = 9/12 and 1/6 = 2/12. Then 9/12 − 2/12 = 7/12, which can’t be simplified further.

Multiplying: 2/3 × 9/4

Multiply across: (2 × 9) / (3 × 4) = 18/12. The GCD of 18 and 12 is 6, so the answer is 3/2, or 1 1/2.

Dividing: 5/8 ÷ 1/4

Flip the second fraction and multiply: 5/8 × 4/1 = 20/8. Dividing by the GCD, 4, gives 5/2, or 2 1/2.

The fraction rules in plain English

  • Add or subtract: give both fractions the same denominator, then add or subtract the numerators. The least common denominator (LCD) is the least common multiple of the two denominators.
  • Multiply: numerator × numerator over denominator × denominator.
  • Divide: multiply by the reciprocal of the second fraction (swap its numerator and denominator).
  • Simplify: divide the top and bottom by their greatest common divisor (GCD).
  • Mixed numbers: whole × denominator + numerator gives the new numerator of the improper fraction.

The GCD comes from Euclid’s algorithm: repeatedly replace the larger number with the remainder of dividing it by the smaller, until the remainder is zero. The last non-zero remainder is the GCD. For 18 and 12: 18 ÷ 12 leaves 6, then 12 ÷ 6 leaves 0, so the GCD is 6.

Common mistakes with fractions

  • Adding denominators. 1/2 + 1/3 is not 2/5. Fractions need a common denominator before adding.
  • Forgetting to flip when dividing. Only the second fraction is flipped, and only for division.
  • Dropping the whole number in mixed numbers. Convert to an improper fraction first, then calculate.
  • Not simplifying. 6/8 is correct but incomplete; most teachers expect 3/4.
  • Sign errors with negative mixed numbers. −1 1/2 means −(1 + 1/2) = −3/2, not −1 + 1/2.

Finding the least common denominator

The least common denominator (LCD) is the smallest number both denominators divide into evenly. There are three ways to find it:

  1. List multiples. For 4 and 6: multiples of 6 are 6, 12, 18…; the first that 4 also divides is 12. Quick for small numbers.
  2. Use prime factors. 12 = 2 × 2 × 3 and 18 = 2 × 3 × 3. Take each prime the greatest number of times it appears in either: 2 × 2 × 3 × 3 = 36.
  3. Use the GCD. LCD = (a × b) ÷ GCD(a, b). For 12 and 18, the GCD is 6, so (12 × 18) ÷ 6 = 36.

Multiplying the two denominators together always gives a common denominator, but not always the smallest, and larger numbers mean more simplifying at the end.

Simplifying with the greatest common divisor

To reduce a fraction to lowest terms, divide the top and bottom by their greatest common divisor (GCD). For larger numbers, Euclid’s algorithm finds the GCD quickly by repeated division:

  • Simplify 84/126. Divide 126 by 84: remainder 42. Divide 84 by 42: remainder 0. So the GCD is 42.
  • 84 ÷ 42 = 2 and 126 ÷ 42 = 3, so 84/126 = 2/3.

This is the method the calculator uses internally, with exact whole-number arithmetic, so even fractions with very large numbers simplify correctly.

Converting decimals to fractions

A terminating decimal becomes a fraction over a power of ten, which you then simplify:

  • 0.6 = 6/10 = 3/5
  • 0.375 = 375/1000 = 3/8
  • 2.25 = 225/100 = 9/4 = 2 1/4

Repeating decimals have exact fraction forms too: 0.333… = 1/3, 0.666… = 2/3 and 0.1666… = 1/6. A rounded decimal such as 0.33 is not the same as 1/3; it equals 33/100. That’s one reason to keep exact fractions until the final step.

Comparing and ordering fractions

To tell which of two fractions is larger without a calculator, cross-multiply: compare the first numerator times the second denominator with the second numerator times the first denominator.

  • Is 5/8 bigger than 3/5? 5 × 5 = 25 and 3 × 8 = 24. Since 25 > 24, 5/8 is larger.
  • To order several fractions, rewrite them all over a common denominator and compare the numerators, or convert each to a decimal.

Mixed numbers and improper fractions

A mixed number like 2 3/4 combines a whole number and a fraction; an improper fraction like 11/4 has a numerator larger than its denominator. They’re two ways of writing the same amount.

  • Mixed to improper: multiply the whole number by the denominator and add the numerator, keeping the same denominator. 2 3/4 → (2 × 4 + 3)/4 = 11/4.
  • Improper to mixed: divide the numerator by the denominator. The quotient is the whole number and the remainder goes over the denominator. 11/4 → 11 ÷ 4 = 2 remainder 3 → 2 3/4.

Improper fractions are easier to calculate with, especially for multiplying and dividing, so convert to them first and back to a mixed number at the end. The calculator accepts either form and shows both in the result.

Solving fraction word problems

Most everyday fraction problems come down to choosing the right operation:

  • “Of” means multiply. Two-thirds of a 3/4 cup of sugar is 2/3 × 3/4 = 1/2 cup.
  • “How many fit into” means divide. A 6-foot board cut into pieces 3/4 of a foot long gives 6 ÷ 3/4 = 8 pieces.
  • Combining parts means add. Running 1 1/4 miles in the morning and 2 2/3 miles in the evening is 1 1/4 + 2 2/3 = 3 11/12 miles.
  • “How much more” means subtract. If a recipe needs 2 1/2 cups of flour and you have 1 3/4, you need 2 1/2 − 1 3/4 = 3/4 cup more.

When a word problem feels confusing, sketch it: a bar split into equal parts often makes the right operation obvious before you do any arithmetic at all.

Where fractions show up every day

Fractions aren’t just homework. They appear wherever things are divided into parts:

  • Cooking. Halving a recipe that calls for 3/4 cup of flour needs 3/4 × 1/2 = 3/8 cup. Scaling a recipe up by 1 1/2 turns 2/3 cup into 2/3 × 3/2 = 1 cup.
  • Measuring and building. US tape measures are marked in sixteenths of an inch. Two boards of 5 3/8 inches and 7 11/16 inches laid end to end measure 13 1/16 inches.
  • Time. A quarter of an hour is 15 minutes; three quarters of an hour is 45.
  • Money. Splitting a bill three ways gives each person 1/3. Stock prices in the US were quoted in fractions such as 1/8 and 1/16 of a dollar until 2001.
  • Probability. The chance of rolling a six is 1/6; rolling two sixes in a row is 1/6 × 1/6 = 1/36.

In each case, keeping the answer as a fraction avoids rounding until the very end, which is why carpenters and bakers often prefer fractions to decimals. When you need a decimal, the calculator shows it alongside the exact fraction.

A useful habit is to estimate first. 3/4 + 2/3 must be a little less than 1 1/2, because both fractions are a bit under 3/4. If the calculator’s answer is far from your estimate, re-check that each number went into the right box.

Key terms

  • Numerator: the top number, how many parts you have.
  • Denominator: the bottom number, how many equal parts make a whole.
  • Mixed number: a whole number and a fraction, like 2 1/3.
  • Improper fraction: numerator larger than or equal to the denominator.
  • Reciprocal: a fraction flipped upside down; 3/4 becomes 4/3.

Frequently asked questions

How do you add fractions with different denominators?

Find a common denominator, rewrite both fractions with it, then add the numerators. For 1/2 + 1/3, the least common denominator is 6, so 1/2 = 3/6 and 1/3 = 2/6, and 3/6 + 2/6 = 5/6. The calculator always uses the least common denominator, which keeps the numbers small.

How do you multiply fractions?

Multiply the numerators together and the denominators together, then simplify. 2/3 × 9/4 = 18/12, and dividing the top and bottom by 6 gives 3/2, or 1 1/2. You can also cancel common factors before multiplying to keep the numbers smaller.

How do you divide fractions?

Flip the second fraction and multiply, often remembered as "keep, change, flip." 5/8 ÷ 1/4 becomes 5/8 × 4/1 = 20/8, which simplifies to 5/2 or 2 1/2.

How do I simplify a fraction?

Divide the numerator and denominator by their greatest common divisor (GCD). For 18/24, the GCD is 6, so 18/24 = 3/4. The calculator finds the GCD with Euclid's algorithm, which works for any size of number.

How do I enter a mixed number?

Put the whole number in the first box, then the numerator and denominator of the fraction part. For 2 2/3, enter 2, 2 and 3. For a plain fraction, leave the whole number as 0. For a negative mixed number such as −1 1/2, make the whole number negative; the sign applies to the whole value.

What is an improper fraction?

A fraction whose numerator is equal to or larger than its denominator, such as 7/4. It's the same value as the mixed number 1 3/4. Improper fractions are easier to calculate with, so the calculator converts mixed numbers first and converts back at the end.
