How to use this discount calculator

Enter the price and the discount, and the sale price updates as you type.

  1. Original price. The price before the sale, from the tag or the listing.
  2. Discount type. Choose Percent off for “30% off” deals, or Dollars off for “$15 off” coupons and gift card credits.
  3. Discount. The percentage or dollar amount.
  4. More options. Add an extra percent off that applies after the first discount, as with “take an extra 20% off sale prices”, and your sales tax rate to see the total at the register.

The result shows the sale price, how much you save and the total discount as a percentage of the original price. The table underneath lists the sale price and savings at 10% to 75% off, so you can compare deals at a glance.

The formula

Savings = original price × discount % ÷ 100

Sale price = original price − savings = original price × (1 − discount % ÷ 100)

For a dollar discount, the sale price is simply original price − amount off, and the equivalent percentage is amount off ÷ original price × 100.

When a second discount applies to the reduced price:

Final price = original × (1 − first %) × (1 − second %)

Sales tax is then charged on the final price: total = final price × (1 + tax rate). Each step is rounded to the cent, the way a register rounds.

Worked example: a jacket with a sale and a coupon

A jacket lists for $129.99. It’s marked 40% off, you have a coupon for an extra 15% off sale prices, and your sales tax is 7%.

  • After 40% off: $129.99 × 0.60 = $77.994, which rounds to $77.99.
  • After the extra 15%: $77.99 × 0.85 = $66.2915, which rounds to $66.29.
  • You save: $129.99 − $66.29 = $63.70.
  • Total discount: $63.70 ÷ $129.99 = 49.0%, not the 55% you might expect from adding 40% and 15%.
  • Sales tax: $66.29 × 0.07 = $4.64.
  • You pay: $66.29 + $4.64 = $70.93.

The quick check: 0.60 × 0.85 = 0.51, so after both discounts you pay 51% of the list price, which is a 49% discount.

Why stacked discounts don’t add up

The second discount is a percentage of a smaller number, so it’s worth less than the same percentage of the original price. That’s why two discounts always combine to less than their sum.

First discount Extra discount Looks like Actually
20% 10% 30% 28%
25% 10% 35% 32.5%
30% 20% 50% 44%
40% 15% 55% 49%
50% 50% 100% 75%

The last row is the clearest: “50% off, then an extra 50% off” doesn’t make the item free. It leaves a quarter of the price.

Percent off or dollars off: which order?

When a store applies both a percentage and a fixed dollar amount, the order matters. On a $100 item with 20% off and a $10 coupon:

  • Percentage first: $100 × 0.80 = $80, then $80 − $10 = $70.
  • Dollars first: $100 − $10 = $90, then $90 × 0.80 = $72.

Taking the percentage first is better for the shopper, because the percentage applies to the bigger number. Stores set the order in their coupon terms. To model a dollar coupon followed by a percentage, choose Dollars off as the first discount and enter the percentage as the extra discount.

Discounts in your head

  • 10% off: move the decimal point one place left and subtract. On $64, that’s $6.40 off, so $57.60.
  • 20% off: double 10%. On $64, $12.80 off, so $51.20.
  • 25% off: take a quarter off, or pay three-quarters. On $64, $48.
  • 30% off: three times 10%. On $70, $21 off, so $49.
  • 50% off: halve it.
  • Any discount: pay what’s left. 35% off means paying 65%, so $40 × 0.65 = $26.

Finding the original price or the percent off

Two related questions come up often, and they need a different calculation.

What was the original price? Divide the sale price by the share you paid. An item that costs $42 after 40% off was $42 ÷ 0.60 = $70. Adding 40% back to $42 gives $58.80, because 40% of $42 is smaller than 40% of $70.

What percent off is it? Divide the savings by the original price. A $120 jacket on sale for $90 is $30 ÷ $120 = 25% off.

Our guide on how to calculate a discount works through both, along with buy-one-get-one deals and price-per-unit comparisons.

Buy one, get one deals

Multi-buy deals are discounts in disguise. Work out the price you pay for everything, then compare it with the full price:

  • Buy one, get one free on two $30 items: you pay $30 for $60 of goods, which is 50% off each.
  • Buy one, get one 50% off: you pay $30 + $15 = $45 for $60, which is 25% off each.
  • Three for the price of two: you pay for two out of three, which is 33.3% off each.

These deals only save money if you’d have bought the extra items anyway.

Is the “original price” real?

The Federal Trade Commission’s Guides Against Deceptive Pricing say a “former price” used to advertise a discount should be one the item was actually offered at, in good faith, for a reasonable period. In practice, some “compare at” prices are higher than anyone ever paid. Before trusting a big percentage, check what the item sells for elsewhere and what this store charged last month. A 60% discount from an inflated list price can be a worse deal than 20% off an honest one.

Sales tax and discounts

Sales tax is usually worked out on the price after the store’s discount, so a discount saves you the tax on the amount taken off as well. On the $129.99 jacket at 7% tax, paying full price would cost $139.09 with tax. After both discounts, you pay $70.93, a total saving of $68.16 once the tax you avoid is counted.

Rates differ a lot by place. Some states have no statewide sales tax, while combined state and local rates in parts of other states are above 9%. Clothing, groceries and other items are exempt or taxed at a lower rate in some states. Enter the rate on your receipt under More options for an exact total. If you’re comparing an online price with a shop price, add tax to both, since most online sellers now collect it too.

Coupons, cash back and rewards

Not every saving comes off the price at the register, which affects how you should count it.

  • Store coupons and sale prices reduce the price you pay, and usually the sales tax too.
  • Manufacturer coupons also reduce what you pay, but some states tax the price before the coupon.
  • Cash back and rebates arrive later, so you pay the full price and tax first. A $20 rebate on a $100 item is a 20% discount only if you actually send in the form.
  • Loyalty points and store credit are worth something only if you spend them. Treat a “spend $100, get $20 back” offer as a 16.7% discount at best, because you have to spend another $20 to use it: $100 of goods for $100 now, then $20 of goods for the $20 credit, so $120 of goods for $100.

When you’re comparing offers, convert each one into the final cash price for the same items. That’s the only number that decides which deal is better.

Sale timing and price history

The same item can be discounted by very different amounts at different times of year. Seasonal clearance at the end of a season, holiday sale events and new model launches all tend to bring deeper markdowns. If a purchase can wait, watching the price for a few weeks often shows what a genuinely good discount looks like. Some stores also offer a price adjustment if the price drops shortly after you buy; it’s worth asking.

Discounts from the seller’s side

For a business, a discount comes out of profit, not just the price. A shop that buys an item for $45 and sells it for $75 makes $30 of gross profit. A 20% discount cuts the price to $60 but the profit to $15, so the shop needs to sell twice as many to earn the same. Sellers can check that effect with the margin calculator.

Key terms

  • Original price (list price): the price before any discount.
  • Sale price: the price after the discount, before tax.
  • Percent off: the discount as a percentage of the original price.
  • Stacked discount: a second discount applied to an already reduced price.
  • Markdown: a permanent price cut by the retailer, often for clearance.
  • Effective discount: the total amount saved as a percentage of the original price, after every discount.

What to do with your result

  • Compare deals on the final price, not the percentage. A 30%-off price at one store can still be higher than the regular price somewhere else.
  • Include tax and shipping before deciding. A bigger discount with $12 shipping can cost more than a smaller one with free delivery.
  • Check the price per unit for groceries and household goods. Bulk packs aren’t always cheaper per ounce.
  • Budget the savings. If you planned to spend $129.99 and paid $70.93, moving the difference to savings is how a discount becomes money you keep.

For other percentage questions, such as percent change or what percent one number is of another, use the percentage calculator. If you’re a seller deciding how much of a discount you can afford, the margin calculator shows what a sale does to your profit margin.

Frequently asked questions

How do I calculate a discount?

Multiply the original price by the discount as a decimal to get the savings, then subtract it from the price. For 25% off $80: $80 × 0.25 = $20 saved, so the sale price is $80 − $20 = $60. Or multiply by what's left: $80 × 0.75 = $60.

How do I calculate 20% off?

Multiply the price by 0.8. On $45, that's $36. In your head: 10% of $45 is $4.50, so 20% is $9.00, and $45 − $9 = $36.

Is 25% off plus an extra 10% off the same as 35% off?

No. The extra 10% comes off the already reduced price. On $80, 25% off gives $60, and 10% off $60 gives $54. That's $26 off, or 32.5% in total. Multiply the remaining shares: 0.75 × 0.90 = 0.675, so you pay 67.5% of the price.

How do I find the original price from the sale price?

Divide the sale price by one minus the discount. A $42 item marked 40% off was originally $42 ÷ 0.60 = $70. Don't add 40% back to $42; that gives $58.80, which is wrong.

How do I work out what percent off a price is?

Subtract the sale price from the original price, divide by the original price and multiply by 100. A $120 jacket on sale for $90 is ($120 − $90) ÷ $120 = 25% off.

Is sales tax charged on the original price or the sale price?

In most states, sales tax is charged on the price you actually pay after a store's own discount. Manufacturer coupons can be treated differently: some states tax the price before the coupon, because the store is reimbursed for it. Check your state's rules if the difference matters.

Which is better, a percent off or a dollar amount off?

Compare them at the price you're paying. A $20-off coupon beats 15% off on anything under $133.33, and 15% off wins above that. Divide the dollar amount by the percentage as a decimal to find the break-even price: $20 ÷ 0.15 = $133.33.
