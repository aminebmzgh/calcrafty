How to use this macro calculator

Worked example: cutting at 192 pounds

A 29-year-old man is 5 feet 11 inches, weighs 192 pounds and trains moderately. He wants to lose about a pound a week while keeping muscle, so he picks the high-protein split.

  • Target for 1 pound a week: 2,880 − 500 = 2,380 calories

How macros are calculated in plain English

Each gram of food energy comes from one of three macronutrients, and each provides a fixed number of calories, known as Atwater factors:

  • Protein: 4 calories per gram
  • Carbohydrate: 4 calories per gram
  • Fat: 9 calories per gram

So: grams = daily calories × macro percentage ÷ calories per gram. Daily calories come from your basal metabolic rate (Mifflin-St Jeor), multiplied by your activity factor and adjusted by about 500 calories per pound a week for your goal. The Show the math panel shows every step with your numbers. Alcohol provides 7 calories per gram but isn’t counted as a macro here.

Choosing a split

  • Balanced sits inside the National Academies’ recommended ranges and suits most people, including endurance athletes who need plenty of carbohydrate.
  • High protein helps with fullness and muscle retention during fat loss, and suits people who lift weights.
  • Low carb can make it easier for some people to control appetite. Fiber may run low, so emphasize vegetables, nuts and seeds.
  • Keto keeps carbs very low (usually under 50 g a day) to shift the body toward burning fat for fuel. It’s restrictive and isn’t suitable for everyone; get medical advice first if you have a health condition.

If you’re unsure, start with balanced or high protein for two to three weeks, track your weight and energy, and adjust from there.

How much protein do you need?

Protein is the macro most worth getting right, because it supports muscle, keeps you full and matters more as you age. Needs are usually expressed per kilogram of body weight:

Body weight RDA (0.8 g/kg) 1.2 g/kg 1.6 g/kg 2.0 g/kg
130 lb (59 kg) 47 g 71 g 94 g 118 g
160 lb (73 kg) 58 g 87 g 116 g 145 g
190 lb (86 kg) 69 g 103 g 138 g 172 g
220 lb (100 kg) 80 g 120 g 160 g 200 g
  • 0.8 g/kg is the Recommended Dietary Allowance, the amount that meets the needs of nearly all healthy, mostly sedentary adults.
  • 1.2 to 2.0 g/kg is the range the International Society of Sports Nutrition suggests for people who exercise regularly, toward the upper end when building muscle or losing fat.
  • Older adults may benefit from at least 1.0 to 1.2 g/kg to help limit age-related muscle loss, according to an international expert group on protein in older age.

If you have kidney disease, talk to your doctor before raising protein intake.

Recommended ranges for each macro

The National Academies’ Acceptable Macronutrient Distribution Ranges (AMDR) for adults are:

Macronutrient Share of calories
Protein 10–35%
Carbohydrate 45–65%
Fat 20–35%

The balanced split fits inside all three. The high-protein split stays within them too, apart from carbohydrate at 40%, slightly below the range. Low-carb and keto splits fall well outside the carbohydrate range by design, which is why they call for more planning to get enough fiber, vitamins and minerals.

The type of each macro matters as well as the amount. The Dietary Guidelines for Americans recommend keeping added sugars and saturated fat each below 10% of daily calories. On a 2,000-calorie diet, that’s under 200 calories from each, or about 50 grams of added sugar and 22 grams of saturated fat.

Macros for different goals

The right split depends more on what you’re training for than on any single “best” diet:

  • Losing fat: keep protein high (around 1.6 to 2.2 g/kg) so more of the weight you lose comes from fat, and let carbohydrate and fat share the rest according to what keeps you full.
  • Building muscle: a small calorie surplus with about 1.6 g/kg of protein, plus enough carbohydrate to fuel hard training sessions.
  • Endurance training: carbohydrate is the main fuel for long or intense sessions. Sports nutrition guidelines from the American College of Sports Medicine suggest roughly 5 to 7 g/kg a day for moderate training and 6 to 10 g/kg for heavy training, more than a low-carb split provides.
  • General health: the balanced split, built mostly from whole foods, covers the needs of most adults.

Tracking macros without obsessing

Weighing every gram isn’t necessary for most people. A food-tracking app for two or three weeks teaches you what your usual meals contain; after that, many people switch to simpler habits, such as building each meal around a palm-sized portion of protein, a fist of carbohydrate, a thumb of fat and plenty of vegetables. Recheck with an app now and then if progress stalls, or after a change in routine such as a new job or training plan. Hitting your protein and calorie targets on most days matters far more than matching every single macro exactly.

Good sources of each macronutrient

  • Protein: chicken, fish, eggs, Greek yogurt, tofu, lentils, beans, lean beef and protein powder.
  • Carbohydrate: oats, rice, potatoes, whole-grain bread, fruit, beans and vegetables.
  • Fat: olive oil, nuts, seeds, avocado, oily fish and cheese.

Spreading protein across meals, with roughly 20 to 40 grams per meal for most adults, is a simple way to reach a higher daily target without forcing a huge serving at dinner.

Adjusting your macros over time

Your first targets are a starting point. After two or three weeks, check your average weight, energy and performance. If weight isn’t moving in the direction you want, adjust calories first, usually by 100 to 200 a day, and keep protein steady. If workouts feel flat on a low-carb split, shifting 10% of calories from fat to carbohydrate often helps. Recalculate whenever your weight changes by about 10 pounds.

Fiber and water matter too, even though they aren’t counted as macros. The Dietary Guidelines for Americans suggest about 14 grams of fiber per 1,000 calories, which comes mostly from vegetables, fruit, beans and whole grains.

Key terms

  • Macronutrients: protein, carbohydrate and fat, the nutrients that provide calories.
  • Atwater factors: 4, 4 and 9 calories per gram of protein, carbohydrate and fat.
  • AMDR: Acceptable Macronutrient Distribution Range, the recommended share of calories from each macro.
  • RDA: Recommended Dietary Allowance; for protein, 0.8 g per kg of body weight.

Frequently asked questions

How do I calculate my macros?

First estimate your daily calories from your basal metabolic rate, activity and goal. Then multiply the calories by each macro's percentage and divide by its calories per gram, which is 4 for protein, 4 for carbohydrate and 9 for fat. On 2,000 calories with 25% protein, that's 2,000 × 0.25 ÷ 4 = 125 grams of protein.

What is a good macro split for weight loss?

Any split can work if total calories are in a deficit, but higher protein helps you feel full and keep muscle while you lose fat. Many people do well with 30% to 35% protein, 35% to 45% carbohydrate and 25% to 30% fat. The best split is the one you can sustain with foods you enjoy.

How much protein do I need a day?

The US Recommended Dietary Allowance is 0.8 grams per kilogram of body weight for adults, the amount that prevents deficiency. The International Society of Sports Nutrition suggests 1.4 to 2.0 g/kg for people who exercise regularly, and more during fat loss. The calculator shows your protein per kilogram so you can check where you land.

What are the recommended ranges for carbs, fat and protein?

The National Academies' Acceptable Macronutrient Distribution Ranges for adults are 45% to 65% of calories from carbohydrate, 20% to 35% from fat and 10% to 35% from protein. Low-carb and keto diets deliberately go outside these ranges; talk to a doctor first if you have diabetes, kidney disease or take medication.

What is IIFYM?

The phrase "if it fits your macros" describes a flexible approach where any food is allowed as long as your daily protein, carb and fat totals hit their targets. It can make tracking easier to stick with, though food quality, fiber and micronutrients still matter for health.

How precise do I need to be with macros?

Not very. Food labels can legally be off by a margin, and daily needs vary. Landing within about 10% of each target most days is enough to see results. Prioritize hitting your protein and calorie targets; carbs and fat can flex from day to day.
