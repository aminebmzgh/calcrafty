How to use this standard deviation calculator

  1. Your numbers. Separate them with commas, spaces or new lines. You can paste a column straight from Excel or Google Sheets. Anything that isn’t a number is skipped and reported.

The calculation uses exact decimal arithmetic, which avoids the rounding problems that can appear when values are very large or have many decimal places.

Worked example: seven quiz scores

A teacher records seven quiz scores: 12, 15, 9, 20, 14, 18 and 11.

  1. Mean: (12 + 15 + 9 + 20 + 14 + 18 + 11) ÷ 7 = 99 ÷ 7 ≈ 14.143
  2. Deviations from the mean: −2.143, 0.857, −5.143, 5.857, −0.143, 3.857, −3.143
  3. Squared deviations: 4.592, 0.735, 26.449, 34.306, 0.020, 14.878, 9.878, which add up to about 90.857
  4. Sample variance: 90.857 ÷ (7 − 1) ≈ 15.143
  5. Sample standard deviation: √15.143 ≈ 3.891

If these seven scores were the entire class rather than a sample, the population variance would be 90.857 ÷ 7 ≈ 12.980 and the population standard deviation √12.980 ≈ 3.603.

So a typical score is a little under 4 points from the average of about 14.

The formulas in plain English

  • Mean: x̄ = Σx ÷ n, the sum of the values divided by how many there are.
  • Population variance: σ² = Σ(x − x̄)² ÷ n
  • Sample variance: s² = Σ(x − x̄)² ÷ (n − 1)
  • Standard deviation: the square root of the variance, σ = √σ² or s = √s².
  • Standard error of the mean: s ÷ √n

Dividing by n − 1 instead of n for a sample is called Bessel’s correction. A sample’s own mean is always closer to its values than the true population mean is, so squared deviations from it are slightly too small; dividing by n − 1 corrects that bias in the variance.

When to use sample vs population

Use population only when your data is the complete group you’re describing: every employee’s salary at one company, all 30 students in one class, every game a team played this season. Use sample when the data represents a larger group you didn’t fully measure: a survey of 500 voters, 20 items from a production line, a clinical trial. When in doubt, use sample; it’s the default in most statistics software, including Excel’s STDEV.S and Google Sheets’ STDEV.

Interpreting your result

Standard deviation is easiest to read relative to the mean. A standard deviation of 5 is large for values averaging 10 but small for values averaging 1,000. The coefficient of variation (standard deviation ÷ mean) puts different data sets on the same footing.

Outliers have a big effect because deviations are squared. One extreme value can inflate the standard deviation a lot, so check the deviations table for a single row that dominates the sum.

The 68-95-99.7 rule

When data follows a roughly bell-shaped (normal) distribution, standard deviation tells you where most values fall:

Range Share of values
Within 1 standard deviation of the mean about 68%
Within 2 standard deviations about 95%
Within 3 standard deviations about 99.7%

For example, if exam scores have a mean of 500 and a standard deviation of 100, about 68% of students score between 400 and 600, about 95% between 300 and 700, and almost everyone between 200 and 800. A score of 750 is unusual: only about 2.5% of students score above 700.

The rule only applies to roughly symmetric, bell-shaped data. Incomes, house prices and waiting times are usually skewed, with a long tail on one side, so a range of mean ± 2 standard deviations can even include impossible negative values. Look at the data before relying on the rule.

Z-scores: comparing results from different groups

A z-score expresses a value as a number of standard deviations from the mean: z = (value − mean) ÷ standard deviation. It lets you compare results measured on different scales.

Say a student scores 82 on a chemistry test where the class mean was 74 with a standard deviation of 6, and 88 on a history test with a mean of 82 and a standard deviation of 8.

  • Chemistry: (82 − 74) ÷ 6 = 1.33 standard deviations above average
  • History: (88 − 82) ÷ 8 = 0.75 standard deviations above average

The chemistry score is the stronger performance relative to the class, even though the raw score is lower.

Standard deviation, standard error and range

These three measures are easy to confuse:

  • Standard deviation describes how spread out the individual values are. For the seven quiz scores, it’s about 3.89.
  • Standard error of the mean (standard deviation ÷ √n) describes how precisely the sample mean estimates the true mean. For the quiz scores, it’s 3.891 ÷ √7 ≈ 1.47. It shrinks as you collect more data; the standard deviation doesn’t.
  • Range is the largest value minus the smallest: 20 − 9 = 11. It’s quick to calculate but depends entirely on the two most extreme values.

Variance is the standard deviation squared. It’s useful in further calculations, such as combining the spread of independent sources, but it’s in squared units, so the standard deviation is easier to interpret.

How sample size affects the result

A sample’s standard deviation is an estimate of the spread in the whole population, and small samples give rough estimates. With five or six values, one unusual number can shift the result a lot; with hundreds, it barely moves it.

Collecting more data doesn’t make the standard deviation smaller. If the population is genuinely spread out, the standard deviation stays roughly the same however many values you add. What shrinks is the standard error, because it divides by √n: quadrupling the number of values halves the standard error, so your estimate of the mean becomes twice as precise. That’s why surveys and experiments need large samples to detect small differences, and why a result based on a handful of values deserves real caution, however precise the number looks.

Mean absolute deviation: a simpler alternative

The mean absolute deviation (MAD) is the average distance from the mean, without squaring. For the seven quiz scores it’s about 3.02, noticeably smaller than the standard deviation of 3.89, because squaring gives extra weight to the values furthest from the mean.

MAD is easier to explain and less affected by a single outlier, which is why it’s often taught first. Standard deviation is used far more widely, though, because it fits neatly into the rest of statistics, from the normal distribution to confidence intervals and regression.

Calculating it in Excel or Google Sheets

Spreadsheets have built-in functions that match this calculator’s results:

  • STDEV.S (or STDEV in Google Sheets): sample standard deviation
  • STDEV.P: population standard deviation
  • VAR.S and VAR.P: sample and population variance
  • AVERAGE: the mean

If your spreadsheet answer differs slightly from the calculator’s, check whether you’ve used the sample or population version, and that no blank or text cells were accidentally included in the range.

Standard deviation in the real world

Standard deviation turns “how spread out is this?” into a single number, which is why it shows up in so many fields:

  • Test scores. Standardized tests report scores relative to the mean in standard deviations; a score one standard deviation above the mean is better than about 84% of test-takers if scores are roughly normal.
  • Finance. The volatility of an investment is the standard deviation of its returns. Two funds with the same average return can have very different risk.
  • Manufacturing. Quality control charts flag a process when measurements drift more than about three standard deviations from the target.
  • Science and medicine. Results are often reported as mean ± SD, and the standard error is used to build confidence intervals around averages.
  • Weather. A month’s temperature can be described as its average and how much days typically vary around it.

In each case, standard deviation is most useful alongside the mean and a look at the data itself. A chart or the deviations table quickly shows whether one unusual value is driving the result, or whether the data is skewed in a way that a single number can’t capture.

In practice, report the standard deviation with the same precision as your data, and say whether it is the sample or population version.

Key terms

  • Mean (x̄): the arithmetic average.
  • Deviation: a value minus the mean.
  • Variance: the average squared deviation.
  • Standard deviation: the square root of variance, in the same units as the data.
  • Bessel’s correction: dividing by n − 1 for a sample.
  • Standard error: the standard deviation of the sample mean.

Frequently asked questions

What is standard deviation?

Standard deviation measures how spread out numbers are around their average. A small standard deviation means values cluster near the mean; a large one means they're scattered. It's in the same units as the data, so a standard deviation of 4 points on a test means a typical score is about 4 points from the class average.

What's the difference between sample and population standard deviation?

Population standard deviation (σ) divides the sum of squared deviations by n, and applies when your data includes every member of the group. Sample standard deviation (s) divides by n − 1, which corrects for the fact that a sample's mean is estimated from the same data. Use the sample version for surveys, experiments and most real-world data.

How do you calculate standard deviation by hand?

Find the mean, subtract it from each value, square each difference, add the squares, divide by n − 1 (sample) or n (population) to get the variance, and take the square root. For 2, 4, 4, 4, 5, 5, 7, 9 the mean is 5, the squared deviations add up to 32, and the population standard deviation is √(32 ÷ 8) = 2.

What is variance?

Variance is the average squared distance from the mean, the step before taking the square root. It's useful in statistical formulas, but because it's in squared units (such as dollars squared), standard deviation is easier to interpret.

What is the standard error of the mean?

It estimates how much a sample's mean would vary from sample to sample: the sample standard deviation divided by the square root of n. It shrinks as samples get larger, which is why bigger studies give more precise averages.

What does a standard deviation tell me about a normal distribution?

For data that is roughly bell-shaped, about 68% of values fall within one standard deviation of the mean, about 95% within two, and about 99.7% within three. This "68-95-99.7 rule" doesn't hold for skewed data, so check the shape of your data before relying on it.
