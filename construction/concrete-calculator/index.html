What this means

You need about 1.36 cubic yards (36.67 cubic feet) of concrete, including 10% extra for waste.

That’s 62 bags of 80 lb mix, about 4,960 lb to haul and mix by hand. At around a cubic yard or more, ready-mix delivered by truck is usually easier: order 1.5 cubic yards.

Concrete can’t be topped up once it starts to set, so running short is worse than having a little left over.

How to use this concrete calculator

  1. Choose the shape.
    • Slab: patios, floors, driveways and walkways.
    • Footing or wall: long, narrow pours such as a continuous footing or a low wall.
    • Round column: post holes, piers and tube forms.
  2. Enter the dimensions. Lengths and widths go in feet; thickness, footing width and depth, and diameter go in inches, because that’s how they’re usually measured.
  3. How many: for identical pieces, such as ten post holes, enter the count and the calculator multiplies the volume.
  4. Extra for waste: add 5% to 10% to cover spillage, uneven ground and forms that bow outward under the weight of wet concrete.
  5. Bag size: pick 80, 60 or 40 lb bags to see how many you’d need.

The formulas

All shapes start with volume in cubic feet:

  • Slab: length (ft) × width (ft) × thickness (in) ÷ 12
  • Footing or wall: length (ft) × width (in) ÷ 12 × depth (in) ÷ 12
  • Round column: π × (diameter (in) ÷ 24)² × height (ft)

Then:

Cubic yards = cubic feet ÷ 27

Bags = cubic feet ÷ yield per bag, rounded up

A cubic yard is a cube 3 feet on each side, so it holds 3 × 3 × 3 = 27 cubic feet. Bag yields are those QUIKRETE publishes for its standard Concrete Mix: about 0.30 cubic feet for a 40 lb bag, 0.45 for 60 lb and 0.60 for 80 lb. Other brands are similar, but check the bag.

Worked example: a 10 × 10 ft patio

A square patio 10 feet on each side, 4 inches thick:

  • Volume: 10 × 10 × (4 ÷ 12) = 33.33 cubic feet.
  • Cubic yards: 33.33 ÷ 27 = 1.23 cubic yards.
  • With 10% waste: 33.33 × 1.10 = 36.67 cubic feet, or 1.36 cubic yards.
  • Bags: 36.67 ÷ 0.60 = 61.1, so 62 bags of 80 lb, about 4,960 lb of mix. In 60 lb bags it’s 82; in 40 lb bags, 123.
  • Ready-mix: 1.36 cubic yards rounded up to the next quarter yard is 1.5 cubic yards.

That’s a lot of bags to carry and mix by hand. For a pour this size, a small ready-mix delivery or a rented mixer is worth pricing.

Bagged concrete or ready-mix?

Bagged concrete is premixed cement, sand and gravel that you mix with water in a wheelbarrow or a portable mixer. It’s practical for small jobs: setting posts, small pads, steps and repairs. You can work at your own pace, a few bags at a time.

Ready-mix concrete is delivered by truck, mixed to a specified strength and ready to place. It makes sense for anything more than about a cubic yard, because:

  • The batch is consistent, so the whole slab cures evenly.
  • It arrives all at once, avoiding cold joints where a fresh batch meets one that has started to set.
  • It saves enormous labor. Even 1.5 cubic yards is 68 bags of 80 lb mix.

Ready-mix is sold by the cubic yard, often in quarter-yard or half-yard increments, and many suppliers charge a short-load fee for small orders. When you call, give the volume from this calculator, the strength you need (often 3,000 to 4,000 psi for residential flatwork, but ask what’s recommended locally) and how the truck will reach the site. If the truck can’t get close, ask about a pump or plan for wheelbarrows.

Measuring accurately

  • Measure the forms, not the plan. Inside the forms is what gets filled.
  • Check depth in several places. Ground is rarely perfectly level; use the average depth, or the deepest point if you want a margin.
  • Account for a gravel base. Slabs usually sit on a compacted gravel base of about 4 inches, which reduces the concrete needed if it’s already in place. Our gravel calculator works out how much base material you need.
  • Divide complex shapes into rectangles. An L-shaped patio is two rectangles; calculate each and add them.
  • Thickened edges and footings: many slabs have a deeper edge. Calculate it as a footing and add it to the slab.

Mixing and curing tips

  • Follow the water quantity on the bag. Extra water makes concrete easier to pour but weaker once it cures.
  • Don’t pour on frozen ground or in extreme heat without the precautions your supplier recommends.
  • Keep it moist while it cures. Concrete gains strength over days and weeks; keeping the surface damp, or covering it, for the first several days helps prevent cracking.
  • Plan control joints. Cutting or tooling joints into a slab encourages cracks to form in straight lines where you want them. A common rule of thumb is a spacing in feet of 2 to 3 times the slab thickness in inches: 8 to 12 feet apart for a 4-inch slab.
  • Wear gloves and eye protection. Wet concrete is caustic and can burn skin.

Concrete, cement and mortar

The words are often used interchangeably, but they are different materials:

  • Cement is the fine gray powder (usually portland cement) that acts as the glue. It is never used on its own for a slab.
  • Concrete is cement mixed with sand, gravel or crushed stone and water. The stone gives it strength, which is why it is used for slabs, footings and posts.
  • Mortar is cement mixed with sand and lime but no gravel. It bonds bricks and blocks together and is not strong enough for a slab.

This calculator is for concrete. If you are buying bags, look for a product labeled concrete mix rather than mortar mix or sand mix. Some products are formulated for specific jobs: fast-setting mixes for fence posts let you skip mixing in a wheelbarrow, and high-strength mixes are made for heavier loads. Their yields per bag can differ from standard concrete mix, so check the bag. If the yield is different, divide the cubic feet shown above by the yield printed on the bag and round up to get the number of bags you need.

Metric conversions

The calculator also shows the volume in cubic meters, using the exact factor 1 cubic foot = 0.028316846592 cubic meters. One cubic yard is about 0.765 cubic meters. If you’re ordering ready-mix in the United States, though, it will be quoted in cubic yards.

Planning the whole project

Concrete is usually only part of the job. If you’re building a patio or shed, you may also need gravel for the base, and if you’re finishing a basement floor or garage, you’ll want to plan flooring and paint. The flooring calculator and paint calculator work out those quantities. For larger home projects financed with home equity, the HELOC calculator shows what borrowing would cost.

Key terms

  • Cubic yard: 27 cubic feet, the unit ready-mix concrete is sold in.
  • Yield: the volume of mixed concrete one bag produces.
  • Ready-mix: concrete batched at a plant and delivered by truck.
  • Footing: the concrete base that spreads the load of a wall or column into the ground.
  • Control joint: a groove in a slab that controls where cracks form.
  • Cold joint: a weak line where fresh concrete meets concrete that has already started to set.
  • psi: pounds per square inch, the unit for concrete compressive strength.
  • Curing: the period after the pour when concrete hardens and gains strength, which depends on keeping it moist and at a moderate temperature.

Frequently asked questions

How much concrete do I need for a 10 x 10 slab?

A 10 × 10 ft slab 4 inches thick is 33.33 cubic feet, or 1.23 cubic yards. With 10% extra for waste, plan on 1.36 cubic yards: 62 bags of 80 lb mix, or a ready-mix order of 1.5 cubic yards.

How many bags of concrete are in a cubic yard?

A cubic yard is 27 cubic feet. An 80 lb bag of premixed concrete yields about 0.60 cubic feet, so a cubic yard takes 45 bags of 80 lb, 60 bags of 60 lb or 90 bags of 40 lb, before any allowance for waste.

How do I calculate cubic yards of concrete?

Multiply length × width × thickness, all in feet, to get cubic feet, then divide by 27. Convert inches to feet by dividing by 12: a 4-inch slab is 0.333 ft thick.

How thick should a concrete slab be?

About 4 inches for patios, walkways and shed floors, and 5 to 6 inches for driveways that carry cars, or more for heavy vehicles. Local building codes and soil conditions can require more, so check before you pour.

Should I use bags or ready-mix concrete?

Bags are practical for small jobs, up to around a cubic yard. Beyond that, the number of bags becomes a lot of lifting and mixing, and a ready-mix truck delivers a consistent batch in one pour. Many suppliers have a minimum order or charge a short-load fee for small deliveries.

How much extra concrete should I order?

Most people add 5% to 10% for spillage, uneven ground and forms that bow slightly. Running short in the middle of a pour can leave a weak joint, so it's better to have a little left over.
