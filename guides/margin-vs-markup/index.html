Margin vs Markup: The Difference, With Conversion Charts

Margin and markup describe the same profit from two different starting points. Here's how each is calculated, how to convert between them, and the pricing mistakes that come from mixing them up.

Reviewed by Amine Boumazzoughe, bank auditor · Updated November 7, 2026

Short answer: markup and margin both measure the profit on a sale, but they divide it by different numbers. Markup divides the profit by the cost. Margin divides it by the selling price. Because the price is bigger than the cost, the margin is always the smaller percentage. A 100% markup is only a 50% margin. The margin calculator shows both for any cost and price.

The two formulas side by side

Start with gross profit: profit = price − cost. Then:

Compared Markup Margin
Formula profit ÷ cost profit ÷ price
Answers the question How much did I add on top of what I paid? How much of each sale is profit?
Range 0% to no upper limit 0% to just under 100%
Used most by wholesalers, contractors, cost-plus pricing retailers, finance, income statements

Take a coffee shop latte. The milk, espresso, cup and lid cost $1.20, and it sells for $5.00. The gross profit is $3.80.

  • Markup: $3.80 ÷ $1.20 = 316.67%.
  • Margin: $3.80 ÷ $5.00 = 76%.

Both numbers are correct. They just answer different questions, and confusing them leads to prices that are wrong by a lot.

Converting between margin and markup

You don’t need the cost or price to convert one percentage to the other. Using decimals:

Margin = markup ÷ (1 + markup)

Markup = margin ÷ (1 − margin)

So a 40% markup is 0.40 ÷ 1.40 = 28.57% margin, and a 35% margin is 0.35 ÷ 0.65 = 53.85% markup.

Notice how the two drift apart. At small percentages they’re close: 5% margin is 5.26% markup. At high percentages the markup explodes: a 90% margin needs a 900% markup, because the cost is only a tenth of the price.

Why the difference matters so much

The most expensive pricing mistake is applying a margin target as if it were a markup. Suppose a distributor needs a 25% margin on everything it sells and has $100,000 of product cost in a month.

  • Applied correctly as a margin: price = $100,000 ÷ 0.75 = $133,333.33. Gross profit is $33,333.33, which is 25% of sales.
  • Applied by mistake as a 25% markup: price = $100,000 × 1.25 = $125,000. Gross profit is $25,000, which is only a 20% margin.

The mistake costs $8,333.33 of gross profit that month, and nothing on any single invoice looks wrong.

It works the other way too. A salesperson told they can offer “up to 10% off” may think that’s small, but on an item priced with a 25% margin, a 10% discount cuts the profit from 25¢ to 15¢ of every original dollar, a 40% drop in profit. How different industries use each

Retail. Shops usually plan in margins, because their accounts and their rent, wages and other overheads are measured against sales. Many speak of keystone pricing, doubling the wholesale cost: a 100% markup, or a 50% margin.

The supply chain. Markups stack up along the way. A manufacturer that makes an item for $8 might sell it to a wholesaler for $16, a 100% markup. If the retailer then doubles that to $32, the item has been marked up 300% from the manufacturer’s cost, even though each business earns a 50% margin on its own sale.

Restaurants. Kitchens often talk about food cost percentage: ingredient cost as a share of the menu price, which is simply 100% minus the margin on the food. A dish with $4.20 of ingredients priced to run a 30% food cost sells for $4.20 ÷ 0.30 = $14.00. That’s a 70% margin on the food, or a 233.33% markup, before labor, rent and everything else.

Contracting and services. Builders, IT providers and agencies often quote “cost plus” a markup, because it’s easy to apply to a list of materials and hours. A 20% markup on a $20,000 job gives $24,000, which is a 16.67% margin.

Financial statements. Accountants report gross margin: gross profit divided by revenue. A business with $250,000 of revenue and $155,000 of cost of goods sold has $95,000 of gross profit and a 38% gross margin. Markup doesn’t appear on an income statement at all.

Worked example: setting a price for a target margin

An online shop pays $20 for a product, including inbound shipping, and wants a 25% margin.

  1. Convert the margin to a decimal: 25% = 0.25.
  2. Divide the cost by one minus the margin: $20 ÷ 0.75 = $26.67.
  3. Check: profit is $6.67, and $6.67 ÷ $26.67 = 25%.
  4. The markup this represents: $6.67 ÷ $20 = 33.33%.

If the shop had simply added 25% to the cost, it would price the item at $25 and earn a 20% margin. If it had added 10% “to be safe” on a $24 cost, giving $26.40, it would earn only about 9.09%.

Price multipliers: a shortcut for price lists

Many businesses turn their target margin into a single price multiplier and apply it to every cost. The multiplier is 1 ÷ (1 − margin):

Target margin Multiply cost by
20% 1.25
25% 1.3333
30% 1.4286
40% 1.6667
50% 2.0
60% 2.5

The multiplier is just one plus the markup. A 40% margin needs a multiplier of 1.6667, which is the same as a 66.67% markup. Put the multiplier in a spreadsheet column next to your costs, and every price comes out at the margin you want, with no confusion between the two percentages.

Raising prices vs cutting costs

Because margin and markup measure the same profit, changes to price and cost affect both, but not equally. Start with an item that costs $30 and sells for $50: a $20 profit, a 40% margin and a 66.67% markup.

  • Raise the price by 10% to $55: profit becomes $25, the margin rises to 45.45% and the markup to 83.33%.
  • Cut the cost by 10% to $27: profit becomes $23, the margin rises to 46% and the markup to 85.19%.
  • The supplier raises the cost by 10% to $33 and you keep the $50 price: profit falls to $17 and the margin to 34%.

A small change in cost moves the margin a lot when margins are thin. That’s why businesses with low margins, such as grocery stores and distributors, watch their purchase prices so closely.

Quick conversions in your head

A few pairs are worth memorizing, because they come up constantly:

  • 20% margin = 25% markup.
  • 25% margin = 33⅓% markup.
  • 33⅓% margin = 50% markup.
  • 50% margin = 100% markup.
  • 60% margin = 150% markup.
  • 75% margin = 300% markup.

For anything else, the rule of thumb is that markup is always the larger number, and the gap widens quickly above a 40% margin.

Gross margin, operating margin and net margin

“Margin” on its own usually means gross margin: price minus the direct cost of the goods, divided by price. Two other margins appear in business reports, and they’re always lower:

  • Operating margin subtracts operating expenses too: wages, rent, marketing, software and so on.
  • Net margin subtracts everything, including interest and income taxes.

A shop might earn a 50% gross margin, an 8% operating margin and a 5% net margin. When someone quotes a margin, ask which one they mean. Markup, by contrast, nearly always refers to the price over the direct cost.

Common mistakes

  1. Treating the two percentages as interchangeable. They’re only close at very low percentages.
  2. Adding a margin to cost. To hit a margin, divide by (1 − margin); never multiply by (1 + margin).
  3. Quoting a markup to a buyer who thinks in margins, or the other way round. Say which one you mean.
  4. Forgetting costs that come with every sale, such as packaging, payment fees and free shipping. They reduce the real margin.
  5. Comparing your markup with a competitor’s margin. A rival’s “40%” and your “40%” may be very different numbers.

Key terms

  • Cost of goods sold (COGS): the direct cost of the products sold in a period, used to work out gross profit.
  • Gross profit: revenue minus cost of goods sold.
  • Markup: gross profit as a percentage of cost.
  • Gross margin: gross profit as a percentage of revenue or selling price.
  • Keystone: a 100% markup, which is a 50% margin.
  • Price multiplier: the number you multiply a cost by to reach a price; equal to one plus the markup.
  • Food cost percentage: ingredient cost as a share of the menu price, used in restaurants.

Which one should you use?

Pick one, write it down and use it everywhere: on quotes, in your price list and in your targets. For most businesses, margin is the better planning number, because it ties directly to how much of each sales dollar is left to pay for everything else. Markup is handy on the shop floor, because it turns a cost into a price with one multiplication. Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between margin and markup?

Markup is profit as a percentage of cost; margin is profit as a percentage of the selling price. A latte that costs $1.20 to make and sells for $5.00 has $3.80 of profit: a 316.67% markup but a 76% margin.

Is a 50% markup the same as a 50% margin?

No. A 50% markup on a $20 cost gives a $30 price and a 33.33% margin. To earn a 50% margin, you need a 100% markup, which doubles the cost to $40.

How do I convert markup to margin?

Divide the markup by one plus the markup, using decimals. A 40% markup is 0.40 ÷ 1.40 = 0.2857, or a 28.57% margin.

How do I convert margin to markup?

Divide the margin by one minus the margin, using decimals. A 30% margin is 0.30 ÷ 0.70 = 0.4286, or a 42.86% markup.

Which is higher, margin or markup?

For any profitable sale, markup is always the higher percentage, because it divides the same profit by the smaller number (the cost). They are equal only at zero profit.

Should I price using margin or markup?

Use whichever your business plans in, but be consistent. Margin connects directly to your income statement and to how much of each sale is left to cover overhead, so many businesses set targets as margins and then convert them to the markup they apply to costs.
