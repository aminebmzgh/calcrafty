How Much Do I Need to Retire?

How to find your retirement number from the income you want, Social Security and how long retirement lasts.

Reviewed by Amine Boumazzoughe, bank auditor · Updated November 2, 2026

Short answer: figure out the yearly income you’ll want, subtract what Social Security and any pension will cover, and multiply the gap by about 25. That rule of thumb, the flip side of the “4% rule,” gives a rough nest egg in today’s dollars. For a precise answer that accounts for your age, savings, returns and inflation, use the retirement calculator.

Step 1: Estimate the income you’ll need

A common starting point is 70% to 80% of your income before retirement. Some costs disappear when you stop working: commuting, payroll taxes and retirement contributions. Others grow, such as healthcare and travel. Build your own estimate from current spending if you can: housing (is your mortgage paid off?), food, transport, insurance, healthcare and the things you want to do.

Step 2: Subtract guaranteed income

Social Security is the foundation for most Americans. Your benefit depends on your 35 highest-earning years and the age you claim: claiming at 62 permanently reduces it, while waiting past full retirement age (67 for anyone born in 1960 or later) increases it up to age 70. Get your estimate at ssa.gov/myaccount and include any pension or annuity income.

The yearly gap your savings must cover is: income you want − Social Security − pensions.

Step 3: Turn the gap into a savings target

Your savings need to pay that gap every year for as long as retirement lasts, rising with inflation, while the rest stays invested. Assuming retirement at 67, money lasting to 92, a 4.5% return during retirement and 2.5% inflation, here’s the nest egg needed at 67, in today’s dollars, for different income targets with $2,000 a month of Social Security:

Income wanted Gap after Social Security Nest egg needed
$50,000 $26,000 about $520,000
$75,000 $51,000 about $1,021,000
$100,000 $76,000 about $1,521,000

Notice the multiplier: each target is about 20 times the yearly gap under these assumptions. The popular 25× rule (the 4% rule) is more conservative because it aims for 30 years or longer and allows for poor market sequences.

The 4% rule in context

The 4% rule comes from studies of historical US stock and bond returns: a 4% first-year withdrawal, raised each year with inflation, survived most 30-year periods. It’s a useful benchmark, but:

  • It assumes a balanced portfolio and no big fees.
  • Retiring early means a longer retirement and a lower safe rate.
  • Poor returns in the first few years of retirement, known as sequence-of-returns risk, hurt the most.
  • Being flexible, such as spending less after a bad year, makes savings last longer.

How much should you have saved by your age?

Fidelity Investments publishes widely used savings milestones, expressed as multiples of your salary, aimed at retiring at 67 with a lifestyle similar to the one you have while working:

By age Saved for retirement
30 1× your salary
40 3× your salary
50 6× your salary
60 8× your salary
67 10× your salary

They’re rough checkpoints, not rules. Someone with a generous pension needs less; someone who plans to retire early or spend more needs more. If you’re behind a milestone, the gap tells you to act, not that it’s too late.

A worked example: on track at 45?

A 45-year-old earning around $100,000 has $150,000 saved and contributes $1,000 a month including the employer match. They want $80,000 a year in today’s dollars from age 67, expect $2,500 a month from Social Security, and plan to age 92 with a 6% return before retirement, 4.5% during and 2.5% inflation.

  • Gap savings must cover: $80,000 − $30,000 = $50,000 a year
  • Nest egg needed at 67: about $1,001,000 in today’s dollars
  • Projected savings at 67: about $642,000 in today’s dollars
  • At this pace, the money runs out around age 81.
  • To close the gap: raise savings to about $2,130 a month.

Working until 70 instead cuts the monthly amount needed to about $1,454, and claiming Social Security at 70 would raise the benefit further. Small adjustments to several levers at once, such as saving a little more, working a little longer and spending a little less, are often easier than one big change.

Retiring before 65

Early retirement adds two hurdles. First, health insurance: Medicare starts at 65, so you’ll need coverage until then, through a spouse’s employer, COBRA or the Health Insurance Marketplace. Second, access to savings: withdrawals from 401(k)s and IRAs before age 59½ usually carry a 10% penalty on top of income tax. Exceptions exist, such as the “rule of 55,” which allows penalty-free withdrawals from your current employer’s 401(k) if you leave that job in or after the year you turn 55. Early retirees also face a longer retirement, so a lower withdrawal rate than 4% is prudent.

When to claim Social Security

Your claiming age permanently changes your monthly benefit. For anyone born in 1960 or later, full retirement age is 67, and the benefit as a share of the full amount is:

Claim at Share of full benefit
62 70%
63 75%
64 80%
65 86.7%
66 93.3%
67 100%
68 108%
69 116%
70 124%

Someone whose full benefit at 67 would be $2,000 a month gets $1,400 by claiming at 62 or $2,480 by waiting until 70, for life, with inflation adjustments either way. Waiting tends to pay off if you’re healthy, expect a long retirement or are the higher earner in a married couple, because a surviving spouse can keep the larger benefit. Claiming early can make sense with health concerns or no other income. The decision interacts with your savings: a bigger nest egg can bridge the years before a delayed claim.

Inflation: why your target is larger than it looks

All the figures in this guide are in today’s dollars, but you’ll spend future dollars. At 2.5% inflation, $80,000 of spending today becomes about $138,000 a year by the time a 45-year-old reaches 67, and keeps rising during retirement. The retirement calculator handles this automatically, inflating the income you want to the year you retire and raising withdrawals every year after. It’s also why some growth investments usually belong in a retirement portfolio even after you stop working: cash alone rarely keeps up with rising prices over a 25-year retirement.

Healthcare and long-term care

Healthcare is one of the largest and least predictable retirement costs. Medicare covers much of it from 65, but you’ll still pay premiums for Part B and usually a supplement or Medicare Advantage plan, plus prescription coverage, deductibles, dental, vision and hearing. Long-term care, such as help at home or in a nursing facility, generally isn’t covered by Medicare and can be very expensive. Including healthcare explicitly in your income estimate, and keeping a separate cushion or insurance for long-term care, makes the plan sturdier. A health savings account (HSA), if you’re eligible, is a tax-efficient way to save for these costs while you’re still working.

Lowering risk as retirement approaches

A market drop just before or after you retire does the most damage, because you start withdrawing from a smaller balance. Many people gradually shift from stocks toward bonds and cash in the decade before retiring, which is what target-date funds do automatically. Another common approach is to keep one to three years of planned withdrawals in cash or short-term bonds, so a downturn doesn’t force you to sell investments at low prices.

Taxes on retirement income

Withdrawals from traditional 401(k)s and IRAs are taxed as ordinary income, and you must start taking required minimum distributions (RMDs) at age 73, or 75 for people born in 1960 or later. Roth accounts have no RMDs for the original owner, and qualified withdrawals are tax-free. Part of your Social Security benefit can also be taxable, depending on your other income. Holding savings in a mix of account types gives you flexibility to manage your tax bill each year.

Step 4: Check whether you’re on track

Your future savings depend on what you have now, what you add, and time. Money invested in your 30s has decades to compound; the same amount in your 50s has far less. The investment calculator shows how contributions grow, and the compound interest calculator shows why starting early matters so much.

If the retirement calculator shows a shortfall, it also gives the monthly saving that would close it.

Levers if you’re behind

  • Save more, starting with the employer match. It’s an immediate return on your money. The 2026 401(k) limit is $24,500, plus catch-up contributions from age 50.
  • Work longer. Each extra year adds savings and growth and shortens the time savings must last.
  • Delay Social Security. Benefits rise for each year you wait past full retirement age, up to 70.
  • Reduce expected spending. Paying off a mortgage before retiring lowers the income you need.
  • Keep fees low. A 1% difference in fees compounds into a large difference over decades.

Raising contributions is easier than it looks, because traditional 401(k) contributions are pre-tax; the paycheck calculator shows how little a 1% increase changes your take-home pay.

Frequently asked questions

How much money do I need to retire comfortably?

Start with the yearly income you want, subtract what Social Security and any pension will pay, and multiply the gap by roughly 20 to 25 for a typical retirement. A $40,000 yearly gap needs very roughly $800,000 to $1,000,000 in today's dollars, depending on returns and how long you live.

What is the 4% rule?

A rule of thumb from research on historical US market returns: withdrawing 4% of your savings in the first year of retirement, then raising the amount with inflation, has usually lasted about 30 years. Dividing your yearly need by 4% (or multiplying by 25) gives a rough savings target.

How much of my income will I need in retirement?

Many planners suggest 70% to 80% of your pre-retirement income, because work costs, payroll taxes and retirement saving stop. Your number could be higher if you plan to travel or have health costs, or lower if your home is paid off.

How much does Social Security replace?

It depends on your earnings history and claiming age. For many middle-income workers it replaces roughly 40% of pre-retirement earnings, less for higher earners. Check your personal estimate at ssa.gov/myaccount.

What if I'm starting late?

Save as much as you can, use catch-up contributions from age 50 ($8,000 extra in a 401(k) in 2026, $11,250 at ages 60 to 63), consider working a few more years, and think about delaying Social Security, which increases your monthly benefit for each year you wait up to age 70.
