How Much Should I Contribute to My 401(k)?

The order to save in, why the employer match comes first, how 15% of pay is reached, and what different contribution rates grow to, with worked numbers.

Reviewed by Amine Boumazzoughe, bank auditor · Updated November 2, 2026

Short answer: contribute at least enough to get the full employer match, because it’s free money. Then work toward saving 15% of your pay for retirement in total, including the match. If that’s too much right now, start lower and raise your contribution by 1 percentage point every year, or every time you get a raise. Once you’ve built an emergency fund and paid off high-interest debt, consider saving more, up to the $24,500 limit for 2026.

Step 1: get the full employer match

An employer match is the best return available to most savers: an immediate 50% or 100% on the money you put in, before any investment growth. Not taking all of it is like turning down part of your salary.

Matches are usually described as a rate up to a cap. Common formulas:

Match formula You contribute Employer adds Contribute at least
100% up to 3% of pay 3% 3% 3%
100% up to 4% of pay 4% 4% 4%
50% up to 6% of pay 6% 3% 6%
100% of the first 3%, then 50% of the next 2% 5% 4% 5%

Check your plan’s summary plan description or benefits portal for your formula. Also check the vesting schedule, which sets when the employer’s money becomes fully yours.

Step 2: protect yourself first

Before saving more than the match, make sure you have:

  • An emergency fund of at least a few months of expenses, so a surprise doesn’t force a 401(k) withdrawal with taxes and a 10% penalty. The emergency fund calculator suggests a target.
  • No high-interest debt. Paying off a credit card at 22% APR is a guaranteed 22% return, much better than expected stock returns. The debt payoff calculator builds a plan.

Step 3: aim for 15% of pay

The most widely used guideline is to save about 15% of your gross pay for retirement each year, including the employer match, starting in your twenties or early thirties. With a 4% match, that means contributing 11% yourself.

Here’s why. For a 30-year-old earning $60,000 with a match of 100% up to 4%, starting from zero, earning 6.5% a year with 3% annual raises, retiring at 67:

You contribute Employer match Balance at 67 In today’s dollars
2% 2% $536,508 $215,176
4% 4% $1,073,017 $430,352
6% 4% $1,341,271 $537,940
10% 4% $1,877,779 $753,115
15% 4% $2,548,415 $1,022,085

(Today’s dollars assume 2.5% inflation.)

Three things stand out:

  • Going from 2% to 4% doubles the result, because it doubles the match as well.
  • Each extra percentage point after the match adds about $134,000 by retirement in this example.
  • At 15% plus the match, the balance reaches about $1 million in today’s dollars, roughly 17 times the starting salary in purchasing power.

Try your own numbers in the 401(k) calculator.

If 15% feels impossible

Very few people jump straight to 15%. A gradual approach works:

  • Start with the match. That’s the non-negotiable minimum.
  • Increase by 1 point a year. On a $60,000 salary paid every two weeks, each extra 1% is about $23 per paycheck before tax, and only about $20 less take-home pay in the 12% bracket.
  • Raise it with every pay rise. Put half of each raise toward your 401(k) and your take-home pay never falls.
  • Use auto-escalation if your plan offers it: it raises your contribution automatically each year.

Many new hires are now enrolled automatically: under the SECURE 2.0 Act, most new 401(k) plans started after 2022 must automatically enroll employees at 3% to 10% of pay and raise the rate by 1 point a year up to at least 10%. Check what you were enrolled at; the default is often below the match.

The cost of waiting five years

Time does as much work as the contribution rate. Take the same saver contributing 10% with the 4% match, but starting at 35 instead of 30, on the salary they’d have reached by then (about $69,556):

Start at Contribution Balance at 67
30 10% $1,877,779
35 10% $1,461,252
35 14% $1,878,753

Waiting five years costs about $416,500 at the same rate. To catch up, the later starter has to contribute 14% instead of 10% for the rest of their career. Starting with even a small amount now beats waiting for the “right” time.

If your employer doesn’t offer a match

Without a match, the 401(k) loses its biggest advantage, but it still offers tax benefits and a high limit. Consider an IRA first if it offers lower-cost investments, then come back to the 401(k). The 15% target still applies; you just have to provide all of it yourself. If you’re self-employed, a solo 401(k) or SEP IRA lets you save a large share of your income with similar tax benefits.

Common mistakes

  • Contributing below the match, often because auto-enrollment set a low default.
  • Never raising the rate after the first year on the job.
  • Cashing out when changing jobs, which triggers taxes and usually a 10% penalty, and loses decades of growth. Roll the money over instead.
  • Taking 401(k) loans for non-emergencies. The borrowed money stops growing, and leaving the job can make the balance due quickly.
  • Investing too cautiously for decades, for example leaving everything in a money market fund. A low-cost target-date fund is a sensible default.

Benchmarks by age

A popular set of guidelines from Fidelity suggests having saved for retirement:

By age Saved, as a multiple of salary
30 1×
40 3×
50 6×
60 8×
67 10×

These assume saving 15% from age 25. If you’re behind, don’t panic: a higher contribution rate, catch-up contributions after 50 and working a few extra years all help. For a fuller picture including Social Security, use the retirement calculator.

Catching up after 40 or 50

Starting late means you need a higher savings rate, but the tools to do it improve with age:

  • Catch-up contributions: from age 50, you can contribute $8,000 a year above the normal limit in 2026, for $32,500 in total. From 60 to 63, the catch-up is $11,250, for $35,750.
  • Higher income: many people earn more in their 40s and 50s, which makes higher percentages more affordable.
  • Fewer expenses: when children leave home or a mortgage is paid off, redirect those payments to your 401(k).

From 2026, if you earned more than $150,000 in FICA wages from the employer the previous year, catch-up contributions must be Roth (after-tax).

When to max out your 401(k)

The 2026 employee limit is $24,500. That’s 41% of a $60,000 salary but 20% of a $120,000 one, so maxing out is mainly realistic for higher earners. It makes sense when:

  • You have an emergency fund and no high-interest debt.
  • You’ve considered an IRA, which may offer lower-cost investments.
  • You’re in a high tax bracket and want the largest possible traditional deduction.
  • You’re catching up for lost time.

Traditional or Roth?

Most plans let you choose between traditional (pre-tax) and Roth (after-tax) contributions. It doesn’t change how much you should save, but it changes how much each dollar costs you now. A traditional contribution reduces your income tax this year; a Roth contribution doesn’t, but withdrawals in retirement are tax-free. Our guide Roth vs traditional 401(k) explains how to choose.

What your contribution costs per paycheck

Because traditional contributions come out before income tax, your take-home pay falls by less than the amount you contribute. See exactly how much with the paycheck calculator: enter your salary and change the 401(k) percentage. Social Security and Medicare are still charged on the contribution.

How to change your contribution

Most plans let you change your contribution rate at any time through the plan provider’s website or app, or your employer’s HR portal. Changes usually take effect within one or two pay periods. While you’re there:

  • Check your investment choices, not just the percentage. New contributions often go into a default fund.
  • Set up auto-escalation if it’s available, with a cap of 15% or more.
  • Name or update your beneficiaries, which override your will for the 401(k).
  • Look at the fees of the funds you hold; the plan’s fee disclosure lists each fund’s expense ratio.

A simple order of saving

  1. Contribute to your 401(k) up to the full match.
  2. Build an emergency fund and pay off debt above about 7% to 8% interest.
  3. Raise your 401(k) toward 15% of pay including the match, or fund an IRA first if it has better investments.
  4. If you still have room, contribute more to the 401(k), up to the limit.
  5. Then invest in a regular taxable account.

Key terms

  • Elective deferral: the part of your pay you choose to put into your 401(k).
  • Employer match: money your employer adds based on your contributions.
  • Vesting: when employer contributions become fully yours.
  • Catch-up contribution: an extra amount allowed from age 50.
  • Auto-escalation: a plan feature that raises your contribution rate each year.

Frequently asked questions

How much should I contribute to my 401(k)?

At minimum, enough to get your employer's full match. Beyond that, a common target is 15% of your pay for retirement, including the match. If you can't reach that yet, increase your contribution by 1 percentage point a year until you do.

What percentage of my salary should go into my 401(k)?

Many planners suggest 10% to 15% of gross pay, including any employer match. Someone with a 4% match would contribute about 11% to reach 15% in total. People who start late may need 20% or more.

Should I max out my 401(k)?

Only after you've built an emergency fund, paid off high-interest debt and, ideally, considered an IRA. The 2026 limit is $24,500, which is about 41% of a $60,000 salary, so maxing out is realistic mainly for higher earners or those catching up.

Is it better to contribute 6% or 10% to my 401(k)?

10%, if you can afford it. For a 30-year-old earning $60,000 with a 4% match, raising contributions from 6% to 10% adds about $536,500 by age 67 in our example, roughly $215,000 in today's dollars.

How much does a 401(k) contribution reduce my paycheck?

Less than the contribution itself, for a traditional 401(k), because it's taken out before income tax. On a $60,000 salary paid every two weeks, each extra 1% is about $23 per paycheck, but take-home pay falls by only about $20 in the 12% bracket.

Is it too late to start contributing to a 401(k) at 40 or 50?

No. You have 25 or more years of growth at 40 and 15 or more at 50, and from age 50 you can add catch-up contributions: $8,000 extra in 2026, or $11,250 at ages 60 to 63.

Should I contribute to my 401(k) if I have debt?

Contribute enough for the full match even while paying off debt, because the match is an immediate 50% or 100% return. Beyond the match, pay off debt above about 7% to 8% interest, such as credit cards, before increasing contributions.
