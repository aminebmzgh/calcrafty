How Much CO2 Does a Car Produce? Per Gallon, Mile and Year

How much carbon dioxide a car emits per gallon, per mile and per year, using EPA figures, why fuel economy matters so much, and how to cut your driving footprint.

Reviewed by Amine Boumazzoughe · Updated November 3, 2026

Short answer: burning a gallon of gasoline produces about 8,887 grams (19.6 lb) of CO2, and a gallon of diesel about 10,180 grams, according to the EPA. A typical U.S. passenger vehicle, at about 22.2 miles per gallon and 11,500 miles a year, emits about 4.6 metric tons of CO2 a year, or roughly 400 grams per mile. Your own car’s figure depends almost entirely on two numbers: how far you drive and how many miles you get per gallon. The car CO2 emissions calculator works it out for your car.

Per gallon: where the number comes from

Gasoline is a mixture of hydrocarbons, molecules made of carbon and hydrogen. When it burns in an engine, the carbon combines with oxygen from the air to form carbon dioxide, and the hydrogen forms water.

Almost all the carbon in the fuel ends up as CO2, so the CO2 from a gallon depends on how much carbon a gallon contains. The EPA’s figures are:

Fuel CO2 per gallon
Gasoline 8,887 g (about 19.6 lb)
Diesel 10,180 g (about 22.4 lb)

Diesel is denser and contains more carbon per gallon than gasoline, so it produces more CO2 per gallon burned.

Why the CO2 weighs more than the fuel

A gallon of gasoline weighs a little over 6 lb, yet it produces nearly 20 lb of CO2. That isn’t a mistake: most of the mass comes from the air. A carbon atom has a mass of about 12, and each CO2 molecule adds two oxygen atoms of about 16 each, for a total of 44. So every pound of carbon in the fuel becomes about 44 ÷ 12 ≈ 3.7 lb of CO2.

Per mile: why fuel economy matters

To get CO2 per mile, divide the grams per gallon by your fuel economy:

grams of CO2 per mile = 8,887 ÷ mpg (for gasoline)

Fuel economy CO2 per mile
15 mpg 592 g
20 mpg 444 g
22.2 mpg (EPA typical) 400 g
25 mpg 355 g
30 mpg 296 g
40 mpg 222 g
50 mpg 178 g

A large pickup or SUV at 15 mpg emits more than three times as much CO2 per mile as a 50 mpg hybrid. Notice, though, that the savings shrink as mpg rises: moving from 15 to 20 mpg saves 148 grams per mile, while moving from 40 to 50 mpg saves 44. Fuel used is what drives emissions, and gallons per mile fall fastest at the low end of the mpg scale.

Per year: the typical car

The EPA’s figure for a typical passenger vehicle, about 4.6 metric tons a year, comes from multiplying the two:

  • 11,500 miles ÷ 22.2 mpg = about 518 gallons.
  • 518 gallons × 8,887 g = about 4.6 million grams, or 4.6 metric tons.

For a 25 mpg car driven the same distance, it’s 460 gallons and 4.09 metric tons. Here’s how distance changes it for that car:

Miles a year CO2 a year (25 mpg)
5,000 1.78 t
10,000 3.55 t
15,000 5.33 t
20,000 7.11 t

A metric ton is 1,000 kg, about 2,205 lb, so a typical car’s yearly emissions weigh more than many cars do.

Why CO2 from cars matters

Carbon dioxide is a greenhouse gas: it lets sunlight through but absorbs some of the heat the Earth gives off, warming the planet. It also stays in the atmosphere for a very long time, so emissions accumulate. Transportation is one of the largest sources of greenhouse gas emissions in the United States, and passenger cars and light trucks make up a large share of it. Because the CO2 comes directly from fuel, it’s one of the parts of a personal carbon footprint that individual choices affect most directly.

Reading the window sticker

New cars sold in the U.S. carry a Fuel Economy and Environment label from the EPA and the Department of Transportation. Besides the combined, city and highway mpg, it shows the car’s tailpipe CO2 in grams per mile, an estimated annual fuel cost, and ratings for greenhouse gases and smog on a scale of 1 to 10. Comparing the grams-per-mile figure is the most direct way to compare two cars’ CO2. The same information for new and used models is on fueleconomy.gov, where you can also compare vehicles side by side.

What the tailpipe number leaves out

The figures above count carbon dioxide from burning fuel, which is by far the largest part of a car’s direct greenhouse gas emissions. They don’t include:

  • Other tailpipe gases such as methane and nitrous oxide, emitted in much smaller amounts.
  • Air-conditioning refrigerant that can leak over the life of the car.
  • Producing and delivering the fuel, including drilling, refining and transport.
  • Manufacturing the vehicle and its eventual disposal.

A full “life-cycle” footprint includes all of these, and it’s the fairest way to compare very different vehicles, such as a gasoline car and an electric one.

Gasoline, diesel, hybrid and electric

  • Gasoline cars emit 8,887 g per gallon; their per-mile emissions depend on mpg.
  • Diesel cars emit more per gallon (10,180 g) but are often more efficient. At the same 30 mpg, a diesel emits about 339 g per mile and a gasoline car 296 g; a diesel that gets substantially better mileage can come out ahead.
  • Hybrids burn gasoline but recover energy when braking and use an electric motor at low speeds, so their mpg, and therefore their CO2 per mile, is much better, especially in city driving.
  • Plug-in hybrids run on electricity for shorter trips and gasoline for longer ones. Their tailpipe emissions depend on how often they’re charged.
  • Electric cars have no tailpipe. Their emissions come from generating electricity and making the battery. On a grid powered largely by low-carbon sources, they typically have much lower life-cycle emissions; on a grid dominated by coal, the advantage is smaller.

Putting your driving in perspective

A few everyday examples, all for gasoline cars:

  • A daily commute of 20 miles round trip, 250 days a year, is 5,000 miles. In a 25 mpg car, that’s about 1.78 metric tons of CO2 a year.
  • A 1,000-mile road trip in a 30 mpg car uses about 33.3 gallons and emits about 296 kg of CO2.
  • Switching from a 20 mpg SUV to a 35 mpg sedan for 12,000 miles a year cuts emissions from about 5.33 to 3.05 metric tons, a saving of 2.28 tons a year, along with roughly 257 fewer gallons of fuel.

How to reduce your car’s CO2

Because CO2 is simply proportional to the fuel you burn, every gallon saved is 8,887 grams of CO2 avoided:

  • Drive less. Combine errands into one trip, carpool, and walk, cycle or take transit when you can. Working from home even one day a week removes a fifth of commuting emissions.
  • Drive smoothly. Hard acceleration and braking waste fuel. Anticipate traffic and coast to stops.
  • Watch your speed. Most cars’ fuel economy drops noticeably at high highway speeds.
  • Don’t idle. An idling engine gets zero miles per gallon, so switch off when parked or waiting.
  • Look after the car. Correct tire pressure, regular maintenance and a clean air filter (on older cars) all help.
  • Travel light. Remove roof boxes and racks when not in use, and avoid carrying unnecessary weight in the trunk.
  • Choose efficiency. When you replace your car, compare models’ CO2 per mile and fuel costs, not just the price.

Fuel economy and money

Cutting CO2 and cutting fuel bills are the same thing for a gasoline car. At $3.20 a gallon, a 25 mpg car driven 11,500 miles spends about $1,472 a year on fuel. A more efficient car can save hundreds of dollars a year, which is worth including when you compare costs with the car affordability calculator or a loan with the auto loan calculator.

How to calculate your own figure

  1. Find your annual miles from your odometer or service records.
  2. Find your real mpg by dividing miles driven between fill-ups by the gallons added.
  3. Divide miles by mpg to get gallons per year.
  4. Multiply by 8,887 g for gasoline or 10,180 g for diesel.
  5. Divide by 1,000,000 to get metric tons.

Or enter your miles and mpg in the car CO2 emissions calculator, which also compares your car with the EPA’s typical vehicle.

Key terms

  • Carbon dioxide (CO2): the main greenhouse gas released by burning fossil fuels.
  • Emission factor: the CO2 produced per unit of fuel, such as grams per gallon.
  • Metric ton: 1,000 kilograms, about 2,205 pounds.
  • Life-cycle emissions: all emissions from making, fueling, running and disposing of a vehicle.
  • Fuel economy: how far a vehicle travels on a given amount of fuel, in miles per gallon.
  • Greenhouse gas: a gas, such as carbon dioxide or methane, that traps heat in the atmosphere.
  • Hybrid: a vehicle that combines a gasoline engine with an electric motor and battery to use less fuel.
  • Carbon footprint: the total greenhouse gas emissions caused by a person, household, activity or product.

Frequently asked questions

How much CO2 does a car produce per year?

The EPA estimates that a typical passenger vehicle emits about 4.6 metric tons of CO2 a year, based on about 22.2 miles per gallon and 11,500 miles of driving.

How much CO2 does a car produce per mile?

About 400 grams for the EPA's typical 22.2 mpg vehicle. Divide 8,887 grams by your car's mpg: a 30 mpg car emits about 296 grams per mile, and a 50 mpg hybrid about 178 grams.

How much CO2 is in a gallon of gas?

Burning one gallon of gasoline produces about 8,887 grams of CO2, roughly 19.6 lb. A gallon of diesel produces about 10,180 grams.

Why does a gallon of gas produce more CO2 than it weighs?

Because most of the CO2's mass comes from oxygen in the air. Each carbon atom in the fuel combines with two oxygen atoms, so a pound of carbon becomes about 3.7 pounds of CO2.

Do electric cars produce CO2?

Not from a tailpipe. Their emissions come from generating the electricity they use and from manufacturing, especially the battery. How much depends on how your local electricity is produced.

What is the easiest way to reduce my car's CO2?

Drive fewer miles and use less fuel per mile: combine trips, drive smoothly, avoid long idling, keep tires inflated, and choose an efficient car when you replace it.
