How to Calculate a Percentage: Formulas and Examples

The three percentage formulas you'll ever need, with worked examples for discounts, tips, grades and percent change.

Reviewed by Amine Boumazzoughe · Updated November 2, 2026

Short answer: a percentage is a number out of 100. Almost every percentage problem uses one relationship: part = whole × percent ÷ 100. Rearrange it to find the part, the percent or the whole. The percentage calculator handles every version and shows the working.

The three formulas

You want Formula Example
X% of a number number × X ÷ 100 15% of 80 = 80 × 15 ÷ 100 = 12
What percent A is of B A ÷ B × 100 30 of 120 = 30 ÷ 120 × 100 = 25%
Percent change (new − old) ÷ old × 100 50 → 65 = 15 ÷ 50 × 100 = 30%

Everything else is a variation. A discount is “X% of the price,” subtracted from the price. A tip is “X% of the bill,” added on. A test score is “what percent your points are of the total.”

Worked examples

Sale price. A $90 pair of shoes is 25% off. The discount is 90 × 25 ÷ 100 = $22.50, so you pay $67.50. Or in one step: 90 × 0.75 = $67.50.

Restaurant tip. A $56 bill with a 20% tip: 10% is $5.60, so 20% is $11.20, and the total is $67.20.

Test score. 43 correct out of 50 is 43 ÷ 50 × 100 = 86%.

Price increase. A monthly subscription goes from $12 to $15: (15 − 12) ÷ 12 × 100 = a 25% increase.

Finding the original price. A jacket costs $68 after 15% off. The sale price is 85% of the original, so the original was 68 ÷ 0.85 = $80.

Sales tax. A $250 purchase with 8% sales tax costs 250 × 1.08 = $270.

Mental math shortcuts

  • 10%: move the decimal point one place left. 10% of 340 = 34.
  • 1%: move it two places. 1% of 340 = 3.4.
  • 5%: half of 10%. 20%: double 10%. 25%: divide by 4. 50%: divide by 2.
  • 15%: 10% plus half of 10%. Handy for tips.
  • The swap trick: X% of Y equals Y% of X. So 4% of 75 is the same as 75% of 4, which is 3.

Percentages, decimals and fractions

They’re three ways of writing the same thing:

  • Percent to decimal: divide by 100. 37.5% = 0.375.
  • Decimal to percent: multiply by 100. 0.06 = 6%.
  • Fraction to percent: divide, then multiply by 100. 3/8 = 37.5%.
  • Percent to fraction: put it over 100 and simplify. 35% = 35/100 = 7/20.

Common mistakes

Using the wrong base. “20% more than” and “20% less than” use different starting values. If A is 120 and B is 100, A is 20% more than B, but B is 16.7% less than A.

Adding successive percentages. A 20% discount plus an extra 10% off is 28% off in total, not 30%, because the second discount applies to the reduced price: 0.80 × 0.90 = 0.72.

Percent vs. percentage points. An interest rate rising from 4% to 5% has gone up 1 percentage point, which is a 25% increase in the rate itself.

Reversing a change. A 25% drop needs a 33.3% rise to get back to the start, not 25%.

Stacked discounts, coupons and sales tax

When several percentages and fixed amounts apply to one price, the order matters.

Percent off, then a coupon. A $100 jacket is 20% off, and you have a $10 coupon. If the store takes 20% off first, you pay $100 × 0.80 − $10 = $70. If it applies the $10 coupon first, you pay ($100 − $10) × 0.80 = $72. Taking the percentage off the larger amount gives the bigger saving, so percentage discounts first, fixed coupons second, is better for you.

Discount, then tax. Sales tax is charged on the price after discounts. A $250 item at 15% off with 8% sales tax costs $250 × 0.85 × 1.08 = $229.50. Because multiplication can be done in any order, it doesn’t matter whether you apply the discount or the tax first; you get the same answer either way.

“Extra 30% off the sale price.” On an item already marked down to $49.99, an extra 30% off gives $49.99 × 0.70 = $34.99, rounded to the cent.

Average growth rates over several years

Percentages that compound shouldn’t be averaged with simple arithmetic. If an investment rises 10% one year and falls 10% the next, the average of the two rates is 0%, but $100 becomes $110 and then $99, a 1% loss overall.

To find the true average yearly rate over several years, use the compound annual growth rate (CAGR):

CAGR = (ending value ÷ starting value)^(1 ÷ years) − 1

For example, $10,000 growing to $16,105 over 5 years is (16,105 ÷ 10,000)^(1/5) − 1 = 10% a year. The scientific calculator handles the fractional power, and the compound interest calculator runs growth forward when you know the rate.

Percentage points and basis points

When a percentage itself changes, use percentage points for the plain difference. A mortgage rate going from 6.5% to 7% has risen by 0.5 percentage points, which is a 7.7% increase in the rate.

Finance uses an even smaller unit, the basis point: one hundredth of a percentage point. A quarter-point change in interest rates is 25 basis points, and a fund fee of 0.15% is 15 basis points. Basis points avoid the confusion of saying “a 0.25% increase” when you mean 0.25 percentage points.

Weighted averages of percentages

When parts of a total count for different amounts, multiply each percentage by its weight and add them up. A course grade where tests count for 40% and homework for 60%, with scores of 80% on tests and 90% on homework, is:

0.40 × 80 + 0.60 × 90 = 32 + 54 = 86%

A plain average of 80 and 90 would give 85%, understating the result because homework carries more weight. The same method works for portfolio returns, survey results and grade point averages, where credits act as the weights.

Percent change vs percent difference

Percent change compares a new value with an old one, so it has a direction and uses the old value as the base. Percent difference compares two values where neither is the starting point, such as two stores’ prices or two lab measurements. It divides the gap by the average of the two:

Percent difference = |A − B| ÷ ((A + B) ÷ 2) × 100

Two stores sell the same item for $40 and $50. The percent difference is $10 ÷ $45 × 100 = 22.2%. By contrast, going from $40 to $50 is a 25% increase, and from $50 to $40 is a 20% decrease. Use percent change when there’s a before and after, and percent difference when there isn’t.

Rounding percentages

When you round each part of a whole, the percentages may not add up to exactly 100%. Three equal parts are each 33.3%, which totals 99.9%. Survey results and pie charts often show 99% or 101% for this reason, and it’s not a mistake. If the parts must total 100%, round all but one and give the last part whatever is left, or show one more decimal place.

Round only at the end. Rounding a percentage early and then using it in another calculation, such as applying 33% instead of 1/3 to a large amount, can noticeably change the result.

Percentages in everyday money

Percentages decide the cost of almost every financial product, and small differences grow large:

  • Interest rates. A credit card at 24% APR charges about 2% a month on any balance you carry. A savings account at 4% earns about a third of a percent a month. Compare loans by APR with the APR calculator.
  • Raises and inflation. A 3% raise in a year when prices rose 4% means your pay buys less than before; the pay raise calculator shows the real raise.
  • Fees. A 1% annual investment fee is $2,000 a year on a $200,000 balance, every year.
  • Debt-to-income ratio. Lenders express your monthly debts as a percentage of your gross income.

Whenever two options are described in percentages, convert them into dollars for your own situation. The difference becomes much easier to judge.

Practice problems

Try these, then check with the calculator:

  1. What is 35% of 240? 84
  2. 18 is what percent of 72? 25%
  3. A price rises from $40 to $46. What’s the percent increase? 15%
  4. A shirt costs $34 after 15% off. What was the original price? $40
  5. A population falls from 5,000 to 4,600. What’s the percent decrease? 8%
  6. After a 25% raise, a salary is $55,000. What was it before? $44,000
  7. What’s 12.5% as a fraction? 1/8
  8. A $60 meal with an 18% tip and 7% tax on the meal: what’s the total? $75.00 ($10.80 tip + $4.20 tax)
  9. What is 0.5% of $80,000? $400
  10. A $1,200 TV is marked down 15%, then an extra 10% off the sale price. What do you pay? $918, a 23.5% total discount

Where you’ll use percentages

Percentages run through everyday decisions: discounts and sales tax, tips, interest rates and loan costs, pay raises, grades and test scores, nutrition labels and statistics in the news. Frequently asked questions

What is the formula for percentage?

Percentage = (part ÷ whole) × 100. Rearranged, part = whole × percentage ÷ 100. For example, 18 out of 24 is 18 ÷ 24 × 100 = 75%, and 75% of 24 is 24 × 75 ÷ 100 = 18.

How do I calculate percentage increase?

Subtract the old value from the new value, divide by the old value and multiply by 100. A price that rises from $40 to $46 has increased by (46 − 40) ÷ 40 × 100 = 15%.

How do I calculate a percentage of a total in my head?

Find 10% by moving the decimal point one place left, then scale. 10% of $64 is $6.40, so 5% is $3.20, 20% is $12.80 and 15% is $6.40 + $3.20 = $9.60.

How do I convert a fraction to a percentage?

Divide the numerator by the denominator and multiply by 100. 3/8 = 3 ÷ 8 = 0.375 = 37.5%. To go from a percentage to a fraction, put it over 100 and simplify: 35% = 35/100 = 7/20.

Why is a 50% loss not undone by a 50% gain?

Because each percentage applies to a different base. $100 down 50% is $50; $50 up 50% is only $75. To recover a 50% loss you need a 100% gain, which is why losses hurt investments more than equal-sized gains help.
