What this means

You have $200,000 of equity, and a lender allowing 85% combined loan-to-value could offer a line of up to $132,500.

Drawing $50,000 costs $354.17 a month while you pay interest only. When the 10 years draw period ends, the payment rises to $433.91 to repay the balance over 20 years, and the total interest comes to $96,640.

HELOC rates are usually variable, so payments rise if rates do. Paying down principal during the draw period softens the jump later.

Show the math

  1. 1. Credit limit

    home value × max CLTV − mortgage balance

    $450,000 × 85% − $250,000 = $132,500

  2. 2. Interest-only payment

    balance × rate ÷ 12

    $50,000 × 8.5% ÷ 12 = $354.17

  3. 3. Repayment payment

    M = P × r(1 + r)ⁿ ÷ ((1 + r)ⁿ − 1), r = rate ÷ 12

    $50,000 over 240 months at 8.5% = $433.91

  4. 4. Total interest

    interest-only payments + interest during repayment

    $42,500.40 + $54,139.23 = $96,639.63

How to use this HELOC calculator

Enter your home’s value, your mortgage balance and the lender’s limit to see how much you can borrow. Then enter the amount you plan to use to see the payments in both phases of the line.

  1. Home value. A realistic current value; the lender will order an appraisal or automated valuation.
  2. Mortgage balance. What you still owe on your first mortgage, plus any other loans secured by the home.
  3. Maximum CLTV. The share of your home’s value the lender lets you borrow against in total. 80% to 85% is common; some lenders go to 90% for strong borrowers.
  4. Amount you’ll draw and rate. HELOC rates are usually the prime rate plus a margin, and they change when prime changes.
  5. Draw and repayment periods. A 10-year draw followed by 20 years of repayment is the most common structure.

Worked example: a $60,000 kitchen renovation

A homeowner with a $520,000 home and a $310,000 mortgage opens a HELOC at 8.75% to pay for a $60,000 renovation. The lender allows 85% CLTV, a 10-year draw period and 20 years of repayment.

  • Equity: $520,000 − $310,000 = $210,000
  • Credit limit: $520,000 × 85% − $310,000 = $132,000
  • CLTV after drawing $60,000: 71.2%
  • Interest-only payment for 10 years: $60,000 × 8.75% ÷ 12 = $437.50 a month
  • Payment once repayment starts: $530.23 a month for 20 years, a jump of $92.73
  • Total interest over 30 years: $119,752.74, about twice the amount borrowed

The total interest is so large because, while paying interest only, the homeowner owes the full $60,000 for a decade. Paying principal along the way changes the picture completely: repaying the same $60,000 over 10 years from day one costs $751.96 a month but only $30,235.38 in interest.

The HELOC formulas in plain English

  • Credit limit = home value × maximum CLTV − all existing loans on the home.
  • Interest-only payment = balance × annual rate ÷ 12. It covers that month’s interest and nothing else, so the balance doesn’t fall.
  • Repayment payment uses the standard loan formula, M = P × r(1 + r)ⁿ ÷ ((1 + r)ⁿ − 1), on the balance at the end of the draw period.
  • Combined loan-to-value (CLTV) = (mortgage + HELOC balance) ÷ home value.

The calculator assumes you draw the full amount at the start and pay interest only during the draw period, which shows the most common worst case. The Show the math panel shows each step with your numbers.

What a rising rate does to the payments

Most HELOCs have a variable rate tied to the prime rate, which moves with the Federal Reserve’s policy rate. For the $60,000 example:

HELOC rate Interest-only payment Repayment payment Total interest
7.50% $375.00 $483.36 $101,004.04
8.75% $437.50 $530.23 $119,752.74
10.75% $537.50 $609.14 $150,691.35
12.75% $637.50 $692.29 $182,646.25

Two percentage points of rate increase add $100 a month during the draw period and about $79 a month in repayment. Check your agreement for a lifetime rate cap, and make sure the payment at a rate two or three points higher would still fit your budget.

How the CLTV limit changes what you can borrow

For the same $520,000 home and $310,000 mortgage:

Maximum CLTV Credit limit
80% $106,000
85% $132,000
90% $158,000

Each 5 points of CLTV is worth $26,000 of borrowing capacity on this home. Lenders set the limit based on your credit score, debt-to-income ratio and the property type; investment properties and second homes usually get lower limits.

Feature HELOC Home equity loan Cash-out refinance
How you get the money Draw as needed, up to the limit Lump sum Lump sum, by replacing your mortgage
Rate Usually variable Usually fixed Fixed or adjustable
Payments Interest-only during draw, then principal and interest Fixed principal and interest One new mortgage payment
Effect on your first mortgage None None Replaced, along with its rate

For the $60,000 example, a 15-year home equity loan at a fixed 8.25% would cost $582.08 a month and $44,775.93 in total interest, with no payment shock and no rate risk. If your first mortgage has a low rate, a HELOC or home equity loan avoids giving it up; refinancing the whole balance to borrow a small amount rarely makes sense.

When a HELOC makes sense

A HELOC works best for costs that are large, spread out over time and likely to add value or save money:

  • Home renovations, especially projects paid in stages, where you draw only as each bill comes due. Interest may also be tax-deductible when the money improves the home.
  • Replacing much higher-rate debt, as long as you close or stop using the paid-off cards and have a plan to clear the HELOC balance within a few years.
  • Large planned expenses such as tuition, where you need funds over several semesters.
  • A backup source of funds for a genuine emergency, alongside, not instead of, a cash emergency fund.

It’s usually a poor fit for everyday spending, vacations, cars that lose value faster than you repay them, or anything you couldn’t afford to repay if the rate rose a few points.

Paying a HELOC down faster

Interest-only payments keep the cost low at first but leave the whole balance outstanding. A few habits cut the total interest dramatically:

  • Pay principal from the start. Treat the line like a loan with a fixed payoff date. Repaying $60,000 over 10 years instead of 30 cuts the interest in the example from about $119,750 to about $30,235.
  • Make lump-sum payments from bonuses or tax refunds; most HELOCs let you repay any amount at any time without penalty.
  • Lock part of the balance. Some lenders let you convert a portion of the balance to a fixed rate, which protects against rising rates.
  • Draw only what you need. You pay interest only on the amount you’ve used, not on the full credit limit.

Applying for a HELOC

Lenders look at much the same things as for a mortgage: your credit score, your debt-to-income ratio including the new payment, your income documentation and the home’s value. Most require an appraisal or automated valuation. Expect the whole process to take a few weeks from application to opening. By law, you have three business days after opening a HELOC on your primary home to cancel it without penalty.

Fees and closing costs

HELOC costs vary widely between lenders. Some charge almost nothing to open a line; others charge appraisal, origination and title fees similar to a small mortgage. Watch for annual fees, inactivity fees, minimum draw requirements and early-closure fees if you close the line within the first few years. Ask for all fees in writing and compare offers on their total cost over the years you expect to use the line, not just the rate.

Risks to understand before you borrow

  • Your home is the collateral. Missing payments can lead to foreclosure, unlike with credit card debt.
  • Variable rates. Payments can rise with interest rates, sometimes sharply.
  • Payment shock. The jump when the draw period ends catches many borrowers off guard, especially if they’ve only paid interest.
  • Frozen lines. Lenders can reduce or freeze a line if home values fall or your credit weakens.
  • Fees. Some HELOCs charge annual fees, early-closure fees or minimum draw requirements; read the disclosure.
  • Converting short-term debt into long-term debt. Paying off credit cards with a HELOC lowers the rate, but if the cards fill back up, you end up with both debts.

Key terms

  • Draw period: the years during which you can borrow from the line, often with interest-only payments.
  • Repayment period: the years after the draw period when the balance must be repaid with principal and interest.
  • CLTV (combined loan-to-value): all loans on the home divided by its value.
  • Prime rate: the benchmark rate banks base many variable loans on.
  • Margin: the fixed amount a lender adds to the prime rate to set your HELOC rate.

What to do with your result

Before applying, check how the new payment would affect your debt-to-income ratio in the debt-to-income ratio calculator, since lenders will. If you’re considering replacing your whole mortgage instead, compare with the refinance calculator.

If you’re using home equity to pay off higher-rate debt, plan the payoff in the debt payoff calculator, and compare a fixed-rate alternative in the loan calculator. The APR calculator shows how any closing costs change the true cost.

Finally, remember that home equity is often a household’s largest financial cushion. Borrowing against it turns that cushion into debt, so keep a healthy margin below the lender’s limit. Leaving at least 20% of your home’s value untouched protects you if prices fall, makes it easier to sell or refinance later, and keeps the line available for a real emergency when you need it most.

Frequently asked questions

How much can I borrow with a HELOC?

Lenders usually cap all loans on the home at 80% to 90% of its value. Multiply your home's value by that percentage and subtract your mortgage balance. On a $450,000 home with a $250,000 mortgage and an 85% limit, that's $450,000 × 0.85 − $250,000 = $132,500.

What is the payment on a $50,000 HELOC?

During the draw period, many HELOCs require interest only: $50,000 × 8.5% ÷ 12 = $354.17 a month. Once repayment starts, the balance is repaid with interest, typically over 20 years; at the same rate that's $433.91 a month.

What is HELOC payment shock?

It's the jump in your payment when the draw period ends and you must start repaying principal. Going from interest-only to a 20-year repayment schedule raises the payment, and if rates have risen since you opened the line, the jump is bigger. Paying principal during the draw period reduces it.

Is HELOC interest tax-deductible?

Only in some cases. Under current rules, interest on home equity debt is deductible only if the money is used to buy, build or substantially improve the home that secures it, and only if you itemize deductions. Interest on money used for other purposes, such as paying off credit cards, isn't deductible.

Is a HELOC or a home equity loan better?

A HELOC is flexible: you borrow as needed and pay interest only on what you use, but the rate is usually variable. A home equity loan gives a lump sum at a fixed rate with fixed payments. HELOCs suit ongoing or uncertain costs like renovations; home equity loans suit a one-time expense where a predictable payment matters.

What happens to a HELOC if home values fall?

The lender can freeze or reduce your line if your home's value drops significantly or your finances change. That's why it's risky to count on an unused HELOC as your only emergency fund.
