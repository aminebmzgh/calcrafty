What this means

On $8,333 a month before tax, lenders’ 28%/36% rules allow a housing payment up to $2,333.33, which buys a home of about $313,114 with $50,000 down.

The housing ratio is the limit, not your other debts; you have $166.67 a month of room under the total-debt ratio.

With 16% down, the payment includes $131.56 a month of PMI.

This is the most a lender might approve, not a budget. Make sure the payment leaves room for savings and everyday costs.

How to use this mortgage affordability calculator

Enter your income, your monthly debts and your down payment, and the calculator finds the highest home price whose full monthly payment fits within lender debt-to-income limits. The answer updates as you type.

  1. Household income. Gross yearly income before taxes for everyone who’ll be on the loan.
  2. Monthly debt payments. Minimums on car loans, student loans, credit cards and other debts.
  3. Down payment. Cash you’ll put toward the purchase, not counting closing costs.
  4. Rate and term. The rate starts at this week’s Freddie Mac average for a 30-year fixed loan.
  5. Property tax and insurance. These are part of the payment lenders count, so include realistic amounts for the area you’re shopping in.

More options lets you add HOA dues and PMI and change the 28% and 36% limits to match your lender or loan program.

Worked example: a $95,000 household income

A buyer earns $95,000 a year, has a $450 car payment and has saved $40,000 for a down payment. They expect a 7% rate on a 30-year loan, 1% property tax and $1,800 a year for insurance, with 0.6% PMI.

  • Monthly income: $95,000 ÷ 12 = $7,916.67
  • Housing limit (28%): $2,216.67; total-debt limit (36% − $450): $2,400.00
  • Maximum home price: $294,602, a $254,602 loan with 13.6% down
  • Payment at that price: $1,693.87 principal and interest + $245.50 tax + $150.00 insurance + $127.30 PMI = $2,216.67

The 28% housing ratio sets the limit here, and the buyer has $183.33 a month of room under the 36% ratio. If their debts were $1,100 a month instead, the 36% ratio would bind and the maximum price would fall to $236,168. If they found a 6% rate instead of 7%, it would rise to $317,443.

How affordability is calculated in plain English

Lenders compare your monthly obligations with your gross monthly income. The calculator works backwards from those limits to a price.

  1. Monthly income = annual income ÷ 12.
  2. Front-end limit = income × 28%. Back-end limit = income × 36% − other debts.
  3. Your housing budget is the lower of the two.
  4. Maximum price is the highest price where principal and interest, property tax, insurance, PMI (if under 20% down) and HOA add up to no more than that budget. Because the payment rises with price, the calculator narrows in on it to the dollar.

The principal and interest part uses the standard mortgage formula, M = P × r(1 + r)ⁿ ÷ ((1 + r)ⁿ − 1). The Show the math panel walks through each limit with your numbers.

How much house can you afford by income?

Using the same assumptions as the example ($450 a month in other debts, $40,000 down, 7% for 30 years, 1% property tax, $1,800 a year for insurance, 0.6% PMI and the 28/36 limits):

Household income Maximum home price Monthly payment Limit that applies
$50,000 $155,766 $1,050.00 36% total debt
$75,000 $236,168 $1,750.00 28% housing
$100,000 $309,209 $2,333.32 28% housing
$125,000 $382,251 $2,916.67 28% housing
$150,000 $455,291 $3,500.00 28% housing
$200,000 $601,373 $4,666.66 28% housing

At $50,000, the $450 car payment uses up so much of the 36% allowance that it, not the 28% housing rule, sets the limit. The lower your income, the more each existing debt payment eats into what you can borrow. A popular shortcut says you can afford about three to four times your income; with these assumptions it comes out nearer three, and higher rates or taxes push it lower.

How interest rates change your buying power

Because the payment limit is fixed by your income, a higher rate means a smaller loan for the same payment. With the example’s $95,000 income and the same $2,216.67 monthly budget:

Each half-point move in rates changes the price this buyer can afford by about $10,000 to $13,000. That’s why a rate lock matters once you’re under contract, and why improving your credit score before applying can be worth as much as a larger down payment.

Every extra dollar of down payment raises the price you can afford by more than a dollar once you reach 20% down, because PMI disappears from the payment:

FHA and other loan programs

The 28/36 limits reflect a conventional loan. Government-backed programs allow higher ratios, which raises the maximum price on the same income:

  • FHA loans use 31% for housing and 43% for all debts as their standard guidelines, and allow down payments as low as 3.5% with a credit score of 580 or more. With the example’s $95,000 income, $450 car payment and $40,000 down, setting the limits to 31% and 43% under More options raises the maximum price from $294,602 to $324,340, with a payment of $2,454.17. FHA charges an upfront mortgage insurance premium and an annual premium instead of PMI, so enter their combined cost in the PMI field for a closer estimate.
  • VA loans for eligible service members and veterans need no down payment and no monthly mortgage insurance, and use a 41% total-debt guideline alongside a residual income test.
  • USDA loans for eligible rural areas use 29% and 41%, with no down payment required.

Higher limits mean a larger loan, not a cheaper home, so check the payment against your real budget before stretching to them.

Cash you’ll need besides the down payment

The down payment is the biggest check you’ll write, but not the only one. Before you set a price, make sure you can also cover:

  • Closing costs: typically about 2% to 5% of the loan amount for lender fees, title insurance, the appraisal and prepaid taxes and insurance.
  • An emergency reserve: many lenders like to see two months or more of payments left in savings after closing, and homeowners face repairs renters never do.
  • Inspection and moving costs: a home inspection, movers or a truck, and utility deposits.
  • First-year furnishing and repairs: appliances, window coverings, and anything the inspection turns up.

If the down payment would drain your savings completely, a slightly lower price that leaves a cushion is often the safer choice.

Key terms

  • Debt-to-income ratio (DTI): monthly debt payments divided by gross monthly income.
  • Front-end ratio: housing payment ÷ gross income.
  • Back-end ratio: housing payment plus all other debts ÷ gross income.
  • PITI: principal, interest, taxes and insurance, the core of a mortgage payment.
  • Pre-approval: a lender’s conditional commitment, based on verified income and credit, to lend up to a certain amount.

Lender limits vs a comfortable budget

The 28/36 rule is a starting point, not a law. Many loan programs allow higher ratios. With the same $95,000 income, $450 car payment and $40,000 down, allowing 36% for housing and 43% for all debts raises the maximum price from $294,602 to $373,903, with a monthly payment of $2,850.

That’s about $633 more a month than the 28% budget. Whether it’s affordable depends on things lenders don’t fully see:

  • Taxes and take-home pay. Ratios use gross income, but bills are paid from take-home pay after taxes, retirement contributions and health insurance.
  • Childcare, commuting and other fixed costs. Two households with the same income and debts can have very different room in their budgets.
  • Maintenance and repairs. Planning for around 1% of the home’s value each year is a common rule of thumb; older homes often need more.
  • Savings goals. A payment that stops you saving for retirement or an emergency fund is a risk even if it fits the lender’s ratio.
  • Local property taxes. They vary widely. In the example, a 2% property tax rate instead of 1% cuts the maximum price from $294,602 to $266,766.

A practical approach is to work out the payment you’re comfortable with from your actual monthly budget, then use the More options ratios to see what price that implies. If the comfortable number is below the lender’s maximum, shop to the comfortable number.

Before you start house hunting, get pre-approved with a lender. Pre-approval verifies your income, assets and credit, gives you a firm price range and shows sellers you’re a serious buyer.

Frequently asked questions

How much house can I afford on my salary?

Under the traditional 28/36 rule, your total housing payment can be up to 28% of gross monthly income, and all debt payments up to 36%. On a $95,000 salary with $450 a month in other debts and $40,000 down at 7%, that's a housing payment of about $2,217 and a home price of about $294,600. Lower rates, bigger down payments and fewer debts all raise the number.

What is the 28/36 rule?

It's a long-standing lending guideline. The front-end ratio says housing costs (principal, interest, taxes, insurance, PMI and HOA) shouldn't exceed 28% of gross income. The back-end ratio says housing plus all other monthly debt payments shouldn't exceed 36%. Many loan programs allow higher ratios, sometimes up to 43% to 50% back-end, but a higher ratio also means a tighter budget.

What counts as debt for the debt-to-income ratio?

Minimum payments on things that show on your credit report or are court-ordered, such as car loans, student loans, credit card minimums, personal loans and child support. Rent you'll stop paying, utilities, groceries, insurance and phone bills don't count, even though they matter for your real budget.

Does paying off a car loan increase how much house I can afford?

It can, when the back-end ratio is the limit. In our example, monthly debts of $1,100 instead of $450 make the 36% total-debt ratio bind, and the maximum price drops from about $294,600 to about $236,200. Paying off or refinancing a debt before applying can lift your budget.

How much does the interest rate change what I can afford?

A lot. In our example, a rate of 6% instead of 7% raises the maximum price from about $294,600 to about $317,400 for the same monthly payment. That's why it pays to improve your credit score and shop several lenders before you start looking at homes.

Should I buy the most expensive home a lender approves?

Not necessarily. The lender's limit is based on gross income and ignores taxes, childcare, savings goals and other real costs. Many buyers aim below the maximum so the payment leaves room for an emergency fund, retirement savings and home repairs, which typically run about 1% of the home's value a year.
