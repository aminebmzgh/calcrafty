Buying comes out ahead after 10 years by$1,510

Over 10 years, buying a $375,000 home with 20% down instead of renting at $2,100 a month leaves you $1,510 better off, breaking even in year 10.

What this means

Buying costs $2,813 in the first month (mortgage, tax, insurance and upkeep) versus $2,115 to rent, and needs $86,250 upfront.

After 10 years, selling the home (worth about $528,975) and repaying the $259,107 loan leaves the buyer with $238,129; the renter, who invested the upfront cash and the monthly savings, has $236,619.

Buying pulls ahead from year 10: the longer you stay, the more the buying and selling costs are spread out.

Show the math

  1. 1. Cash to buy

    down payment + closing costs

    $375,000 × (20% + 3%) = $86,250

  2. 2. Each month

    buyer pays mortgage + tax + insurance + maintenance + HOA; renter pays rent + insurance; the cheaper side invests the difference

    month 1: $2,813 vs $2,115

  3. 3. Buyer’s net worth

    home value × (1 − selling costs) − loan balance + buyer’s investments

    $528,975 × (1 − 6%) − $259,107 + investments = $238,129

  4. 4. Renter’s net worth

    invested upfront cash + invested monthly savings, grown at the investment return

    $86,250 invested at 6% plus monthly differences = $236,619

How to use this rent vs buy calculator

Enter how long you’ll stay, the home you’d buy and the rent for a similar place. The calculator works out how much you’d be worth at the end under each choice, the first year buying pulls ahead, and how monthly costs compare.

  1. How long you’ll stay. The single biggest factor, because buying and selling costs are spread over these years.
  2. Home price, down payment and rate. The rate starts at this week’s Freddie Mac average for a 30-year fixed mortgage.
  3. Rent and rent growth. Use the rent for a genuinely comparable home, not your current place if it’s smaller.
  4. Price growth and investment return. How fast the home’s value rises, and what the renter’s invested money would earn.
  5. More options. Property tax, insurance, maintenance (about 1% of value a year is a common rule of thumb), HOA dues and closing costs on both ends.

Worked example: a $400,000 home or $2,200 a month in rent

A household compares buying a $400,000 home with 20% down at 7% against renting a similar home for $2,200 a month. They assume 1.1% property tax, $1,800 a year of insurance, 1% maintenance, 3% closing costs to buy, 6% to sell, 3.5% yearly growth in both home prices and rent, and a 6% return on investments.

  • Cash needed to buy: $92,000 ($80,000 down + $12,000 closing costs)
  • First month: $2,978.97 to own versus $2,215.00 to rent
  • After 10 years: the home is worth about $564,240 and the loan balance is $274,599. Net worth is $255,785.87 if they buy and $257,629.63 if they rent and invest the difference.

So after 10 years renting is ahead by just $1,843.75, essentially a tie. Time changes everything:

Stay Result
5 years Renting ahead by $25,450
10 years Renting ahead by $1,844
15 years Buying ahead by $41,358 (break-even in year 11)

The assumptions matter too. If comparable rent were $1,800 instead of $2,200, renting would be ahead by $76,838 after 10 years. If home prices grew 2% a year instead of 3.5%, renting would be ahead by $63,154.

How the comparison works in plain English

The calculator follows two households month by month.

  • The buyer pays the down payment and closing costs upfront, then each month pays the mortgage, property tax, insurance, maintenance and HOA. At the end, they sell the home, pay selling costs and repay the remaining loan; what’s left is their home equity.
  • The renter invests the cash the buyer spent upfront, then each month pays rent and renters insurance.
  • Every month, whichever household spent less invests the difference at the investment return.

Net worth at the end is equity plus investments for the buyer, and investments for the renter. Taxes, the mortgage interest deduction and capital gains are left out, so treat small differences as a tie.

Key terms

  • Opportunity cost: what the down payment could have earned if invested instead.
  • Closing costs: fees to buy (lender, title, appraisal) and sell (agent commissions, transfer taxes).
  • Home equity: the home’s value minus what you owe on it.
  • Break-even year: the first year in which buying leaves you with more than renting.
  • Unrecoverable costs: money you don’t get back: rent for renters; mortgage interest, property tax, insurance, maintenance and transaction costs for owners.

What makes buying win or lose

Four inputs drive most of the answer, so it’s worth testing each one:

  • Time. Buying costs a few percent of the price upfront and about 6% when you sell. Staying longer spreads those costs over more years; moving within a few years rarely recovers them.
  • The price-to-rent ratio. Divide the home price by a year’s rent for a comparable place. In our example, $400,000 ÷ ($2,200 × 12) is about 15. The higher the ratio, the more renting tends to win; in markets where homes are cheap relative to rents, buying wins sooner.
  • Mortgage rates. A higher rate raises the owner’s monthly cost without adding any equity, which pushes the break-even year later. Refinancing later can help, but it isn’t guaranteed.
  • Price growth versus investment returns. Buying is effectively a leveraged bet on local home prices; renting and investing is a bet on the broader markets. If you expect investments to beat home price growth by a wide margin, renting looks better, and vice versa.

How mortgage rates change the answer

The mortgage rate is one of the most powerful inputs, because interest is the largest unrecoverable cost of owning in the early years. Keeping everything else from the example the same:

Each half-point of rate moves the 10-year result by roughly $20,000 for this home. When rates are high, renting and waiting can make sense, but no one can reliably predict where rates or prices go next, so compare at today’s rate rather than a hoped-for one.

The down payment matters less than you might expect. With 10% down the 10-year result is renting ahead by $7,772; with 30% down, buying is ahead by $4,085. A bigger down payment lowers interest but also leaves less money invested, so the two effects partly cancel out.

The 5% rule: a quick sanity check

Before running detailed numbers, a shortcut popularized by Canadian financial planner Ben Felix gives a rough answer. It estimates the yearly unrecoverable cost of owning as about 5% of the home’s price:

  • about 1% for property tax,
  • about 1% for maintenance,
  • about 3% for the cost of capital: mortgage interest on the borrowed part and the investment return the down payment could have earned.

For a $400,000 home, 5% is $20,000 a year, or about $1,667 a month. If you can rent a comparable home for less than that, renting is likely to be financially competitive; if rent is much higher, buying probably wins over the long run. The rule ignores your specific rate, taxes and timeline, so use it to frame the decision and the calculator to make it.

Costs owners often underestimate

  • Maintenance and repairs. Roofs, water heaters, HVAC systems and appliances all wear out. The common rule of thumb of 1% of the home’s value a year can be low for older homes.
  • Rising property taxes and insurance. Both tend to climb with home values and, in some regions, with disaster risk.
  • Selling costs. Agent commissions, transfer taxes and repairs to prepare for sale often add up to 6% to 10% of the price.
  • Furnishing and moving. A larger space often means more furniture, and every move costs money.

What about taxes?

The calculator leaves taxes out, and for most households that’s close to reality:

  • The mortgage interest deduction only helps if you itemize. With the 2026 standard deduction at $16,100 for single filers and $32,200 for married couples filing jointly, many owners get little or no extra benefit from mortgage interest and property tax, especially since the deduction for state and local taxes is capped.
  • Selling your home is usually tax-free. If you’ve owned and lived in it for at least two of the last five years, up to $250,000 of profit ($500,000 for married couples) is excluded from capital gains tax.
  • The renter’s investments are taxed. Dividends and gains in a taxable account are taxed along the way and when sold, unless the money is in a retirement account.

Together these tend to tilt the result slightly toward buying for long stays. If you itemize, or expect large gains, check the details with a tax professional.

When renting is the smarter move

Renting usually comes out ahead when you might move within about five years, when local prices are high relative to rents, when you don’t yet have a down payment plus a cash cushion for repairs, or when your income is uncertain. Renting while investing the difference consistently is a perfectly sound path to building wealth; the key word is consistently.

Beyond the numbers

Owning brings stability, control over your home and protection from rent increases, but also responsibility for repairs and less flexibility to move for a job. Renting keeps you mobile and leaves big repair bills to the landlord, at the cost of rent rises and less control. If the numbers are close, as in our 10-year example, these personal factors should decide.

Frequently asked questions

Is it better to rent or buy a house?

It depends mostly on how long you'll stay, local prices compared with rents, mortgage rates and how fast home values and rents rise. Buying has large one-time costs, typically a few percent of the price to buy and around 6% to sell, so it usually only pays off if you stay several years. This calculator compares the net worth you'd have either way.

How long do I need to stay for buying to make sense?

Often five to ten years, but it varies widely. In our $400,000 example, renting is still slightly ahead after 10 years, while buying comes out about $41,000 ahead after 15 years, breaking even in year 11. Higher rents, lower mortgage rates or faster price growth bring the break-even point sooner.

Why does the calculator invest the difference?

Because that's the fair comparison. A buyer ties up the down payment and closing costs in the home; a renter could invest that cash instead. Likewise, whoever has the lower monthly cost can invest the savings. Ignoring this makes buying look better than it is, because it compares home equity with zero.

Isn't rent just throwing money away?

Not exactly. Rent buys housing, just as mortgage interest, property tax, insurance and maintenance do for an owner. Those owning costs aren't recovered either. What matters is which path leaves you with more money after paying for housing, which is what the net worth comparison shows.

Does this include the mortgage interest tax deduction?

No. Since the standard deduction roughly doubled in 2018, most homeowners don't itemize, so the mortgage interest deduction doesn't lower their taxes. If you itemize, buying may look somewhat better than shown. Capital gains on a home sale are also left out; most sellers of a main home can exclude up to $250,000 of gain ($500,000 for married couples).

What home price growth should I assume?

Long-run national home price growth has often been close to or slightly above inflation, but local markets vary a lot and prices can fall. Try a conservative number and a lower one with Add to compare to see how sensitive the answer is.
