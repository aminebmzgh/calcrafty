Worked examples

A loan to a family member. You lend a relative $8,000 at 3% simple interest for 2 years. Interest = 8,000 × 0.03 × 2 = $480, so they repay $8,480. Putting the terms in a short written agreement avoids misunderstandings and, for larger loans, helps with IRS rules on below-market interest.

Interest for part of a year. A $15,000 balance at 6% accrues 15,000 × 0.06 × 30 ÷ 365 = $73.97 in 30 days on an actual/365 basis, or $75.00 under the banker’s rule (actual/360).

Finding the rate. A $2,500 deposit earns $93.75 in 9 months. Rate = 93.75 ÷ (2,500 × 0.75) = 5%.

Finding the time. How long until $4,000 at 6% earns $500? Time = 500 ÷ (4,000 × 0.06) = 2.08 years, a little over 2 years and 1 month.

Finding the principal. What deposit earns $337.50 in 18 months at 4.5%? Principal = 337.50 ÷ (0.045 × 1.5) = $5,000.

The simple interest formulas in plain English

Simple interest grows in a straight line: the same amount every year, because it’s always calculated on the original principal.

  • Interest: I = P × r × t
  • Total (future value): A = P × (1 + r × t)
  • Principal: P = I ÷ (r × t)
  • Rate: r = I ÷ (P × t)
  • Time: t = I ÷ (P × r)

Here r is the annual rate as a decimal (5% = 0.05) and t is in years. Months become years by dividing by 12, and days by dividing by 365 or 360. The Show the math panel shows each step with your numbers.

Simple vs compound interest

Simple interest earns the same amount every year. Compound interest adds each year’s interest to the balance, so the next year’s interest is a little larger. For $10,000 at 5%:

Years Simple interest total Compound interest total (annual) Difference
1 $10,500.00 $10,500.00 $0
5 $12,500.00 $12,762.82 $262.82
10 $15,000.00 $16,288.95 $1,288.95
20 $20,000.00 $26,532.98 $6,532.98
30 $25,000.00 $43,219.42 $18,219.42

For a year or less the two are almost identical. Over decades, compounding makes a large difference, which is why long-term savings grow much faster than simple interest suggests, and why unpaid credit card interest snowballs. The compound interest calculator models compounding with regular deposits.

Day counts: actual/365 vs actual/360

When time is measured in days, the result depends on how many days count as a year:

  • Actual/365: daily interest = annual rate ÷ 365. Common for consumer loans and deposit accounts.
  • Actual/360 (banker’s rule): daily interest = annual rate ÷ 360. Common for commercial loans, money market instruments and many business lines of credit.

Because 365 ÷ 360 = 1.0139, a loan quoted at the same rate costs about 1.4% more interest under actual/360. On a $500,000 commercial loan at 7%, that’s roughly $490 more a year. When comparing business loans, check which convention each lender uses. Treasury bills use a 360-day year for their quoted discount rate: a $10,000 investment at 4.5% for 182 days earns $227.50 on that basis.

Where simple interest shows up

  • Auto loans. Most car loans are simple interest loans: interest accrues daily on the balance you still owe. Paying early or extra reduces the interest straight away, and paying late increases it.
  • Personal loans and promissory notes, including loans between family members and private notes.
  • Bonds. A bond’s coupon is simple interest on its face value: a $1,000 bond with a 4% coupon pays $40 a year, usually in two $20 payments.
  • Short-term lending, such as bridge loans, trade credit and some payday products, often quoted as a flat percentage for a period.
  • Accrued interest when you buy a bond between coupon dates or settle an account partway through a period.

Savings accounts, CDs, credit cards and most mortgages use compounding or amortization instead, so use the matching calculator for those.

Simple interest loans: why paying early helps

On a simple interest auto or personal loan, each payment first covers the interest accrued since the last payment, and the rest reduces the principal. That has two practical effects:

  • Paying a few days early means fewer days of interest, so more of the payment goes to principal.
  • Paying late means more days of interest, so less of the payment reduces the balance, even if there’s no late fee yet.

Over a five-year car loan, consistently paying a week early saves a modest amount; paying even $50 extra a month saves much more. The auto loan calculator shows the full schedule.

Add-on interest: a costlier variant

Some lenders, especially for used cars and furniture, quote “add-on” interest: they calculate simple interest on the full original amount for the whole term, add it to the principal, and divide by the number of payments. Because you pay interest on money you’ve already repaid, the true annual rate is much higher than the quoted one, often close to double for a multi-year loan. Federal law requires the APR to be disclosed, so compare that figure, not the add-on rate.

Calculating simple interest by hand

You can do most simple interest problems in four steps:

  1. Write the rate as a decimal. 6.5% becomes 0.065.
  2. Write the time in years. 9 months is 0.75; 200 days is 200 ÷ 365 = 0.548.
  3. Multiply principal × rate × time. $12,000 × 0.065 × 4 = $3,120.
  4. Add the principal if you need the total. $12,000 + $3,120 = $15,120.

A quick sense check helps: one year of interest is just principal × rate. $12,000 at 6.5% earns $780 a year, so four years should be four times that, $3,120.

Common mistakes

  • Using the percentage instead of the decimal. 5% is 0.05, not 5. Using 5 makes the answer 100 times too large.
  • Mixing months and years. A rate is almost always yearly. Six months is 0.5 years, not 6.
  • Using the wrong day count. Check whether a loan uses 365 or 360 days; the difference adds up on large balances.
  • Assuming simple interest when it compounds. Savings accounts, CDs and credit cards usually compound, so simple interest understates what you’ll earn or owe over time.
  • Confusing an add-on rate with an APR. An add-on rate looks low; the APR on the loan disclosure is the true yearly cost.

Using simple interest to compare short-term offers

Simple interest is a handy way to put short-term costs and returns on the same yearly footing. Suppose a supplier offers a 2% discount if you pay an invoice in 10 days instead of 30. Skipping the discount is like borrowing the money for 20 extra days at a cost of 2%. Solving for the rate, that’s 2% × 365 ÷ 20, about a 36.5% simple annual rate, far more than most business credit lines charge. So taking the discount is usually the better deal, even if you have to borrow to do it. The same approach works well for comparing a short-term certificate, a promotional financing fee or the cost of paying a bill late.

Simple interest on savings and bonds

A few deposit products pay simple interest: interest is paid out to you, by check or transfer, rather than added to the balance, so it never earns interest itself. Some CDs offer this option, and a bond’s coupon works the same way. If you reinvest those payments yourself, you get a result close to compounding; if you spend them, your balance grows by simple interest only. When comparing products, the APY tells you the effective yearly return assuming interest stays in the account.

Key terms

  • Principal: the original amount borrowed, lent or deposited.
  • Simple interest: interest calculated only on the principal.
  • Accrued interest: interest that has built up but hasn’t been paid yet.
  • Day count convention: the rule for converting days into a fraction of a year.
  • Future value: principal plus interest at the end of the term.

Frequently asked questions

What is the simple interest formula?

I = P × r × t, where P is the principal, r is the annual rate as a decimal and t is the time in years. $5,000 at 4% for 3 years earns 5,000 × 0.04 × 3 = $600. The total you'd have, or owe, is A = P + I = P × (1 + r × t) = $5,600.

How do I calculate simple interest for months or days?

Convert the time to years first. For months, divide by 12: 18 months is 1.5 years. For days, divide by 365 (or 360 under the banker's rule): 90 days on $10,000 at 5% is 10,000 × 0.05 × 90 ÷ 365 = $123.29, or $125.00 on a 360-day year.

What is the difference between simple and compound interest?

Simple interest is calculated only on the original principal. Compound interest is also calculated on interest already added. $10,000 at 5% for 10 years earns $5,000 with simple interest but $6,288.95 compounded annually. Over 30 years, the gap grows to more than $18,000.

Where is simple interest used?

On most auto loans and many personal loans, where interest accrues daily on the remaining balance; on short-term loans and promissory notes; on some bonds' coupon payments; and in Treasury bill and money market calculations that use a 360-day year. Savings accounts and credit cards usually compound.

How do I solve for the interest rate?

Rearrange the formula: r = I ÷ (P × t). If $2,500 earns $93.75 in 9 months (0.75 years), the rate is 93.75 ÷ (2,500 × 0.75) = 5%. Choose Rate under Solve for and the calculator does it for you.

What is the banker's rule?

It's the actual/360 day count: interest for each day is the annual rate divided by 360 rather than 365. Because a year is treated as 360 days, the borrower pays slightly more, about 1.4% more interest. Many commercial loans and money market instruments use it.
