How to use this compound interest calculator

Enter a starting balance, a monthly deposit, a rate and a number of years. The final balance, total interest and APY update as you type, and the chart shows how much of the balance is your money versus interest.

  1. Starting balance. What you have saved today. Use 0 if you’re starting from scratch.
  2. Monthly deposit. How much you’ll add every month.
  3. Interest rate. The default is the current 3-month Treasury bill yield from the Federal Reserve, a useful benchmark for what high-yield savings accounts and money market funds pay. Enter your bank’s rate for a precise answer.
  4. Compounded. Daily is typical for savings accounts; CDs may compound monthly or quarterly.
  5. Deposit timing. Under More options, choose start-of-month deposits if you save on payday at the beginning of the month; they earn one extra month of interest.

Worked example: $5,000 plus $150 a month for 15 years

Someone puts $5,000 into a high-yield savings account paying 4.5% compounded daily and adds $150 at the end of every month for 15 years.

  • Total deposited: $5,000 + ($150 × 180 months) = $32,000
  • Interest earned: $16,307.93
  • Final balance: $48,307.93
  • APY: 4.6025%

In the first year the account earns $267.79 in interest. In the fifteenth year it earns $2,082.35, nearly eight times as much, even though the rate never changed. That’s compounding: interest is paid on past interest, so growth speeds up as the balance grows.

Without the monthly deposits, the $5,000 would grow to only $9,819.76. Leaving the plan running for 30 years instead of 15 turns $59,000 of deposits into $133,362.60, with interest ($74,362.60) larger than everything deposited.

The compound interest formula in plain English

Each period, interest is added to the balance, and the next period’s interest is calculated on that larger balance.

A = P × (1 + r ÷ n)^(n × t)

  • P is the starting balance and t the number of years.
  • r is the annual rate as a decimal (4.5% = 0.045).
  • n is how many times a year interest compounds (365 for daily, 12 for monthly).

Because deposits arrive monthly, Calcrafty steps through every month using the monthly rate that’s exactly equivalent to your compounding choice, m = (1 + r ÷ n)^(n ÷ 12) − 1, and adds each deposit when it’s made. For continuous compounding, m = e^(r ÷ 12) − 1. The Show the math panel shows m and each part of the balance with your numbers.

Key terms

  • Principal: the money you start with.
  • Compounding: earning interest on previously earned interest.
  • APY (annual percentage yield): the real one-year return including compounding.
  • Rule of 72: 72 ÷ rate ≈ years to double.
  • Annuity due vs ordinary annuity: deposits at the start versus the end of each period.

Why time matters more than the rate

Compound growth is slow at first and fast later. Interest is paid on a balance that already includes past interest, so each year adds more dollars than the year before, even though the rate is unchanged.

Take a single $10,000 deposit at 4.5% compounded daily, with nothing added:

Years left alone Balance Growth
10 $15,682.69 $5,682.69
20 $24,594.67 $14,594.67
30 $38,571.05 $28,571.05

The second decade earns about $8,900 and the third about $14,000, from the same original deposit. That’s why starting early matters so much, and why pulling money out early costs more than the amount you withdraw: you also lose all the growth it would have earned later.

A few practical points follow from this:

  • Automate deposits. Regular deposits on payday keep the balance growing without relying on willpower, and each deposit starts compounding right away.
  • Don’t chase tiny compounding differences. Daily versus monthly compounding changes the result by a few dollars; a better rate or a longer horizon changes it by thousands.
  • Mind inflation and taxes. Interest in a regular savings account is taxable each year, and inflation lowers what the balance buys. A rate below inflation means your savings lose buying power even as the number grows.
  • Use the right account for the timeline. Insured savings accounts and CDs suit money you’ll need within a few years. For money you won’t touch for decades, long-term investing has historically grown faster, with more ups and downs along the way.

Starting early vs saving more later

The clearest way to see compounding is to compare two savers earning the same 6% a year, compounded monthly.

  • Alex saves $200 a month from age 25 to 35, then stops and never adds another dollar. Total deposited: $24,000.
  • Blake starts at 35 and saves $200 a month for 30 years, until 65. Total deposited: $72,000.

At 35, Alex’s account holds $32,775.87. Left alone for 30 more years, it grows to $197,395.14 by 65. Blake, who deposited three times as much, ends with $200,903.01, only about $3,500 more. Alex’s ten early years did almost as much as Blake’s thirty, because Alex’s money had decades longer to compound.

The lesson isn’t that late starters are out of luck: Blake still built a six-figure balance. It’s that every year of waiting has to be made up with larger deposits. If you’re starting later, the monthly deposit field shows how much more each month closes the gap.

Compound interest vs simple interest

With simple interest, you earn interest only on the original amount. With compound interest, you also earn interest on interest already paid. The gap is small at first and large over time.

Here’s $10,000 at 5% a year, with interest added once a year and nothing withdrawn:

Years Simple interest Compound interest Difference
10 $15,000 $16,288.95 $1,288.95
20 $20,000 $26,532.98 $6,532.98
30 $25,000 $43,219.42 $18,219.42

Savings accounts, CDs and most investments compound. Simple interest is more common on some loans, such as most auto loans, where interest is charged only on the remaining principal and not added to the balance.

How often interest compounds, and why it matters less than you think

More frequent compounding means interest starts earning interest sooner, so the effective yearly return, the APY, is slightly higher than the stated rate. For $10,000 at a 5% stated rate for one year:

Compounding Balance after 1 year APY
Annually $10,500.00 5.0000%
Quarterly $10,509.45 5.0945%
Monthly $10,511.62 5.1162%
Daily $10,512.67 5.1267%
Continuously $10,512.71 5.1271%

Going from monthly to daily compounding adds about a dollar a year on $10,000. Compare accounts by APY, which banks must disclose under the Truth in Savings Act, rather than by compounding frequency. A 5.25% APY compounded monthly beats a 5% rate compounded daily.

The Rule of 72: a quick check

To estimate how long money takes to double, divide 72 by the annual rate. It’s a mental shortcut, and it’s surprisingly accurate for typical rates:

Rate Rule of 72 Exact doubling time (annual compounding)
3% 24.0 years 23.4 years
4% 18.0 years 17.7 years
6% 12.0 years 11.9 years
8% 9.0 years 9.0 years
10% 7.2 years 7.3 years
12% 6.0 years 6.1 years

It works for costs too: at 3% inflation, prices double in about 24 years, so $1,000 today buys roughly what $500 will in 2050.

A similar shortcut, the Rule of 115, estimates how long money takes to triple: divide 115 by the rate. At 6%, that’s about 19 years (the exact figure is 18.9). Both rules assume the rate holds steady, interest stays in the account and nothing is withdrawn, so treat them as quick sanity checks and use the calculator above for real planning, especially when you’re adding money every month.

When compounding works against you

The same math that grows savings grows debt. Most credit cards charge interest daily on the balance, so unpaid interest is added to what you owe and starts accruing interest of its own.

Take a $5,000 credit card balance at 22% APR, compounded daily, with no payments and no new purchases (in reality, minimum payments are required, so this shows the pull of compounding rather than a real statement):

Time Balance
Today $5,000.00
1 year $6,229.97
3 years $9,672.04
5 years $15,015.85

The effective yearly rate is about 24.6%, higher than the 22% APR on the statement, and the balance roughly doubles every 3.2 years. That’s why paying off high-interest debt usually beats saving at 4% or 5%: every dollar of a 22% balance you clear earns you a guaranteed 22%, tax-free. Frequently asked questions

What is the compound interest formula?

For a single deposit, A = P(1 + r/n)^(nt), where P is the starting amount, r is the annual rate as a decimal, n is the number of compounding periods per year and t is the number of years. With regular deposits, each deposit grows the same way from the month it's made, and the calculator adds them all up month by month.

How much does compounding frequency matter?

Less than most people think. $5,000 plus $150 a month at 4.5% for 15 years grows to $48,307.93 compounded daily, $48,269.98 monthly and $47,853.19 annually. The rate itself and how long you leave the money matter far more than daily versus monthly compounding.

What's the difference between APR and APY?

APR is the stated annual rate before compounding. APY is what you actually earn in a year once compounding is included, so it's always equal to or higher than the APR. A 4.5% rate compounded daily has an APY of about 4.60%. US banks must quote APY on savings accounts under the Truth in Savings Act, which makes it the right number for comparing them.

How long does it take to double my money?

Divide 72 by the interest rate for a quick estimate (the Rule of 72). At 4.5%, that's 16 years. The exact figure at 4.5% compounded daily is 15.4 years, which the calculator shows in the explanation. Regular deposits get you to any target much sooner.

Is compound interest calculated daily or monthly on savings accounts?

Most US high-yield savings accounts compound interest daily and pay it into your account monthly. CDs vary; some compound daily, others monthly or quarterly. Check the account's Truth in Savings disclosure, then choose the matching option here.

Does this include taxes or inflation?

No. Interest in a regular savings account is taxed as ordinary income each year, which lowers the real growth, and inflation reduces what the final balance can buy. Use the investment calculator to see a balance in today's dollars, adjusted by current inflation.
