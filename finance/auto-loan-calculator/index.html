How to use this auto loan calculator

Enter the price, down payment, rate and term to see your monthly payment immediately. Then add trade-in, tax and fees to get the number the dealer will actually quote.

  1. Vehicle price. The negotiated price before tax, after any rebates.
  2. Down payment and rate. The APR starts at the Federal Reserve’s latest average for 60-month new-car loans at commercial banks. Used-car loans and lower credit scores usually carry higher rates.
  3. Loan term. Tap a term from 36 to 84 months.
  4. Trade-in. Enter what the dealer offers for your current car, and the payoff amount if you still have a loan on it.
  5. Sales tax and fees. Use your combined state and local rate, plus title, registration and documentation fees.

Worked example: a used SUV with a trade-in

A buyer agrees on a $28,400 used SUV, puts $2,500 down and trades in a car worth $6,000 that still has $1,800 owed on it. Sales tax is 6.25% and fees come to $450. They’re approved at 7% for 60 months.

  • Sales tax: ($28,400 − $6,000) × 6.25% = $1,400
  • Net trade-in: $6,000 − $1,800 = $4,200
  • Amount financed: $28,400 + $1,400 + $450 − $2,500 − $4,200 = $23,550
  • Monthly payment: $466.32
  • Total interest: $4,429.12, so the SUV costs $30,479.12 including the down payment

Here’s how the term changes the same $23,550 loan:

Term Monthly payment Total interest
36 months $727.16 $2,627.58
48 months $563.93 $3,518.87
60 months $466.32 $4,429.12
72 months $401.50 $5,358.36
84 months $355.43 $6,306.46

Stretching from 60 to 84 months saves about $111 a month but adds $1,877 in interest.

The car payment formula in plain English

There are two steps: work out how much you’re borrowing, then turn it into a fixed monthly payment.

Amount financed = price + sales tax + fees − down payment − (trade-in value − amount owed)

Payment = P × r(1 + r)ⁿ ÷ ((1 + r)ⁿ − 1), where P is the amount financed, r is the APR ÷ 12 and n is the number of months.

Each payment covers that month’s interest (balance × r) and the rest reduces the balance. If you get 0% dealer financing, the payment is simply the amount financed divided by the number of months. The Show the math panel above walks through both steps with your numbers.

Key terms

  • Amount financed: what you actually borrow after tax, fees, down payment and trade-in.
  • APR: the yearly cost of the loan including required finance charges.
  • Negative equity (being “upside down”): owing more on a car than it’s worth.
  • Out-the-door price: the total you’d pay in cash, including tax and fees.
  • Pre-approval: a conditional loan offer from a lender before you shop.

How your rate changes the cost of a car

Your APR matters as much as the price you negotiate. It depends mostly on your credit score, whether the car is new or used, the loan term and the lender, so two buyers can pay very different amounts for the same car.

Here’s the same $23,550, 60-month loan at different rates:

APR Monthly payment Total interest
5% $444.42 $3,115.03
7% $466.32 $4,429.12
9% $488.86 $5,781.56
12% $523.86 $7,881.30

Going from 12% to 7% saves about $3,450 over the loan, more than most buyers manage to knock off the sticker price. A few habits help you get the lower end of that range:

  • Get pre-approved first. A bank or credit union offer gives you a rate to beat. Dealers can often arrange financing, but you’ll know whether their offer is actually good.
  • Compare the APR, not the payment. A dealer can lower the payment by stretching the term, which usually raises the total cost.
  • Shop within a short window. Credit scoring models generally treat several auto loan inquiries within a short period as one, so comparing lenders over a week or two has little effect on your score.
  • Read the buyer’s order line by line. Optional add-ons such as extended warranties, paint protection or GAP insurance are often rolled into the loan. Each one adds interest as well as its price, and you can say no or buy them elsewhere.
  • Watch 0% offers. Manufacturer 0% financing can be excellent, but it’s sometimes offered instead of a cash rebate. Compare the 0% deal with taking the rebate and financing elsewhere.

New vs used car loans

Lenders usually charge more to finance a used car than a new one: used cars are worth less as collateral and lose value less predictably, and manufacturers often subsidize new-car rates to move inventory. The gap can be several percentage points.

On a $25,000 loan for 60 months, here’s what a 4-point difference does:

Loan Rate Monthly payment Total interest
New car 7% $495.03 $4,701.82
Used car 11% $543.56 $7,613.65

A cheaper used car can still be the better deal, because a lower price and slower depreciation often outweigh the higher rate. But compare the total cost, not just the sticker price: a used car financed at a high rate over a long term can end up costing nearly as much as a new one. Credit unions are often the most competitive lenders for used-car loans and for refinancing an existing car loan.

How long should a car loan be?

The shorter the better, as long as the payment fits. Cars lose value quickly in the first few years, and a long loan pays the balance down slowly, which can leave you owing more than the car is worth.

Here’s the $23,550 loan from the example at 7%, comparing a 48-month and a 72-month term:

Term Monthly payment Balance after 2 years Balance after 3 years
48 months $563.93 $12,595.64 $6,517.62
72 months $401.50 $16,766.98 $13,003.45

After three years, the 72-month borrower still owes twice as much. If the car is totaled, stolen or needs replacing, that balance has to be paid off even though the car is gone. GAP insurance covers the difference after an insurance payout, but a shorter loan avoids the problem in the first place. To see what price fits a four-year loan on your income, use the car affordability calculator.

Paying off a car loan early

Most auto loans charge simple interest on the remaining balance, so any extra payment toward principal cuts future interest straight away. On the $23,550, 60-month loan at 7%, adding $100 a month pays it off a year early, in 48 months, and saves $927.51 in interest.

Before paying extra:

  • Check for a prepayment penalty. Most auto loans don’t have one, but some subprime loans do.
  • Ask the lender to apply extra payments to principal. Otherwise some servicers treat it as an early payment of next month’s bill.
  • Pay higher-rate debt first. If you have credit card balances at 20% or more, paying those down saves more than prepaying a 7% car loan.

Cash rebate or 0% financing?

Manufacturers often make you choose between a cash rebate and a 0% financing offer. Which is better depends on the rate you’d pay elsewhere.

Take a $30,000 car with a choice of 0% for 60 months or a $2,500 rebate:

Option Amount financed Monthly payment Total paid
0% financing $30,000 $500.00 $30,000.00
Rebate, financed at 7% $27,500 $544.53 $32,672.02
Rebate, financed at 4% $27,500 $506.45 $30,387.29

Here 0% wins in both cases, but a bigger rebate, a lower outside rate or a shorter loan can flip the answer. Run both versions through the calculator with Add to compare, and remember that 0% offers usually require excellent credit. If you’re paying cash rather than financing, the rebate is simply money off the price, so take it.

Dealer financing traps to avoid

Dealers can arrange good financing, but the finance office is also where a deal quietly gets more expensive:

  • Rate markup. A dealer can add to the rate the lender approved and keep part of the difference. Two points more on the example loan, 9% instead of 7%, costs $1,352.44 extra. A pre-approval tells you what rate you qualify for.
  • Payment packing. Quoting a monthly payment that already includes add-ons, then presenting them as free. Ask for an itemized price.
  • Stretching the term. “We can get you to $400 a month” usually means a longer loan, not a lower price.
  • Spot delivery (“yo-yo” financing). Driving home before the financing is final, then being called back to sign at a higher rate. Don’t let go of your trade-in until the loan is approved.

What to do with your result

Negotiate the out-the-door price first and the financing second; a low monthly payment can hide a long term or a high price. Check the payment against your take-home pay with the paycheck calculator, and plan a bigger down payment with the savings goal calculator.

Frequently asked questions

How is a car payment calculated?

First the amount financed is worked out as the price plus sales tax and fees, minus your down payment and the net value of your trade-in. Then the standard amortization formula, M = P × r(1+r)^n ÷ ((1+r)^n − 1), gives the fixed monthly payment, where r is the APR divided by 12 and n is the number of months.

Does a trade-in reduce sales tax?

In most states, yes. You pay sales tax only on the price minus the trade-in value. On a $28,400 car with a $6,000 trade-in at 6.25%, that's $1,400 in tax instead of $1,775. A few states, including California, tax the full price regardless of a trade-in; untick the option under More options if that applies to you.

What happens if I owe more on my trade-in than it's worth?

That gap is called negative equity, and dealers usually add it to your new loan. You then pay interest on the old car's debt as well as the new one, and you'll likely owe more than the new car is worth for longer. If you can, pay down the old loan or pay the difference in cash.

Is a 72- or 84-month car loan a good idea?

Longer loans lower the payment but raise the total cost and often come with higher rates. On $23,550 at 7%, an 84-month loan costs $355.43 a month but $6,306.46 in interest, versus $466.32 and $4,429.12 over 60 months. Because cars lose value quickly, long loans also leave you owing more than the car is worth for much of the term.

How much should I put down on a car?

Many lenders and consumer guides suggest about 20% down on a new car and 10% on a used one. A bigger down payment shrinks the loan, cuts interest and lowers the risk of owing more than the car is worth. Any amount helps, though, and the calculator shows the exact effect.

Should I get pre-approved for an auto loan?

Getting a pre-approval from a bank or credit union before visiting the dealer gives you a rate to compare against dealer financing and keeps the conversation on the car's price, not just the monthly payment. The CFPB recommends shopping for financing the same way you shop for the car itself.
