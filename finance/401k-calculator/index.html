What this means

Putting 6% of a $70,000 salary into your 401(k) is $4,200 this year, and your employer adds $2,100. With raises and a 6.5% return, the account could reach $1,628,470 by 67, about $421,567 in today’s money.

You're getting the full employer match, which adds $138,966 over your career.

Show the math

  1. 1. Your contribution this year

    salary × contribution % (capped at the IRS limit)

    $70,000 × 6% = $4,200

  2. 2. Employer match this year

    salary × min(your %, match limit %) × match rate

    $70,000 × 6% × 50% = $2,100

  3. 3. Growth each month

    balance × (1 + return ÷ 12) + contributions ÷ 12

    6.5% a year, compounded monthly, salary rising 3% a year = 37 years

  4. 4. In today’s dollars

    balance ÷ (1 + inflation)^years

    $1,628,470 ÷ (1 + 3.72%)^37 = $421,567

How to use this 401(k) calculator

Enter where you are today and how you save, and the calculator projects your 401(k) to retirement, month by month, with raises and the employer match.

  1. Ages. Your current age and the age you plan to retire.
  2. Salary. Your current annual pay before tax.
  3. Your contribution. The percentage of each paycheck you put in. It’s automatically capped at the IRS limit for your age.
  4. Employer match. Two numbers from your plan: the match rate (for example, 50% means 50 cents per dollar) and the cap (for example, up to 6% of pay).
  5. Balance and return. What’s in the account now and an average yearly return after fees.
  6. More options. Your expected yearly raise and inflation, used to show the result in today’s dollars.

The result shows where the money comes from, a table of your balance every five years, and how much match you’d gain by contributing more.

Worked example: a 42-year-old catching up

Sam is 42, earns $95,000, contributes 8% and has $120,000 saved. Sam’s employer matches 100% of contributions up to 4% of pay. Sam plans to retire at 67 and assumes 6% returns, 2.5% raises and 2.5% inflation.

  • This year: Sam contributes $7,600 and the employer adds $3,800.
  • Balance at 67: about $1,370,035, roughly $738,984 in today’s dollars.
  • Where it comes from: $120,000 already saved, $259,599 of Sam’s contributions, $129,799 of employer match and $860,636 of investment growth.

Growth is the biggest contributor, even for someone starting in their 40s, because the money compounds for 25 years. And because Sam already contributes more than 4%, the full match is captured.

How the projection works

Each year, the calculator works out your contribution as salary × your percentage, capped at that year’s IRS limit, and the employer match as salary × the matched percentage × the match rate. Those contributions are added monthly, and the balance grows by the expected return compounded monthly:

balance = balance × (1 + return ÷ 12) + (your contribution + match) ÷ 12

At the end of each year, your salary rises by the raise you entered. The final balance is converted to today’s dollars by dividing by (1 + inflation)^years. IRS limits are held at their 2026 levels; in practice they rise with inflation, so the projection is slightly conservative for high savers. The Show the math panel shows each step with your numbers.

Traditional or Roth 401(k)?

Many plans offer both. With a traditional 401(k), contributions come out of your pay before income tax, lowering this year’s tax bill, and withdrawals in retirement are taxed as ordinary income. With a Roth 401(k), contributions are made after tax, but qualified withdrawals, including all the growth, are tax-free.

The choice mostly comes down to whether you expect your tax rate to be higher now or in retirement. Earlier in a career, when income and tax brackets are lower, Roth contributions are often attractive; in peak earning years, the traditional deduction is usually worth more. Employer matches go into the traditional (pre-tax) side by default, although plans may now allow Roth matching. Splitting contributions between the two gives you flexibility to manage taxes later. Our guide to Roth vs traditional 401(k) works through examples.

401(k) or IRA?

A 401(k) is offered through your employer and has much higher contribution limits ($24,500 in 2026, versus $7,500 for an IRA) and, often, a match. An IRA is opened on your own, usually with a wider choice of investments and lower fees. A common order is: contribute to the 401(k) up to the full match, then fund an IRA, then go back and raise 401(k) contributions toward the limit. Traditional IRA deductions phase out at higher incomes if you’re covered by a workplace plan, and Roth IRA contributions phase out at higher incomes too.

What the match is really worth

For the default saver, raising the contribution from 3% to 6% of a $70,000 salary costs $2,100 a year before tax, and the employer adds $1,050 more. That’s a guaranteed 50% return on the extra money before any investment growth, and the table above shows it compounding to about $700,000 more by 67. Because traditional contributions are pre-tax, the drop in take-home pay is smaller than $2,100.

Vesting: when the match becomes yours

Employer contributions often vest over time. Under a cliff schedule, you own none of the match until a set date (at most three years), then all of it. Under a graded schedule, ownership rises in steps, commonly 20% a year from year two, reaching 100% by year six at the latest. If you’re thinking of leaving a job, check how close you are to the next vesting date; staying a few extra months can be worth thousands.

When you change jobs

Your own contributions and their growth are always yours. When you leave, you can usually:

  • Leave the money in your old plan, if the balance is above the plan’s minimum.
  • Roll it into your new employer’s plan, keeping everything in one place.
  • Roll it into an IRA, often with more investment choices.
  • Cash it out, which usually triggers income tax plus a 10% penalty if you’re under 59½, and should generally be a last resort.

Ask for a direct rollover, from one plan to the other, so no tax is withheld along the way.

Taking money out in retirement

You can generally withdraw from a 401(k) without penalty from age 59½, or from 55 if you leave the employer in or after the year you turn 55. Traditional 401(k) balances are subject to required minimum distributions starting at age 73, or 75 for people born in 1960 or later. Roth 401(k) balances are no longer subject to required distributions during the owner’s lifetime.

Getting the most from your 401(k)

  • Always take the full match. It’s an instant return of 50% or 100% on the money you put in, more than any investment.
  • Increase your rate with each raise. Raising your contribution by one point when your pay goes up means your take-home pay never falls.
  • Check your vesting schedule. Employer contributions may vest over several years; leaving before then can mean forfeiting part of the match.
  • Choose low-cost funds. Fees compound just like returns.
  • Avoid early withdrawals and loans. Money taken out before 59½ usually triggers income tax plus a 10% penalty, and loses years of growth.
  • Roll it over when you change jobs. Moving an old 401(k) into your new plan or an IRA keeps it invested and easier to manage.

Key terms

  • Deferral: the part of your pay you choose to put into the 401(k).
  • Employer match: money your employer adds based on your contributions.
  • Vesting: the schedule on which employer contributions become fully yours.
  • Catch-up contribution: an extra amount workers aged 50 and over may contribute.
  • Expense ratio: a fund’s yearly fee as a percentage of your investment.
  • Traditional vs Roth 401(k): pre-tax contributions taxed on withdrawal, versus after-tax contributions withdrawn tax-free.

Frequently asked questions

How much will my 401(k) be worth when I retire?

It depends on your contributions, your employer's match, your returns and time. A 30-year-old earning $70,000, contributing 6% with a 50% match on up to 6%, starting with $20,000 and earning 6.5% a year with 3% raises, could have about $1.63 million at 67, roughly $546,000 in today's dollars.

What is the 401(k) contribution limit for 2026?

$24,500 for employee deferrals. Workers aged 50 and over can add an $8,000 catch-up, for $32,500, and those aged 60 to 63 can add $11,250 instead, for $35,750. Employer contributions don't count toward the employee limit, though there's a higher overall limit for combined contributions.

How does a 401(k) match work?

Your employer adds money based on what you contribute. A common formula is 50% of your contributions up to 6% of pay: contribute 6% of a $70,000 salary ($4,200) and your employer adds $2,100. Contribute less than 6% and you get proportionally less of the match.

What percentage should I contribute to my 401(k)?

At minimum, enough to get the full employer match, since that's an immediate return. Many planners suggest saving 15% of pay for retirement in total, including the match. If that's too much now, raise your contribution by 1 percentage point each year.

Is the 401(k) balance shown before or after tax?

Before tax. Traditional 401(k) withdrawals are taxed as ordinary income in retirement, so the spendable amount will be lower. Roth 401(k) contributions are made after tax, and qualified withdrawals, including the growth, are tax-free.

What return should I assume?

Use a realistic long-run average after fund fees. Many planners use 5% to 7% a year for a diversified portfolio of stocks and bonds. Try a lower rate as well; the difference between 5.5% and 7.5% changes the default projection by about $800,000.
