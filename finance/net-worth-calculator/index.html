How to use this net worth calculator

Your net worth is a snapshot of your finances on one day: what you own minus what you owe. The calculator works it out in a few steps.

  1. Assets. Enter the current value of your cash and savings, taxable investments (brokerage accounts, stocks, funds), retirement accounts (401(k), IRA, HSA), your home, vehicles and other valuables.
  2. Debts. Enter what you still owe on your mortgage, car loans, student loans, credit cards and anything else, such as a personal loan or a HELOC.
  3. Age. Optional, and used only to compare your result with the median household in your age group.

The result shows your net worth, how it compares with the median for your age, your liquid net worth, your home equity and your debt-to-asset ratio. The Show the math panel lists each total. Nothing you type leaves your browser.

The formula

Net worth = total assets − total liabilities

The other figures the calculator shows are:

  • Home equity = home value − mortgage balance.
  • Liquid net worth = cash + taxable investments − credit card balances − other debts. It leaves out the home, vehicles and retirement accounts, which you can’t easily turn into spending money.
  • Debt-to-asset ratio = total debts ÷ total assets × 100. Below 50% means you own more than you owe; above 100% means net worth is negative.

  • Net worth: $525,000 − $329,500 = $195,500.
  • Compared with the median: the median for ages 35 to 44 is about $135,000, so this household is about $60,500 above it.
  • Home equity: $380,000 − $290,000 = $90,000.
  • Liquid net worth: $12,000 + $25,000 − $3,500 = $33,500.
  • Debt-to-asset ratio: $329,500 ÷ $525,000 = 62.8%.

Most of this household’s wealth is in the home and retirement accounts; the liquid figure is much smaller. That’s typical, and it’s why liquid net worth is worth tracking alongside the headline number.

Net worth by age

The Federal Reserve’s Survey of Consumer Finances, run every three years, is the most authoritative source on U.S. household wealth. Its 2022 medians, rounded, are:

Age of head of household Median net worth (2022)
Under 35 $39,000
35 to 44 $135,000
45 to 54 $247,000
55 to 64 $364,000
65 to 74 $410,000
75 and over $335,000

The median for all households was about $193,000. Net worth typically grows through a working life as mortgages are paid down and retirement savings compound, peaks around retirement, then declines as people spend their savings.

The median is the middle household: half have more, half have less. The average (mean) is several times higher, because a small number of very wealthy households pull it up. That’s why we compare with the median, which better describes a typical household. Our guide to a good net worth by age goes further, with benchmarks based on income.

What counts as an asset, and at what value

Use what each thing would realistically fetch today, not what you paid:

  • Cash: checking, savings, money market accounts and CDs, at their balance.
  • Investments: the current market value of brokerage accounts, stocks, bonds and funds.
  • Retirement accounts: the current balance. Traditional 401(k) and IRA balances will be taxed when withdrawn, so some people count them at, say, 75% to 85% of their value. Either approach is fine if you’re consistent.
  • Home: a realistic sale price, from recent sales of similar homes nearby. Online estimates can be off by 5% to 10%; selling costs would take another 6% to 10%.
  • Vehicles: private-sale value from a pricing guide, which falls every year.
  • Other assets: only things with a real resale value, such as a boat, collectibles or a business stake. Furniture, clothes and electronics are usually worth far less than you’d think; leave them out.

Don’t count future income, a pension you can’t cash out, or expected inheritances.

What counts as a debt

Everything you’d need to pay off to be debt-free: the mortgage principal (not the total of future payments), HELOC balance, car loans, student loans, credit card balances (even if you’ll pay them off this month), personal loans, medical debt, money owed to family, and any tax you owe.

Why some moves don’t change net worth

Paying a $3,500 credit card balance from savings reduces both assets and debts by the same amount, so net worth stays at $195,500. It still helps, because it stops interest charges, but net worth only rises when you spend less than you earn, when investments grow, or when an asset rises in value.

The opposite also holds. Buying a new car with a loan adds the car as an asset and the loan as a debt, but the car loses value as soon as you drive it away, so net worth falls over time. Money spent on things that don’t hold value, such as holidays or dining out, reduces net worth directly.

How to grow your net worth

  • Save a fixed share of income automatically, into a 401(k), IRA or savings account, before you see it.
  • Take the full employer match. It’s an instant 50% or 100% return; see the 401(k) calculator.
  • Pay off high-interest debt first. Every dollar of credit card debt repaid at 22% APR is a guaranteed 22% return. The debt payoff calculator builds a plan.
  • Keep an emergency fund so surprises don’t go on a credit card; the emergency fund calculator suggests a target.
  • Invest for the long term in low-cost, diversified funds, and let compounding work.
  • Avoid lifestyle creep. When income rises, save at least half of the raise.
  • Be wary of depreciating purchases on credit, especially new cars.

Reading your debt-to-asset ratio

The debt-to-asset ratio shows how much of what you own is financed by borrowing. It’s the same idea a lender uses when looking at a business’s balance sheet.

Debt-to-asset ratio What it usually means
0% to 30% Low leverage, typical of people close to or in retirement
30% to 60% Moderate, common for homeowners a few years into a mortgage
60% to 100% High, often a recent home purchase or large student loans
Over 100% You owe more than you own; net worth is negative

The example household’s 62.8% is high mainly because it bought a home with a large mortgage, which is normal. The ratio falls as the mortgage is paid down and savings grow. A high ratio driven by credit cards is more concerning than one driven by a mortgage, because card debt costs far more and isn’t backed by an asset that holds its value. For a view based on income rather than assets, see the debt-to-income ratio calculator.

Couples and joint finances

The Federal Reserve figures are per household, so if you share finances with a partner, include both of your assets and debts and compare the combined total. If you keep finances separate, you can calculate each net worth individually, splitting jointly owned assets such as a home by your ownership shares. Either way, be consistent from one year to the next.

Tracking net worth over time

A single number is less useful than the trend. Recalculate on the same dates each year with the same method, and write the figures down. Over several years, your net worth should rise by at least what you save, plus investment growth. If it doesn’t, look at whether debts are growing, whether spending has crept up, or whether a big depreciating purchase is weighing on the total.

Market swings can move net worth sharply in a single year, especially when most of it is in investments. Judge progress over three to five years rather than one.

Key terms

  • Asset: something you own that has value.
  • Liability: a debt you owe, such as a mortgage, loan or credit card balance.
  • Equity: the value of an asset minus the debt secured on it, such as home value minus mortgage.
  • Liquid assets: cash and investments you can turn into cash quickly without penalties.
  • Median: the middle value; half of households are above it and half below.

What to do with your result

If your net worth is below where you’d like it, the biggest levers are usually your savings rate and your debts. Project how savings could grow with the investment calculator, and check whether you’re on track for retirement with the retirement calculator.

Frequently asked questions

How do I calculate my net worth?

Add up the current value of everything you own (cash, investments, retirement accounts, your home, vehicles and other assets), then subtract everything you owe (mortgage, car and student loans, credit cards and other debts). With $525,000 of assets and $329,500 of debts, net worth is $195,500.

What is a good net worth for my age?

The Federal Reserve's 2022 Survey of Consumer Finances found median household net worth of about $39,000 under 35, $135,000 at 35 to 44, $247,000 at 45 to 54, $364,000 at 55 to 64, $410,000 at 65 to 74 and $335,000 at 75 and over. A good net worth is one that's rising each year and on track for your own goals.

Should I include my home in my net worth?

Yes. Include its realistic market value as an asset and the mortgage balance as a debt; the difference is your home equity. Because you can't easily spend home equity, also look at your liquid net worth, which leaves out the home and retirement accounts.

Should I include my car in my net worth?

Yes, at what it would sell for today, not what you paid. Cars lose value quickly, so update the figure each time you recalculate. Include any car loan as a debt.

Is negative net worth bad?

It's common early in a career, especially with student loans, and isn't a sign of failure. What matters is the direction: paying down debt and building savings each year moves net worth toward zero and then above it.

How often should I calculate my net worth?

Once or twice a year is enough for most people, for example every January and July. Monthly tracking mostly shows market swings. Use the same method each time so the trend is meaningful.

What is the difference between net worth and liquid net worth?

Net worth counts everything. Liquid net worth counts only money you could reach quickly, such as cash and taxable investments, minus consumer debts such as credit cards. It shows how much of a cushion you actually have.
