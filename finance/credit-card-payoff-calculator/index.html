How to use this credit card payoff calculator

Enter the balance, the APR and either the payment you can afford or the number of months you want to be debt-free in. The calculator shows the payoff time and interest instantly, and how both compare with paying only the minimum.

  1. Card balance. Your current balance from your statement or app.
  2. APR. The default is the Federal Reserve’s latest average interest rate on all credit card accounts at commercial banks. Your card’s purchase APR is on your statement.
  3. I want to. Choose Pay a fixed amount to see how long it takes, or Be debt-free by to see the payment you need.
  4. More options. Change the minimum payment rule to match your card; many issuers use interest plus 1% of the balance with a $25 or $35 floor.

The results assume you don’t add new purchases while paying the card down.

Worked example: $6,400 at 22.9% APR

A cardholder owes $6,400 at 22.9% and decides to pay a fixed $250 a month.

  • This month’s interest: $6,400 × 22.9% ÷ 12 = $122.13, almost half the payment
  • Debt-free in: 3 years (36 payments)
  • Total interest: $2,467.58, total paid $8,867.58

If they paid only the minimum (interest plus 1% of the balance, at least $25), it would take 21 years and 5 months and cost $11,125.33 in interest. The fixed $250 plan saves $8,657.75.

The payoff formula in plain English

Each month, interest is added to the balance and your payment is subtracted. Whatever the payment exceeds the interest by is what actually reduces the debt.

  • Monthly interest = balance × APR ÷ 12.
  • Months to pay off at a fixed payment P: n = −ln(1 − r × B ÷ P) ÷ ln(1 + r), rounded up, where r is the monthly rate and B the balance.
  • Payment to be debt-free in n months: P = B × r(1 + r)ⁿ ÷ ((1 + r)ⁿ − 1), the same formula as a loan payment.

If your payment is less than or equal to the monthly interest, the balance never falls; the calculator tells you the minimum needed to make progress. Calcrafty runs the payoff month by month, rounding interest to the cent, so the final payment is exactly what’s left.

Why minimum payments take so long

The minimum payment is designed to keep your account current, not to clear the debt. Because it’s mostly interest plus a small slice of the balance, it shrinks as the balance shrinks, so you pay less and less principal each month and the payoff stretches out for years.

The biggest gain comes from switching to any fixed payment. The first payment on the minimum plan is $90, so a fixed $100 is only $10 more at the start, yet it finishes more than 11 years sooner. Your statement shows the same comparison in its “minimum payment warning” box, which issuers have been required to print since the CARD Act of 2009.

What a missed payment does to the math

A payment that’s 60 days late can trigger a penalty APR, often around 29.99%, on top of a late fee. Under the CARD Act, an issuer must give you 45 days’ notice before raising the rate on an existing balance, and must review it after six months of on-time payments, but the higher rate can still cost a lot in the meantime.

At 29.99% instead of 22.9%, the $6,400 balance from the example takes 42 months to clear at $250 a month instead of 36, and costs $3,941.79 in interest instead of $2,467.58. A payment 30 or more days late can also be reported to the credit bureaus and stay on your report for seven years. Setting up autopay for at least the minimum, then paying extra manually, protects against both.

How card interest is actually charged

Most issuers calculate interest using your average daily balance: they add up the balance at the end of each day in the billing cycle, divide by the number of days, and apply a daily rate (APR ÷ 365) for each day. This calculator uses the simpler monthly rate (APR ÷ 12), which gives very close results for planning.

Two details make a real difference:

  • The grace period. If you pay the full statement balance by the due date, new purchases usually aren’t charged interest at all. Carry any balance, and you typically lose the grace period, so new purchases start accruing interest immediately until you pay in full again.
  • Payment timing. Because interest is figured on the daily balance, paying as soon as you’re paid, or splitting the payment into two halves each month, lowers the average balance slightly and trims the interest.

Should you pay off cards or save first?

Most guidance suggests a small emergency fund first, often around $1,000, so a surprise bill doesn’t go straight back on the card. After that, high-interest card debt is usually the best place for extra money: paying off a 22% balance is a guaranteed 22% return, more than any savings account. Keep contributing enough to retirement to collect any employer match, then send everything else to the cards.

Key terms

  • APR: the annual interest rate on the balance. Cards often have different APRs for purchases, cash advances and balance transfers.
  • Minimum payment: the smallest payment that keeps the account in good standing.
  • Grace period: the time between the end of a billing cycle and the due date when new purchases don’t accrue interest if you paid in full.
  • Balance transfer: moving a balance to another card, often at a promotional rate and with a fee.
  • Credit utilization: your balance ÷ credit limit; paying it down usually helps your credit score.

Balance transfers and other ways to cut the interest

Paying more each month is the most reliable way out, but cutting the rate can speed things up. Here are the main options, with the trade-offs.

A 0% balance transfer card. These typically charge a fee of 3% to 5% of the amount moved and give a promotional 0% period, often 12 to 21 months. Moving the $6,400 balance with a 4% fee ($256) to an 18-month 0% offer means paying $6,656 in total; clearing it before the promotion ends takes $369.78 a month. The same $369.78 a month at 22.9% would take 22 months and cost $1,442.82 in interest, so the transfer saves over $1,100. The catch: any balance left when the promotion ends goes to the card’s regular rate, and new purchases may not get the 0% rate.

A debt consolidation loan. A fixed-rate personal loan replaces card debt with one payment and a set end date. It’s worth it when the APR, including any origination fee, is clearly below your card’s rate. Check the total cost with the loan calculator before signing.

Ask your issuer for a lower rate. Card issuers sometimes reduce the APR for customers with a good payment history, and some offer hardship programs if you’re struggling. A short phone call costs nothing.

Nonprofit credit counseling. Accredited agencies can review your budget and may set up a debt management plan that lowers rates across several cards. Be wary of companies that charge large upfront fees or promise to settle debts for less; the FTC warns about debt relief scams.

Whatever you choose, stop adding to the balance. Moving debt to a new card while the old one fills back up leaves you worse off than before.

Frequently asked questions

How long will it take to pay off my credit card?

It depends on the balance, the APR and your payment. A $6,400 balance at 22.9% APR takes 36 months to clear at $250 a month, with $2,467.58 of interest. Paying only the minimum (interest plus 1% of the balance, at least $25) would take over 21 years and cost $11,125.33 in interest.

How is credit card interest calculated?

Most issuers charge a daily periodic rate (APR ÷ 365) on your average daily balance. This calculator uses the standard monthly approximation, APR ÷ 12 on the balance, which gives results within a few dollars for most balances. If you pay your full statement balance by the due date, you usually pay no interest on purchases at all, thanks to the grace period.

Why does paying the minimum take so long?

Because the minimum shrinks as your balance shrinks. A typical minimum is that month's interest plus 1% of the balance, so you only ever pay down about 1% a month. Since the CARD Act of 2009, statements must show how long minimum payments would take and what they would cost.

How much should I pay to be debt-free in 18 months?

Switch the calculator to "Be debt-free by" and enter 18. For a $6,400 balance at 22.9%, that takes $423.46 a month and costs $1,222.29 in interest. The payment is the standard loan formula applied to your balance and APR.

Is a balance transfer or consolidation loan worth it?

Often, if it cuts your rate a lot and you stop adding to the card. A 0% balance transfer usually charges a 3% to 5% fee and only lasts a promotional period, so divide the balance by the months of the offer to find the payment that clears it in time. A personal loan at a lower fixed rate gives a set end date.

Should I pay off my credit card or save first?

Most guidance suggests keeping a small emergency fund first, so a surprise expense doesn't go back on the card, then putting every spare dollar toward high-interest card debt. Paying off a 22% card is effectively a guaranteed 22% return, higher than most savings or investments.
