A $20,000 loan at 7% for 5 years with $600 in fees has an APR of 8.29% and a monthly payment of $396.02.

What this means

The $600 in fees raises the yearly cost from 7% to an APR of 8.29%. In total you pay $4,361.20 to borrow $19,400: $3,761.20 in interest plus the fees.

If you’ll repay or refinance early, the fees cost more per year than the APR shows, because the APR spreads them over the full term.

Show the math

  1. 1. Amount financed

    loan amount − prepaid fees

    $20,000 − $600 = $19,400.00

  2. 2. Monthly payment at the interest rate

    M = P × r(1 + r)ⁿ ÷ ((1 + r)ⁿ − 1), r = rate ÷ 12

    M = $20,000 × 0.005833(1 + 0.005833)^60 ÷ ((1 + 0.005833)^60 − 1) = $396.02

  3. 3. Finance charge

    payment × n − amount financed

    $396.02 × 60 − $19,400.00 = $4,361.20

  4. 4. APR

    the rate a where amount financed = M × (1 − (1 + a/12)⁻ⁿ) ÷ (a/12), solved numerically

    $19,400.00 = $396.02 × (1 − (1 + a/12)^−60) ÷ (a/12) = 8.29%

How to use this APR calculator

Enter the loan amount, the interest rate and the term, then add the lender’s fees. The APR updates as you type, along with the monthly payment, the amount you actually receive and the total cost of borrowing.

  1. Loan amount. The amount on the loan offer.
  2. Interest rate. The note rate, before fees. It’s the rate used to work out your payment.
  3. Loan term. In months: 36 for three years, 60 for five, 360 for a 30-year mortgage.
  4. Fees and points. The charges the lender requires to give you the loan: origination or underwriting fees and discount points. On mortgages, leave out third-party costs such as the appraisal and title insurance.
  5. How the fees are paid. Most personal loans deduct the fee from the money you receive; choose Upfront or deducted. If the fee is added to your balance, choose Added to the loan.

Offer A has the lower rate and the lower payment, but you receive $750 less and pay $367 more to borrow. Its APR of 13.55% shows that straight away. The APR is the only number that puts both offers on the same footing.

How lenders calculate APR

The method comes from the federal Truth in Lending Act and its Regulation Z, so every lender in the US uses the same one.

  1. Amount financed = loan amount − prepaid finance charges. If fees are added to the balance instead, the amount financed is the loan amount and the payment is worked out on the larger balance.
  2. Payment = the standard loan payment at the interest rate: M = P × r(1 + r)ⁿ ÷ ((1 + r)ⁿ − 1), where r is the rate ÷ 12.
  3. APR = the yearly rate at which the present value of all the payments equals the amount financed. There’s no direct formula for it, so it’s found numerically, the same way a spreadsheet’s RATE function works.

The Show the math panel above runs through all three steps with your numbers. Regulation Z lets a lender’s disclosed APR be off by at most 1/8 of a percentage point on a regular loan, which is why APRs are usually quoted to two decimals.

Why the term changes the APR

Fees are paid once, but the APR spreads them over the whole loan. The shorter the loan, the more each year has to carry. Here’s a $20,000 loan at 7% with the same $600 fee:

Term Monthly payment APR
12 months $1,730.53 12.76%
24 months $895.45 10.03%
36 months $617.54 9.07%
60 months $396.02 8.29%
84 months $301.85 7.95%

When APR misleads: points on a mortgage

APR assumes you keep the loan to the end. Most people sell or refinance a mortgage long before 30 years, and that’s where APR can point the wrong way.

Compare two $300,000, 30-year mortgages:

  • With points: 6.5% plus $6,000 in points and fees. Payment $1,896.20, APR 6.70%.
  • Without points: 6.75% with no points. Payment $1,945.79, APR 6.75%.

Over 30 years, paying points wins. But if you sell or refinance after five years, the $6,000 is spread over only 60 payments, and the real yearly cost of the first loan is about 6.99%, more than the 6.75% loan without points. After three years it’s about 7.26%. Before paying points, work out how many months of lower payments it takes to earn the fee back, and be honest about how long you’ll keep the loan.

APR on different kinds of credit

The Truth in Lending Act requires an APR on almost every kind of consumer credit, but what goes into it varies:

  • Mortgages. The APR includes the interest rate plus points, origination and other lender charges, and mortgage insurance. It appears on page 3 of the Loan Estimate, and the “APR” on a mortgage advertisement must be calculated the same way.
  • Personal loans. Usually the rate plus any origination fee. Many online lenders deduct the fee from the money you receive, which is the Upfront or deducted option above.
  • Auto loans. The rate plus required finance charges. Optional add-ons such as extended warranties or GAP coverage are generally not part of the APR, but rolling them into the loan still raises the total you repay.
  • Credit cards. The APR is simply the interest rate on balances you carry. Annual fees and balance transfer fees aren’t built into it, and most cards have several APRs: purchases, balance transfers, cash advances and a penalty rate.
  • Payday and other short-term loans. Because the fee is spread over just a couple of weeks, the APR is enormous. A $15 fee on a $100 two-week loan works out to an APR of about 391%.

APR vs APY

APR and APY measure different things. APR is the yearly rate without compounding within the year. APY (annual percentage yield) includes compounding and is used mainly for savings. On a balance that compounds monthly, the effective yearly cost is higher than the APR:

APR Effective yearly rate with monthly compounding
6% 6.17%
12% 12.68%
18% 19.56%
24% 26.82%
29.99% 34.48%

On an installment loan you pay down every month, the APR on your disclosure is the right number for comparing offers. On a credit card balance that you let grow, the effective rate is higher, which is why unpaid card debt grows so quickly.

How to compare loan offers step by step

  1. Get offers for the same amount and term. An APR on a 36-month loan isn’t comparable with one on a 60-month loan, because fees are spread differently.
  2. Read the disclosure, not the ad. For mortgages, use page 3 of each Loan Estimate; for other loans, the Truth in Lending box shows the APR, finance charge and amount financed.
  3. Enter each offer here and save it with Add to compare to see the APR, payment and finance charge side by side.
  4. Check how long you’ll keep the loan. If you might repay early, favor lower fees over a slightly lower rate.
  5. Look for anything the APR leaves out: prepayment penalties, variable rates, and optional products added to the balance.

How your credit affects the APR you’re offered

Lenders price loans mainly on credit score, income, existing debt and the loan’s term, so two borrowers can be quoted very different APRs for the same loan. Rate shopping is how you find out where you stand. Many lenders prequalify you with a soft credit check that doesn’t affect your score. For mortgages, auto loans and student loans, credit scoring models count several hard inquiries for the same type of loan within a short window (typically 14 to 45 days, depending on the model) as a single inquiry, so comparing several lenders in the same couple of weeks costs little.

What APR doesn’t tell you

APR is the best single number for comparing loans, but it has limits. It assumes a fixed rate held to the end, so on an adjustable-rate mortgage it reflects only the starting terms. It says nothing about the total amount you’ll repay, which depends on the term as much as the rate: a 7-year loan can have a lower APR than a 3-year loan and still cost far more in interest. And it excludes some real costs, such as third-party closing costs on a mortgage and late fees. Use the APR to compare, and the finance charge and total of payments to decide whether the loan is worth it.

Key terms

  • APR (annual percentage rate): the yearly cost of credit including required fees, calculated under Regulation Z.
  • Note rate: the interest rate in the loan contract, used to calculate the payment.
  • Amount financed: the credit you actually receive: the loan amount minus prepaid finance charges.
  • Finance charge: everything the credit costs you: all interest plus the fees counted in the APR.
  • Discount points: an upfront payment to lower the interest rate, usually 1% of the loan per point.
  • Origination fee: a lender’s charge for processing the loan, common on personal loans (often 1% to 10%).

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between APR and interest rate?

The interest rate is the cost of borrowing the money itself. The APR adds the lender's required fees, such as origination fees and points, and spreads them over the loan term as a yearly rate. With no fees, the two are the same; the bigger the fees, the further the APR rises above the rate.

How is APR calculated?

Lenders follow Regulation Z: the APR is the rate at which the present value of your payments equals the amount financed, which is the loan amount minus prepaid fees. On $20,000 at 7% for 60 months with a $600 fee, the payment is $396.02 and you really receive $19,400, which works out to an APR of 8.29%.

Which fees are included in APR?

Finance charges the lender requires as a condition of the loan: origination and underwriting fees, discount points, mortgage broker fees and prepaid interest, plus mortgage insurance on home loans. Third-party costs you'd pay anyway, such as appraisal, title insurance, credit reports and government recording fees, are generally excluded for mortgages.

Is a lower APR always better?

Not always. APR assumes you keep the loan for its full term. Fees are paid once, so if you repay or refinance early, they cost more per year than the APR shows. A mortgage with points can have the lower APR over 30 years but cost more if you sell after five.

Why does a short loan with fees have such a high APR?

The same fee is spread over fewer months. A $600 fee on a $20,000 loan at 7% gives an APR of 12.76% over 12 months but 8.29% over 60 months. That's why APR matters most when comparing short personal loans and cash advances.

Is APR the same as APY?

No. APR is used for loans and doesn't include compounding within the year. APY (annual percentage yield) is used for savings and does include compounding, so a savings account paying 5% compounded monthly has an APY of about 5.12%.
