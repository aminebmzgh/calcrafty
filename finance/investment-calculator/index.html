What this means

You’d put in $202,182 and growth would add $260,437, for $462,619 after 25 years if returns average 6% a year.

With 3.72% inflation, that balance would buy what $185,636 buys today.

More than half the final balance (56%) is growth rather than money you put in: time does most of the work.

Raising your contribution 2% a year takes it from $500 to $804 a month by the final year.

    contributions + growth

How to use this investment calculator

Enter a starting amount, a monthly contribution, an expected return and a time frame. The projected balance, the share that comes from growth and the value in today’s dollars all update as you type.

  1. Starting amount. What’s already invested, or 0.
  2. Monthly contribution. What you’ll add each month, for example through a 401(k) or an automatic transfer to a brokerage account.
  3. Expected annual return. Use an average you’re comfortable planning with, after fund fees.
  4. Years to grow. How long until you need the money.
  5. More options. Raise contributions each year to match pay raises, and set inflation. The inflation default is the latest 12-month change in the Consumer Price Index from the Bureau of Labor Statistics.

Worked example: $15,000 plus $400 a month for 25 years

A 35-year-old has $15,000 invested, adds $400 a month, raises the contribution 3% each year and plans with a 6.5% average annual return and 3% inflation.

  • Projected value at 60: $472,116.99
  • Total contributed: $190,004.47
  • Investment growth: $282,112.52, which is 59.8% of the final balance
  • Monthly contribution in year 25: $813.12
  • In today’s dollars: $225,485.70

Three what-ifs show what drives the result:

Change Projected value
As planned $472,116.99
No yearly increase in contributions $375,377.58
Returns average 4.5% instead of 6.5% $347,368.51
Invest for 15 years instead of 25 $184,965.19

Cutting the time in half cuts the result by more than half, because the last years of compounding add the most dollars.

How the projection is calculated in plain English

Each month, the balance earns one month of return and then the contribution is added:

balance = balance × (1 + r ÷ 12) + contribution

At the start of each new year, the contribution rises by the percentage you chose. After the final month, the projected balance is converted to today’s dollars by dividing by (1 + inflation)^years. With no contributions this reduces to the classic compound growth formula, A = P(1 + r ÷ 12)^(12t).

The projection uses a steady average return. Real markets don’t move in a straight line, so treat the result as a planning estimate, and test lower returns too.

A common guideline for retirement is to save 15% of your pre-tax income, including any employer match. If that feels out of reach, start with enough to get the full match and raise the rate by one percentage point each year, ideally when you get a raise, so take-home pay never goes down. The Raise contributions each year option under More options models exactly that.

Lump sum or monthly investing?

If you have a large amount to invest at once, such as an inheritance, a bonus or the sale of a home, you can invest it all now or spread it out over several months, which is called dollar-cost averaging.

Because markets have risen more often than they’ve fallen over long periods, investing a lump sum right away has historically come out ahead more often than spreading it out. Vanguard’s research on US, UK and Australian markets found lump-sum investing beat a 12-month phase-in about two-thirds of the time. Spreading it out mainly helps emotionally: it reduces the regret of investing everything just before a drop.

As an example, $50,000 invested once and left for 20 years at 6% grows to $165,510.22, or about $91,639 in today’s dollars after 3% inflation. Contributions you make every month from your paycheck are dollar-cost averaging by default, which is one reason automatic investing works so well.

Taxes on investment growth

This calculator shows growth before tax. How much you keep depends on the type of account:

  • Traditional 401(k) and IRA: contributions are usually pre-tax and growth isn’t taxed along the way, but withdrawals in retirement are taxed as ordinary income.
  • Roth 401(k) and Roth IRA: contributions are made with after-tax money, and qualified withdrawals, including all the growth, are tax-free.
  • HSA: for those with an eligible health plan, contributions are pre-tax and withdrawals for medical costs are tax-free, the only account with both breaks.
  • Taxable brokerage account: dividends are taxed each year, and selling at a gain triggers capital gains tax. Gains on investments held more than a year are taxed at 0%, 15% or 20% depending on income, lower than the rates on wages.

Because tax drag slows compounding, filling tax-advantaged accounts first usually leaves you with more, especially over decades.

Choosing a return to plan with

The expected return is the input that moves the result most, and the one nobody knows in advance. Some guidance for choosing it:

  • Stocks have historically returned much more than bonds or cash over long periods, but with deep drops along the way. The broad US stock market has averaged roughly 10% a year before inflation over the past century, with individual years ranging from large losses to large gains.
  • A mix of stocks and bonds returns less but swings less. Many planners use 5% to 7% a year for a diversified long-term portfolio.
  • Cash and CDs are steadier still; use the current savings rate rather than a stock-market return for money you’ll need soon.
  • Subtract fees. Enter the return after fund expenses and advisory fees.

When in doubt, run the plan at a cautious return and treat anything above it as a bonus.

Key terms

  • Rate of return: the yearly percentage gain on an investment, including dividends reinvested.
  • Compounding: growth earning further growth.
  • Real vs nominal value: nominal is the future dollar amount; real is what it’s worth in today’s prices.
  • Expense ratio: a fund’s yearly fee as a percentage of your balance; it comes straight out of returns.
  • Dollar-cost averaging: investing a fixed amount on a schedule regardless of market prices.

How fees and returns change the result

Small differences in yearly return compound into large differences in the final balance. That’s true whether the difference comes from markets or from costs you control, such as fund fees.

Using the same plan as the example ($15,000 to start, $400 a month rising 3% a year, 25 years), here’s what happens as the net return falls:

Net annual return Value after 25 years
6.5% $472,116.99
6.0% $436,318.05
5.5% $403,804.84

A fund charging 0.5% a year more than a cheaper alternative effectively lowers your return by about that much. Here it costs roughly $35,800 over 25 years, about 8% of the final balance. A 1% difference costs more than $68,000.

Ways to keep more of your return:

  • Check expense ratios. Every fund lists its expense ratio. Broad index funds often charge a small fraction of a percent; some actively managed funds and advisory wrappers charge 1% or more a year.
  • Use tax-advantaged accounts first. A 401(k), IRA or HSA lets growth compound without yearly taxes. In a taxable account, dividends and realized gains are taxed along the way, which slows compounding.
  • Diversify. Spreading money across many companies, sectors and countries reduces the chance that one bad investment derails your plan.
  • Match risk to your timeline. Money needed within a few years usually belongs in safer assets; long-term money can ride out market swings.
  • Avoid selling in a panic. Markets have historically recovered from downturns over long periods, but investors who sell after a drop lock in the loss and often miss the recovery.

Rebalance occasionally. Over time, the parts of a portfolio that grow fastest take up a bigger share and change its risk. Many investors rebalance once a year, or use target-date funds that do it automatically, to keep the mix they chose.

What to do with your result

If the projection is meant for retirement, test it with the retirement calculator, which adds your age, income needs and withdrawals. To find the monthly amount needed for a specific target, use the savings goal calculator. For money you’ll need within a few years, a savings account or CD is usually more suitable; model it with the compound interest calculator.

Raising contributions with each raise is one of the easiest wins. Check what a 1% bump costs per paycheck with the paycheck calculator; because 401(k) contributions are pre-tax, it costs less take-home pay than you’d expect.

Frequently asked questions

What rate of return should I use?

It depends on what you invest in and for how long. Diversified stock portfolios have historically earned more than bonds or savings over long periods, but with large swings from year to year, and past returns don't guarantee future ones. Many people plan with a conservative average and test a lower one too; the compare feature makes that easy.

Why does the calculator show a value in today's dollars?

Because prices rise. A balance of $472,117 in 25 years won't buy what $472,117 buys today. At 3% average inflation it has the buying power of about $225,486 now. Planning in today's dollars keeps goals like retirement income realistic.

How much difference does raising my contributions make?

A lot over long periods. Investing $400 a month at 6.5% for 25 years grows to about $375,378 with flat contributions. Raising the contribution 3% a year, roughly in line with typical pay raises, grows it to about $472,117, around $97,000 more.

Does this calculator include taxes and fees?

No. Enter a return that's already net of fund fees, such as an expense ratio, if you want them reflected. Taxes depend on the account type. Growth in a 401(k) or traditional IRA is taxed when withdrawn, Roth accounts can be tax-free, and taxable brokerage accounts owe tax on dividends and gains as they happen.

Is it better to invest a lump sum or monthly?

Research on historical markets has found that investing a lump sum right away has more often come out ahead, because markets have risen more often than they've fallen. Investing monthly (dollar-cost averaging) reduces the regret of bad timing and is how most people invest anyway, straight from each paycheck.

How are returns compounded in this calculator?

The annual return is applied as a nominal rate compounded monthly, and contributions are added at the end of each month. The result is an estimate that assumes the same average return every year; real portfolios will end higher or lower depending on the order of good and bad years.
