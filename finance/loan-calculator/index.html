How to use this loan calculator

Enter the amount, the APR and the term, and the monthly payment appears as you type. The calculator works for personal loans, student loans, business loans and any other fixed-rate installment loan.

  1. Loan amount. The amount you’ll owe at the start, including any fees rolled into the loan.
  2. Interest rate (APR). The default is the Federal Reserve’s latest average for 24-month personal loans at commercial banks. Replace it with the APR from your offer.
  3. Loan term. Use the Years / Months toggle; the calculator converts the value when you switch.
  4. Extra payment. Under More options, add a fixed amount to pay toward principal every month.

Save two or three versions with Add to compare to see how term length changes both the payment and the total cost.

Worked example: $18,500 over 4 years at 10.5%

Say you borrow $18,500 to consolidate credit card balances, at 10.5% APR for 4 years (48 payments). The calculator shows:

  • Monthly payment: $473.66
  • Total interest: $4,235.81, which is 22.9% of the amount borrowed
  • Total repaid: $22,735.81

The first payment is $161.88 in interest ($18,500 × 0.105 ÷ 12) and $311.78 in principal, leaving a balance of $18,188.22. By the last year, almost all of each payment goes to principal.

Changing the term shows the trade-off clearly:

Term Monthly payment Total interest
3 years $601.30 $3,146.58
4 years $473.66 $4,235.81
5 years $397.64 $5,358.18

Keeping the 4-year loan but paying an extra $75 a month pays it off in 41 months and saves $717.83 in interest.

The loan payment formula in plain English

Your payment is the fixed amount that, paid every month, covers that month’s interest and slowly pays down the balance until it reaches exactly zero on the final payment.

M = P × r(1 + r)ⁿ ÷ ((1 + r)ⁿ − 1)

  • P is the loan amount.
  • r is the monthly rate: APR ÷ 12. At 10.5%, r = 0.00875.
  • n is the number of monthly payments.

Each month, interest = balance × r, and the rest of the payment reduces the balance. Calcrafty rounds each month’s interest to the cent, as lenders do, and adjusts the final payment so the schedule ends at exactly $0.00. If the rate is 0%, the payment is simply P ÷ n.

Which loans this calculator works for

Any loan with a fixed rate and equal monthly payments, which covers most consumer borrowing:

  • Personal loans for debt consolidation, home improvements, medical bills or major purchases, usually 2 to 7 years.
  • Student loans, both federal (on the standard 10-year plan) and private fixed-rate loans.
  • Small business term loans and equipment loans with a fixed rate.
  • Home equity loans, which are paid out as a lump sum with fixed payments.

It isn’t built for revolving credit. Credit cards and home equity lines of credit have no fixed payment schedule; use the credit card payoff calculator for cards. For adjustable-rate loans, the result only holds until the rate changes.

Secured vs unsecured loans

A secured loan is backed by collateral, such as a car, savings account or home, that the lender can take if you stop paying. An unsecured loan relies only on your credit and income. Because the lender takes less risk, secured loans usually have lower rates.

On $30,000 over 5 years, an unsecured loan at 12% costs $667.33 a month and $10,040.08 in interest. A secured loan at 8.5% costs $615.50 a month and $6,929.69 in interest, about $3,110 less. The trade-off is that you could lose the collateral. Only secure a loan with something you can afford to risk, and be especially careful about turning unsecured debt like credit cards into debt secured by your home.

What you save by paying a loan off early

Interest is charged on the balance you still owe, so paying a loan off early saves all the interest that would have built up on the remaining balance. Most personal loans have no prepayment penalty, but check your agreement before paying extra.

In the $18,500 example, after 24 of the 48 payments you’ve paid $3,081.42 in interest and reduced the balance to $10,213.58. Paying that balance off in full at that point saves the remaining $1,154.39 of interest. Notice that halfway through the term, only about 45% of the principal has been repaid ($8,286.42), because early payments carry more interest.

Smaller steps help too. The Extra payment field under More options shows how a regular extra amount shortens the loan, and rounding a $473.66 payment up to $500 is an easy way to start.

Fixed vs variable rate loans

A fixed-rate loan keeps the same rate and payment for the whole term, so the schedule this calculator shows is exactly what you’ll pay. A variable-rate loan is tied to a benchmark rate and can change, usually once a month, quarter or year. Variable loans often start lower, which makes the first payments look attractive.

The risk is on the upside. If the benchmark rises two percentage points, a variable loan at 8.5% becomes 10.5%, and both the payment and the total interest go up for the rest of the term. Some loans cap how far the rate can rise; many private student loans and lines of credit don’t.

A variable rate can make sense for a short loan you expect to pay off quickly, or when you have room in your budget for a higher payment. For a long loan, or a tight budget, the certainty of a fixed rate is usually worth a slightly higher starting rate. To see the effect, run the loan twice: once at today’s rate and once at a rate two or three points higher.

Red flags in a loan offer

Before you sign, read the Truth in Lending disclosure and watch for these:

  • An APR far above the rate. A large gap means high fees. The APR calculator shows exactly how much they add.
  • Fees before you get the money. A legitimate lender deducts origination fees from the loan; one that asks you to pay a fee upfront by gift card or wire transfer is almost certainly a scam.
  • Add-ons you didn’t ask for. Credit life or disability insurance is optional and can usually be declined.
  • A balloon payment. Some loans have low monthly payments and a large lump sum at the end. Make sure the payment schedule ends at zero.
  • Pressure to borrow more than you need. Every extra dollar costs interest for the whole term.

Key terms

  • Principal: the amount you borrowed and still owe.
  • APR: the annual percentage rate, including required fees; required on every US loan disclosure under the Truth in Lending Act.
  • Term: how long you have to repay the loan.
  • Amortization: paying off a loan in equal installments that cover interest first and principal after.
  • Prepayment penalty: a fee some lenders charge for paying a loan off early. Most personal loans don’t have one, but check your agreement.
  • Origination fee: an upfront fee, often 1% to 8% of the loan, usually deducted from the money you receive.

How your credit score changes the cost of a loan

Lenders set personal loan rates mainly by credit score, income, existing debts and the loan term. The difference between a good and a fair credit profile can be thousands of dollars on the same loan.

Here’s the same $18,500, 4-year loan at a range of APRs:

APR Monthly payment Total interest
9.5% $464.78 $3,809.34
10.5% $473.66 $4,235.81
13.5% $500.91 $5,543.77
18% $543.44 $7,584.97

Moving from 18% to 9.5% saves about $3,800 in interest and $79 a month. Ways to get a lower rate:

  • Check your credit reports first. You can get them free from each of the three nationwide credit bureaus at AnnualCreditReport.com. Disputing errors before you apply can raise your score.
  • Prequalify with several lenders. Many banks, credit unions and online lenders show estimated rates with a soft credit check that doesn’t affect your score. Formal applications come later.
  • Compare APRs, not just rates. An origination fee raises the APR and reduces the cash you receive. A loan at 10% with a 5% fee can cost more than one at 11.5% with no fee.
  • Consider a credit union. Federal credit unions are limited to an 18% APR on most loans, and many offer lower rates to members.
  • Borrow only what you need. Every extra $1,000 at 10.5% over 4 years adds about $229 in interest.
  • Add a co-borrower carefully. A co-signer with stronger credit can lower the rate, but they’re equally responsible for the debt.

Frequently asked questions

How is a monthly loan payment calculated?

A fixed-rate loan uses the amortization formula M = P × r(1+r)^n ÷ ((1+r)^n − 1), where P is the amount borrowed, r is the monthly rate (APR ÷ 12) and n is the number of payments. It produces the one level payment that covers each month's interest and pays the balance to exactly zero on the last payment.

What is a good interest rate on a personal loan?

It depends mostly on your credit score and income. The Federal Reserve's survey of commercial banks puts the average 24-month personal loan rate near 12%, which this calculator uses as its default. Borrowers with excellent credit often see single-digit APRs, while fair or poor credit can mean 20% to 36%. Compare at least three offers on APR, not just the monthly payment.

Is a shorter or longer loan term better?

A shorter term costs less overall but has a higher payment. Borrowing $18,500 at 10.5% costs $601.30 a month over 3 years ($3,146.58 interest) or $397.64 a month over 5 years ($5,358.18 interest). Pick the shortest term whose payment fits comfortably in your budget.

Do extra payments really save money on a loan?

Yes, as long as your lender applies them to principal and doesn't charge a prepayment penalty. Every extra dollar reduces the balance that interest is charged on. On an $18,500 loan at 10.5% over 4 years, adding $75 a month saves $717.83 and ends the loan 7 months early.

What's the difference between the interest rate and APR?

The interest rate is what's charged on the balance. The APR also includes required fees, such as an origination fee, spread over the loan, so it's the better number for comparing offers. If your loan has an origination fee taken out of the proceeds, use the APR here and remember you'll receive less than the loan amount.

What is an EMI?

EMI stands for equated monthly installment, the term used in India and some other countries for a fixed monthly loan payment. It's calculated with exactly the same formula as a US monthly loan payment, so this calculator works as an EMI calculator too; just enter amounts in your own currency and ignore the dollar sign.
