How to use this mortgage calculator

Enter the price, your down payment and a rate, and the monthly payment updates as you type. Everything else is optional, but adding property tax and insurance turns a rough estimate into the number you’ll actually pay each month.

  1. Home price and down payment. Switch the down payment between % and $ with the toggle; the calculator converts the value for you.
  2. Interest rate. It starts at this week’s national average for a 30-year fixed loan. If a lender has quoted you a rate, use that instead.
  3. Loan term. 30 years is the most common choice; 15 years costs much less interest but raises the payment.
  4. Property tax and insurance. These are usually collected by your lender in an escrow account and paid on your behalf, so they belong in the monthly total.
  5. More options. Add PMI if you’re putting down less than 20%, HOA dues if the home has them, and any extra principal you plan to pay each month.

Use Add to compare to save up to three scenarios, such as 10% versus 20% down, and see them side by side. Copy link creates a URL with your numbers in it, so you can send the exact scenario to a partner or a loan officer.

Worked example: a $420,000 home with 10% down

A buyer puts 10% down ($42,000) on a $420,000 house, borrows $378,000 at 7% for 30 years, pays 1.1% a year in property tax and has a $2,100 insurance quote. Here is what the calculator shows:

Part of the payment Monthly amount
Principal and interest $2,514.84
Property tax ($420,000 × 1.1% ÷ 12) $385.00
Home insurance ($2,100 ÷ 12) $175.00
PMI ($378,000 × 0.55% ÷ 12) $173.25
Total $3,248.09

In the first month, $2,205 of the $2,514.84 goes to interest and only $309.84 reduces the balance. That’s normal: interest is charged on what you still owe, so early payments are mostly interest. PMI drops off after 115 payments (just under 10 years), when the balance falls to $327,600, which is 78% of the original price. Over the life of the loan PMI costs $19,923.75.

Two changes make a large difference here. Putting 20% down instead ($84,000) cuts the loan to $336,000, removes PMI and brings the total payment to $2,795.42. Keeping 10% down but adding $250 a month to principal saves about $146,163 in interest, pays the loan off in 22 years and 11 months, and ends PMI about three and a half years sooner.

The mortgage formula in plain English

Your principal and interest payment is the fixed amount that, paid every month, brings the balance to exactly zero on the last payment. The formula is:

M = P × r(1 + r)ⁿ ÷ ((1 + r)ⁿ − 1)

  • P is the amount you borrow (price minus down payment).
  • r is the monthly interest rate: the annual rate divided by 12. At 7%, r = 0.07 ÷ 12 = 0.005833.
  • n is the number of payments: 360 for a 30-year loan.

Each month, interest is the balance times r. The rest of the payment reduces the balance, which lowers next month’s interest, so slightly more goes to principal every month. The Show the math panel above substitutes your own numbers into each step. Calcrafty does this arithmetic in exact decimal, rounding interest to the cent each month as lenders do, so the schedule ends at exactly $0.00.

15-year vs 30-year mortgage

A 15-year mortgage has a much higher payment but costs far less in total, because you borrow for half as long and lenders usually offer a lower rate on shorter loans. On the same $378,000 loan from the example:

Term Rate Principal and interest Total interest
30 years 7.0% $2,514.84 $527,346.44
15 years 6.4% $3,272.04 $210,967.70

The 15-year loan costs $757.20 more a month but saves about $316,379 in interest, and you own the home outright at the halfway point of the 30-year schedule. Even at the same 7% rate, the 15-year loan would save $293,784.

A 30-year loan with extra payments sits in between. You keep the lower required payment, which protects you if money gets tight, and choose how much extra to pay. Adding one-twelfth of a payment each month ($209.57 here), which works out to one extra payment a year, pays the 30-year loan off in 23 years and 9 months and saves about $129,056 in interest. The Extra monthly payment field under More options shows the effect for your numbers.

How your payment splits between interest and principal over time

Your principal and interest payment stays the same for 30 years, but what it pays for changes completely. Early on, most of it is interest; by the end, almost all of it pays down the balance.

That’s why selling or refinancing after a few years leaves most of the loan still owed, and why extra payments early in the loan save the most interest. Ways to lower your monthly payment

If the payment is more than you’re comfortable with, these levers make the biggest difference, roughly in order:

  1. A bigger down payment. Going from 10% to 15% down on the $420,000 home cuts the loan to $357,000 and the principal and interest to $2,375.13, about $140 less a month, and reduces PMI. At 20% down, PMI disappears entirely.
  2. A lower rate. Compare at least three lenders. Improving your credit score before you apply can move you into a better pricing tier.
  3. Discount points. Paying 1 point ($3,780) to cut the rate from 7% to 6.75% lowers the payment by $63.14 a month. It takes about 60 months to earn the cost back, so points only pay off if you keep the loan more than five years.
  4. Lower insurance and taxes. Shop homeowners insurance every year, and apply for any homestead exemption your state offers; it can cut the property tax on a primary residence.
  5. Removing PMI early. Once your balance reaches 80% of the original value, you can ask the lender to cancel PMI. If your home has risen in value, a new appraisal may get you there sooner.

A longer term also lowers the payment, but a 40-year loan or an interest-only period adds a lot of interest, so treat it as a last resort.

Common mistakes when estimating a mortgage payment

  • Looking only at principal and interest. Property tax, insurance, PMI and HOA dues can add a third or more to the payment. In the example, they add $733.25 a month.
  • Using last year’s property tax. Many areas reassess a home when it sells, so the new owner’s tax bill can be much higher than the seller’s. Check the local rate and apply it to your purchase price.
  • Forgetting closing costs. Expect roughly 2% to 5% of the loan amount in fees at closing, on top of the down payment.
  • Comparing rates without the fees. A lower rate with high points isn’t always cheaper. Compare the APR on each Loan Estimate, or use the APR calculator.
  • Budgeting to the lender’s maximum. Approval is based on gross income and ignores childcare, savings goals and repairs. Leave room for maintenance, often estimated at 1% to 2% of the home’s value a year.

Key terms

  • Principal: the amount you borrowed and still owe.
  • APR vs. interest rate: the interest rate sets your payment. APR also includes fees and points, so it’s useful for comparing lenders but isn’t used to calculate the payment.
  • Escrow: an account your lender uses to collect and pay your property tax and insurance.
  • PMI (private mortgage insurance): insurance that protects the lender on conventional loans with less than 20% down. FHA loans charge a different mortgage insurance premium (MIP) with its own rules.
  • LTV (loan-to-value): the loan amount divided by the home’s value. 90% LTV means 10% down.
  • Amortization: paying a loan off with level payments over a set term.

What to do with your result

Start with affordability, not the maximum a lender will approve. A common guideline is to keep your total housing payment under about 28% of your gross monthly income. Check where you stand with the mortgage affordability calculator, and if you’re unsure whether to buy at all, run the same numbers through the rent vs. buy calculator.

Then shop the rate. Even 0.5 percentage points matters: on a $378,000 loan, 6.5% instead of 7% lowers the payment from $2,514.84 to $2,389.22, a saving of about $125 a month for 30 years. Get quotes from at least three lenders on the same day, and compare the Loan Estimates they’re required to give you.

Finally, plan your down payment. Use the savings goal calculator to see how long it takes to reach 20% and avoid PMI, and the loan calculator for any other debt you’re weighing against the purchase. The paycheck calculator shows your take-home pay, which is what the payment actually comes out of.

Frequently asked questions

How is a mortgage payment calculated?

The principal and interest part uses the standard amortization formula, M = P × r(1+r)^n ÷ ((1+r)^n − 1), where P is the loan amount, r is the annual rate divided by 12, and n is the number of monthly payments. Property tax, homeowners insurance, PMI and HOA dues are then added on top to get the full monthly payment.

What is included in a monthly mortgage payment?

Most payments include four parts, often called PITI: principal, interest, property taxes and homeowners insurance. If you put less than 20% down on a conventional loan you'll usually also pay private mortgage insurance (PMI), and condos or planned communities add HOA dues, which you pay to the association rather than the lender.

When does PMI go away?

Under the Homeowners Protection Act, lenders must cancel PMI automatically when your balance is scheduled to reach 78% of the home's original value, provided you're current on payments. You can ask to cancel it earlier, once the balance reaches 80%. This calculator removes PMI at 78%, which is where it stops without you doing anything.

How much does an extra payment save on a mortgage?

Every extra dollar goes straight to principal, so it stops charging interest for the rest of the loan. On a $378,000 loan at 7% for 30 years, an extra $250 a month saves about $146,000 in interest and pays the loan off 7 years and 1 month early. Add your own amount under More options to see the effect.

Is a 15-year or 30-year mortgage better?

A 15-year loan has a higher monthly payment but costs far less in total. On a $378,000 loan at 7%, the 15-year payment is $3,397.57 versus $2,514.84, but total interest drops from about $527,000 to about $234,000. In practice 15-year rates are usually lower too, which widens the gap. A 30-year loan keeps the payment lower and leaves more room in your budget.

Why is the default interest rate different from my quote?

The default is the national weekly average for a 30-year fixed loan from Freddie Mac's Primary Mortgage Market Survey. Your rate depends on your credit score, down payment, loan type, points and lender, so it can be higher or lower. Type your quoted rate over the default for an accurate payment.
