What this means

In 32 years your savings could grow to $1,207,681, worth about $375,277 in today’s money.

After Social Security, your savings need to provide $36,000 a year in today’s dollars, rising with inflation, for 25 years. That takes a nest egg of $2,651,130 at 67.

To close the gap, save $1,996.85 a month instead of $750, retire later, or plan on less income. At the current pace the money runs out around age 77.

Your first-year withdrawal would be 9.6% of your savings; the widely cited 4% guideline is a common benchmark for a sustainable rate.

How to use this retirement calculator

Enter your age, your savings and what you put away each month, then the income you want in retirement. The calculator projects your savings, works out the nest egg you need, and tells you whether you’re on track.

  1. Ages. Your current age and the age you want to stop working.
  2. Savings and contributions. Add up 401(k), 403(b), IRA and other retirement accounts. Include your employer’s match in the monthly contribution.
  3. Income you want. A yearly amount in today’s dollars. Many planners start from 70% to 80% of your pre-retirement income.
  4. Social Security. Your estimated monthly benefit in today’s dollars, from your statement at ssa.gov.
  5. More options. Returns before and during retirement, the age to plan to, and inflation. The inflation default is the latest 12-month change in the Consumer Price Index.

Worked example: a 40-year-old with $85,000 saved

A 40-year-old has $85,000 in a 401(k), saves $900 a month including the employer match and wants to retire at 67 with $70,000 a year in today’s dollars. Their Social Security estimate is $2,300 a month. They plan to age 92 with a 6% return before retirement, 4.5% during, and 2.5% inflation.

  • Savings at 67: $1,153,674.61, worth $592,296.23 in today’s dollars
  • Yearly gap savings must cover: $70,000 − ($2,300 × 12) = $42,400 in today’s dollars
  • Nest egg needed at 67: $1,653,291.99 (about $848,800 today)
  • Shortfall: $499,617.38. At this pace, savings run out around age 83.
  • Monthly saving needed instead of $900: $1,519.46

Retiring at 70 instead changes the picture: savings grow to $1,415,982.43, the shortfall falls to $193,339.56, and the monthly amount needed drops to $1,092.48. Small changes in timing and savings rate add up because they compound for decades.

How the calculation works in plain English

There are three steps.

  1. Project your savings. Today’s balance and your monthly contributions grow at the pre-retirement return, compounded monthly, until your retirement age.
  2. Work out the nest egg you need. Your yearly gap (income wanted minus Social Security) is inflated to retirement-year dollars. Savings must pay that amount at the start of each year, rising with inflation, until the age you plan to. The nest egg needed is the present value of those withdrawals at your retirement return.
  3. Compare. If savings fall short, the calculator solves for the level monthly contribution that would grow to the nest egg, and simulates withdrawals year by year to show when the money would run out.

All returns are steady averages. Real markets vary, and a bad sequence of returns early in retirement can shorten how long savings last, so build in a margin.

Key terms

  • Nest egg: the savings you need on the day you retire.
  • Withdrawal rate: your first year’s withdrawal as a percentage of savings.
  • Sequence-of-returns risk: the danger of poor market returns early in retirement, when withdrawals hurt most.
  • Real (today’s) dollars: future amounts adjusted for inflation to today’s buying power.
  • Employer match: money your employer adds to your 401(k) when you contribute; part of your total savings rate.

The cost of waiting to start

Every year you wait, the monthly amount needed to reach the same goal rises, because each dollar has less time to grow. Using the example’s retirement goal ($70,000 a year of income from age 67, $2,300 a month of Social Security, 6% returns before retirement, 4.5% after and 2.5% inflation), here’s what someone starting from zero would need to save each month:

Waiting from 30 to 40 raises the monthly amount by about 58%; waiting until 50 nearly triples it. If you’re starting late, the levers that matter most are saving more now, working a few years longer, using catch-up contributions after 50, and delaying Social Security, which increases your monthly benefit for each year you wait up to age 70.

Catch-up contributions help late starters. In 2026, workers aged 50 and older can add $8,000 a year to a 401(k) on top of the $24,500 limit, and those aged 60 to 63 can add $11,250 instead. IRA savers aged 50 and older can add $1,100 on top of the $7,500 limit.

When you claim matters. For anyone born in 1960 or later, full retirement age is 67. Claiming at 62 permanently reduces the monthly benefit to 70% of the full amount; waiting until 70 raises it to 124%. The 4% rule and how much you need

A widely quoted shortcut, the “4% rule”, comes from research by financial planner William Bengen in 1994: withdrawing 4% of your savings in the first year, then raising the amount with inflation, historically lasted at least 30 years in US markets. It implies a nest egg of about 25 times the yearly amount savings need to cover.

This calculator works it out directly from your returns, inflation and how many years retirement lasts. For a 25-year retirement (67 to 92) at a 4.5% return and 2.5% inflation, the nest egg needed comes out at about 20 times the yearly gap:

The 4% rule is more conservative because it assumes a 30-year retirement and allows for bad market sequences. If you retire early, expect a long retirement, or want a margin of safety, set the “Plan to age” field higher or the retirement return lower. In the example, the first-year withdrawal would be 7.2% of savings at 67, well above 4%, another sign the plan needs more savings or a later retirement.

Which accounts to fill first

The order you save in can matter as much as the amount, because each account type has different tax breaks. A common sequence:

  1. 401(k) or 403(b) up to the employer match. A typical match of 50% to 100% on part of your pay is an instant return no investment can promise.
  2. Health savings account (HSA), if you have an eligible high-deductible plan. Contributions go in pre-tax and come out tax-free for medical costs, which most retirees have plenty of.
  3. Roth or traditional IRA, up to $7,500 in 2026. A Roth often suits younger savers in lower brackets, since withdrawals in retirement are tax-free; a traditional IRA can lower today’s tax bill if you’re eligible to deduct it.
  4. The rest of the 401(k), up to $24,500 in 2026, plus catch-up contributions from age 50.
  5. A taxable brokerage account for anything beyond those limits.

Include every account in the savings and monthly contribution fields above so the projection reflects your whole plan.

What to do with your result

If you’re short, start with your employer match: not taking it means leaving part of your pay on the table. The 2026 401(k) contribution limit is $24,500, plus an $8,000 catch-up from age 50. See what a higher contribution costs per paycheck with the paycheck calculator; because it’s pre-tax, it costs less than it looks.

Model other accounts and goals with the investment calculator, and see how steady deposits compound with the compound interest calculator. If you’re saving toward something before retirement, such as a house deposit, plan it separately with the savings goal calculator so it doesn’t eat into retirement contributions.

Frequently asked questions

How much do I need to retire?

It depends on the income you want and what Social Security covers. Multiply the yearly gap your savings must fill by roughly 20 to 30, depending on how long retirement lasts and your investment returns. Someone who needs $42,400 a year from savings for 25 years, with withdrawals rising 2.5% a year and a 4.5% return, needs about $1.65 million at 67 in future dollars, or about $849,000 in today's money.

What is the 4% rule?

It's a guideline from research on historical US market returns suggesting that withdrawing 4% of your savings in the first year of retirement, then raising the amount with inflation, has usually lasted 30 years. It's a rule of thumb, not a guarantee; it depends on your investments, fees, taxes and the markets you retire into. The calculator shows your first-year withdrawal rate so you can compare.

How much should I save each month for retirement?

A common guideline is 15% of pre-tax income including any employer match, starting in your 20s or early 30s. Starting later means saving more. In our example, a 40-year-old with $85,000 saved needs $1,519.46 a month, not $900, to fund $70,000 a year of income at 67.

How does Social Security fit in?

Social Security replaces part of your income for life and rises with inflation, which lowers how much your savings must cover. Your estimate depends on your earnings history and the age you claim; check it at ssa.gov/myaccount. Enter it in today's dollars, and the calculator subtracts it from the income you want.

Does retiring a few years later make a big difference?

Yes, because it works three ways at once: more years of contributions, more years of growth, and fewer years of withdrawals. In our example, retiring at 70 instead of 67 cuts the shortfall from about $500,000 to about $193,000 and the required monthly saving from $1,519 to $1,092.

Are the results in today's dollars or future dollars?

Both. Savings at retirement and the nest egg needed are shown in future dollars, which is what your account statement will actually say. Each is also converted to today's dollars using your inflation rate so you can judge whether the amount feels sufficient.
