How to use this fuel cost calculator

Enter the trip and your car’s fuel economy, and the cost of fuel appears straight away.

  1. Units. Choose miles and gallons, or kilometres and litres. Switching converts the numbers you’ve already entered, so the trip stays the same.
  2. Distance. The one-way distance from a map app or the odometer.
  3. Fuel economy. Your car’s miles per gallon (or litres per 100 km). Use your real-world average if you know it; otherwise use the combined EPA rating from fueleconomy.gov.
  4. Gas price. The default is the latest weekly U.S. average for regular gasoline from the Energy Information Administration, with the date shown under the field. Enter the price at the pump where you’ll fill up for a closer estimate.
  5. Round trip and splitting. Tick Round trip to double the distance, and enter the number of people sharing the cost.

The result shows the fuel cost (or each person’s share), the gallons used, the cost per mile and the CO₂ from the fuel. The table shows what the same trip would cost in cars from 15 to 50 mpg.

The formula

Gallons = miles ÷ mpg

Fuel cost = gallons × price per gallon

Each person’s share = fuel cost ÷ people

In metric units, litres = km × L/100 km ÷ 100. Shares are rounded up to the cent so the fuel is fully covered. CO₂ uses the EPA’s emission factors of 8,887 grams per gallon of gasoline and 10,180 grams per gallon of diesel.

Worked example: a weekend road trip

Four friends are driving 470 miles each way in an SUV that averages 24 mpg, with gas at $4.15 a gallon.

  • Round-trip distance: 470 × 2 = 940 miles.
  • Gas needed: 940 ÷ 24 = 39.17 gallons.
  • Fuel cost: 39.17 × $4.15 = $162.54.
  • Each person’s share: $162.54 ÷ 4 = $40.635, rounded up to $40.64.
  • CO₂ from the fuel: about 348 kg, or 767 pounds.

Taking a car that gets 35 mpg instead would need about 26.9 gallons and cost about $111, saving around $51 on the trip.

Commuting costs

The same formula works for any regular drive. Enter your daily round-trip distance, then multiply the result by the days you drive.

A 32-mile round-trip commute, 5 days a week for 48 weeks, adds up to 7,680 miles a year. In a car that gets 30 mpg, at $4.15 a gallon:

  • Gas: 7,680 ÷ 30 = 256 gallons.
  • Cost: 256 × $4.15 = $1,062.40 a year, or about $88.53 a month.

That’s fuel only. The IRS standard mileage rate, which businesses use to reimburse employees, is much higher than the fuel cost per mile because it also covers depreciation, insurance, maintenance and tires.

Gas prices and how to plan for them

Gas prices change weekly and differ by region. Prices in some West Coast states are often more than a dollar above the national average, while parts of the Gulf Coast are well below it. On a long trip through several states, use an average of the prices along your route, or the higher figure if you want a safe budget.

A $0.50 change in the price of gas moves the cost of the 940-mile trip above by about $19.58. Prices also tend to run higher in late spring and summer, when demand rises and summer-blend fuel is required in many areas.

Getting better mileage

Your real mpg depends a lot on how and where you drive. Fueleconomy.gov, run by the Department of Energy and the EPA, highlights a few habits that make the biggest difference:

  • Slow down on the highway. Fuel economy usually drops quickly above about 50 mph.
  • Drive smoothly. Hard acceleration and braking can lower gas mileage significantly, both on highways and in town.
  • Remove roof boxes and racks when you aren’t using them, and avoid carrying unnecessary weight.
  • Keep tires inflated to the pressure on the door sticker.
  • Avoid long idling. An idling engine burns fuel and goes nowhere.

For a long trip, it’s sensible to add 10% to the fuel estimate for traffic, detours and hills. On the 940-mile trip, that raises the budget from $162.54 to about $178.80.

Splitting gas money fairly

There’s no single rule for sharing fuel costs, but a few approaches are common:

  • Fuel only, split evenly: the total fuel cost divided by everyone in the car, including the driver. This is what the calculator does.
  • Fuel plus wear: add a per-mile amount for the driver’s tires, maintenance and depreciation. Some groups use part of the IRS mileage rate as a guide.
  • Riders pay, driver drives: the passengers split the fuel and the driver pays nothing, as thanks for driving and providing the car.

Agree on the method before you set off, and include tolls and parking in the total.

Premium, diesel and E85

The calculator works with any liquid fuel; just enter the right price and fuel economy.

  • Premium gasoline usually costs noticeably more per gallon than regular. If your owner’s manual says premium is only recommended, not required, regular is generally fine, and you’ll pay less for every trip. Enter the price you actually pay.
  • Diesel is priced differently from gasoline and often costs more per gallon, but diesel engines usually travel farther on a gallon. Choose Diesel under More options so the CO₂ figure uses the EPA’s diesel factor of 10,180 grams per gallon.
  • E85 (up to 85% ethanol) is often cheaper per gallon, but a flex-fuel vehicle typically gets fewer miles per gallon on it, because ethanol holds less energy than gasoline. Compare the cost per mile, not the price per gallon: divide the price by the mpg you get on each fuel.

Budgeting the whole trip

Fuel is often the biggest single cost of a road trip, but not the only one. A complete budget usually includes:

  • Fuel, from this calculator, plus a margin for detours.
  • Tolls, which can add a lot on some interstate routes. Route planners and toll authorities publish rates.
  • Parking at hotels, attractions and in city centers.
  • Lodging for overnight stops on long drives.
  • Food and drinks along the way.
  • A rental car, if you’re not taking your own, including its fuel policy: returning a rental with less fuel than you collected it with usually costs more per gallon than filling it yourself.

Splitting the fuel and tolls evenly and letting each person pay for their own food is a simple way to keep things fair.

Common mistakes

  1. Using the highway rating for a city trip. Stop-and-go driving uses more fuel; use the city or combined rating for town driving.
  2. Forgetting the return leg. Tick Round trip if you’re coming back the same way.
  3. Using an old gas price. Prices can move by tens of cents in a few weeks. Check the latest average or your local station.
  4. Ignoring the load. A full car, a roof box or a trailer can lower your mpg well below the rating.
  5. Comparing cars by price per gallon instead of cost per mile. A cheaper fuel isn’t cheaper if the car goes much less far on it.

Key terms

  • Miles per gallon (mpg): how far a car travels on one gallon of fuel. Higher is better.
  • Litres per 100 km (L/100 km): the metric measure of fuel consumption. Lower is better. 30 mpg is about 7.84 L/100 km.
  • EPA combined rating: the official fuel economy estimate, weighted 55% city and 45% highway driving.
  • Cost per mile: fuel cost divided by distance; at 28 mpg and $4.15 a gallon, about 14.8¢.
  • Round trip: the journey there and back, twice the one-way distance.

Frequently asked questions

How do I calculate gas cost for a trip?

Divide the distance by your car's miles per gallon to get the gallons needed, then multiply by the price per gallon. A 350-mile drive in a car that gets 28 mpg needs 12.5 gallons; at $4.15 a gallon, that's $51.88.

How much gas money should I give a driver?

A fair share of the fuel is the total fuel cost divided by everyone in the car, including the driver. For a $162.54 round trip with four people, that's $40.64 each. Some groups add a little for tolls, parking or wear on the car.

How do I find my car's mpg?

Fill the tank, reset the trip odometer, drive normally, then fill up again. Divide the miles driven by the gallons it took to refill. You can also look up the EPA rating on fueleconomy.gov or read the average from your car's trip computer.

What is the average gas price right now?

The U.S. average for regular gasoline was $4.153 a gallon in the week of November 2, 2026, according to the U.S. Energy Information Administration's weekly survey. The calculator uses the latest figure as its default and shows its date under the price field.

Does driving faster use more gas?

Yes. Fuel economy usually falls quickly above about 50 mph because air resistance rises with speed. Fueleconomy.gov estimates that driving 5 to 10 mph slower on the highway can improve fuel economy by roughly 7% to 14%.

Is it cheaper to drive or fly?

It depends on the distance and how many people are going. Fuel for one person on a 940-mile round trip at 24 mpg costs about $163, often less than a plane ticket; with four people, it's about $41 each. Add tolls, parking, food and any overnight stays to compare fairly.

How much CO₂ does a gallon of gas produce?

Burning a gallon of gasoline produces about 8,887 grams (19.6 pounds) of carbon dioxide, and a gallon of diesel about 10,180 grams, according to the EPA. The calculator shows the CO₂ from your trip's fuel.
